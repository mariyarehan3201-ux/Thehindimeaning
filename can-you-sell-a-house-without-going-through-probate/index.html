Can You Sell a House Without Probate?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, you can sell a house without going through probate, provided the deceased homeowner established non-probate property transfer mechanisms prior to their passing. If the real estate was held inside a revocable living trust, titled in joint tenancy with right of survivorship, or conveyed via a recorded Transfer on Death deed, title passes automatically to successors outside probate court.

The Mechanics of Real Estate Probate and Title Conveyance

When a homeowner passes away, their real estate cannot simply be listed on the Multiple Listing Service (MLS) and conveyed by grieving family members. In American real property law, a valid deed transfer requires a living, legally competent grantor possessing clear marketable title to sign the deed. If the sole name listed on the county property deed is deceased, the property's title freezes in place.

Ordinarily, clearing this title freeze requires initiating formal probate proceedings in county surrogate or probate court. A probate judge reviews the decedent's last will, appoints an Executor or Personal Representative, and issues official Letters Testamentary granting legal authority to sell the real estate. However, formal probate is notoriously bureaucratic, expensive, and protracted, often consuming between nine months and two years while property taxes, insurance premiums, and mortgage payments steadily erode estate equity.

Fortunately, property owners and estate planning attorneys utilize several statutory instruments to bypass probate entirely. When these non-probate transfer instruments are properly executed and recorded prior to death, legal title to the residential real estate vests immediately in designated beneficiaries. The table below delineates the primary legal instruments that permit selling a home without going through probate court.

Revocable Living Trust Property deeded to trust; successor trustee controls asset Certificate of Trust + Death Certificate Zero: 100% private administrative conveyance
Transfer on Death (TOD) Deed / Beneficiary Deed Statutory deed recorded during life; transfers title at death Certified Death Certificate + Affidavit of Survivorship Zero: Title transfers automatically by operation of law

Selling Through a Revocable Living Trust or TOD Deed

The two most common vehicles allowing heirs to immediately sell an inherited residential property are Revocable Living Trusts and Transfer on Death (TOD) deeds. When a decedent transfers their home's warranty deed into a living trust during life, the trust itself—not the individual—becomes the legal owner of the real estate.

Upon the creator's death, the designated Successor Trustee immediately assumes administrative fiduciary control over all trust assets without court supervision. To sell the property, the successor trustee simply provides the title insurance company with a certified Death Certificate and a notarized Certificate of Trust or Memorandum of Trust confirming their legal authority. The trustee can list the home, accept buyer offers, sign the warranty deed at closing, and deposit the net proceeds directly into the trust bank account for distribution to beneficiaries.

Similarly, more than thirty states authorize Transfer on Death (TOD) deeds or Beneficiary Deeds. Under state statute, the homeowner records a TOD deed during their lifetime designating who receives the property upon their death. During life, the owner retains complete ownership, rights to sell, or revoke the deed. At death, the named beneficiary files a certified death certificate and a state-mandated affidavit of survivorship with the county recorder of deeds, instantly vesting clear marketable title in the beneficiary's name. The table below illustrates the procedural timeline differences between probate sales and trust-based non-probate sales.

What If the Estate Qualifies for Small Estate Procedures?

If the decedent died without a living trust or TOD deed, family members may still avoid full, formal court-supervised probate administration if the estate qualifies for simplified Small Estate procedures under state probate codes.

Virtually every state establishes statutory financial caps—ranging from $25,000 to over $184,500 depending on the jurisdiction—permitting successors to claim estate assets using an expedited Small Estate Affidavit or Summary Administration petition. In jurisdictions like California, Texas, and Washington, simplified probate procedures allow heirs to clear title to real estate with a single court petition that bypasses months of creditor claims and inventory accounting.

However, if the real property exceeds state small estate statutory limits, has extensive unresolved mortgage liens, or involves disputing heirs contesting ownership shares, attempting to sell without formal probate will result in title insurance underwriters rejecting the transaction. Securing a consultation with a qualified probate and estate planning attorney ensures clear marketable title and eliminates post-closing legal liabilities.

How to Sell an Inherited House Without Probate Court

Step-by-step roadmap to transfer title and execute a real estate sale outside probate administration.

  1. Examine County Land Records and Existing Deed

    Retrieve the most recent recorded deed from the county recorder's office to identify ownership structure: trust, JTWROS, or TOD designation.

  2. Obtain Multiple Certified Copies of Death Certificate

    Order 5 to 10 certified death certificates from the department of vital records to submit to title insurers, county recorders, and lenders.

  3. Record Survivorship or TOD Affidavit with County

    File a formal Affidavit of Death of Trustee or Affidavit of Survivorship alongside the death certificate to update county tax and title rolls.

  4. Engage a Title Company Familiar with Trust Conveyance

    Provide the title underwriter with trust documents or beneficiary filings to secure clear title insurance commitments prior to listing.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sell my deceased parents' house if I am named in the will?

A will alone does not give you authority to sell. A will must be admitted to probate court so a judge can grant formal Letters Testamentary.

Q2: Does a Transfer on Death deed bypass probate completely?

Yes. In states that recognize TOD deeds, title transfers automatically to the designated beneficiary upon the grantor's death without court intervention.

Q3: Can a successor trustee sell trust property immediately?

Yes. Once the trustee obtains a death certificate and provides a Certificate of Trust to the title company, they can list and sell the house immediately.

Q4: What happens if a house has an existing mortgage?

The mortgage remains attached to the real estate. The buyer's purchase funds pay off the existing lender at closing, with net equity paid to heirs.

Q5: Do all heirs have to agree to sell an inherited home?

If multiple heirs hold title jointly, all titled owners must sign the listing and deed; if held in trust, the trustee possesses sole legal authority to sell.

Q6: What is an Affidavit of Death of Joint Tenant?

A notarized legal document filed in county property records by a surviving co-owner to remove the deceased person's name from title.

Q7: Will title insurance companies insure a non-probate real estate sale?

Yes, provided the non-probate instrument (living trust, TOD deed, or joint tenancy) complies with state recording and statutory requirements.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Selling a house without going through probate is entirely achievable when decedent estate planning incorporates living trusts, transfer on death deeds, or survivorship titles. These statutory instruments keep real estate transactions swift, cost-effective, and completely private. Verify the existing county land records deed, gather certified death certificates, coordinate with an experienced title company, and consult an estate planning attorney to ensure an airtight conveyance.
