Can You Sue a Drunk Driver for Hitting You?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Yes, you have an unambiguous legal right to file a civil personal injury lawsuit against an impaired driver who causes a motor vehicle collision resulting in bodily injury or property destruction. While state prosecutors bring criminal charges to punish unlawful operation and enforce public safety, a civil lawsuit is an independent legal action initiated by the victim to secure financial compensation. In cases involving severe driver impairment, civil law allows plaintiffs to pursue substantial punitive damages in addition to compensatory damages, and in many jurisdictions, third-party liability claims against commercial establishments that over-served the motorist.

Civil Personal Injury Lawsuits vs Criminal DUI Prosecutions

When an impaired motorist causes a severe car crash, two distinct legal processes unfold concurrently: the state criminal prosecution and the victim civil personal injury lawsuit. These proceedings operate in entirely separate court systems with different objectives, procedural rules, and standards of proof. The criminal prosecution is managed exclusively by the district attorney or state prosecutor representing the public interest. Its goal is penal: to impose fines, suspend driving privileges, mandate treatment programs, or incarcerate the offender. The injured victim is a witness in the criminal case, not a party who controls legal strategy.

Conversely, a civil lawsuit is brought directly by the injured plaintiff represented by their chosen legal counsel. Its primary objective is restorative: to force the at-fault party to pay monetary compensation for medical treatment, surgical procedures, physical therapy, lost income, and emotional suffering. In criminal court, guilt must be established beyond a reasonable doubt, the highest evidentiary threshold in jurisprudence. In civil court, liability requires only a preponderance of the evidence, meaning the plaintiff must demonstrate it is more likely than not that the driver negligent impairment caused the crash.

Because of this lower burden of proof, an injured victim can successfully win a civil personal injury lawsuit even if the impaired driver avoids criminal conviction, secures a plea bargain to a lesser offense like reckless driving, or has chemical test evidence excluded due to police procedural technicalities. Criminal courts occasionally order restitution as part of sentencing, but criminal restitution is narrowly limited to direct, out-of-pocket economic losses. Restitution almost never covers non-economic general damages like chronic pain, disfigurement, or loss of life enjoyment, making a civil lawsuit essential for securing complete compensation for being hit by a drunk driver.

Gross Negligence, Compensatory Damages, and Punitive Awards

In standard motor vehicle accident claims, liability hinges on ordinary negligence, defined as the failure to exercise reasonable care under the circumstances. However, getting behind the wheel while severely impaired elevates driver culpability to gross negligence or willful and wanton misconduct. By consciously consuming intoxicating substances and operating a two-ton motor vehicle on public roads, the driver exhibits conscious indifference to the safety of others. This distinction provides plaintiffs with extraordinary leverage during insurance settlement negotiations and courtroom litigation.

Compensatory damages in an impaired driving lawsuit fall into two primary categories: economic and non-economic damages. Economic damages represent measurable monetary losses, including past hospital stays, future reconstructive surgeries, specialized medical equipment, lost earnings, and diminished earning capacity. Non-economic damages compensate the victim for physical pain, mental anguish, post-traumatic stress disorder, physical impairment, and loss of consortium. Because juries view impaired drivers with deep moral disapproval, non-economic damage awards in these cases frequently reach substantial multiples of economic losses.

The most distinctive feature of lawsuits against impaired motorists is the availability of punitive damages, also referred to as exemplary damages. Unlike compensatory damages, which aim to make the victim whole, punitive damages are awarded specifically to punish the wrongdoer and make an example of them to deter society. While standard automobile insurance policies generally indemnify compensatory damages up to policy limits, insurance policies frequently contain exclusions for punitive damages. Understanding insurance limits and filing timelines is critical, making it necessary to consult seasoned counsel regarding hiring an attorney for a personal injury claim within the statutory how long you can sue someone after an accident deadlines.

Third-Party Liability: Commercial Vendor Acts and Social Host Responsibility

A frequent challenge in suing impaired motorists is that negligent drivers often carry only minimum statutory automobile insurance, such as twenty-five thousand dollars in bodily injury coverage. In catastrophic injury cases where medical treatment costs hundreds of thousands of dollars, the driver insurance policy is grossly inadequate. To bridge this financial gap, experienced personal injury attorneys investigate third-party liability through commercial dram shop statutes and social host liability doctrines.

Under dram shop acts enacted in most states, commercial establishments like bars, taverns, restaurants, and entertainment venues can be held civilly liable if they continue serving intoxicating beverages to a person who is visibly or noticeably intoxicated, and that individual subsequently causes a vehicle collision. To prevail in a commercial dram shop claim, plaintiffs present electronic point-of-sale receipts, bar surveillance footage, and testimony from patrons establishing that staff observed slurred speech, uncoordinated movement, or aggressive behavior but continued serving drinks. Commercial establishments carry commercial hospitality liability insurance with policy limits often reaching one to five million dollars.

Social host liability applies when private individuals host social gatherings and furnish intoxicating beverages to underage motorists. While most states protect private hosts from liability for adult guests, almost all jurisdictions impose strict civil liability on homeowners who knowingly provide intoxicating drinks to minors who subsequently cause crashes. Finally, victims should look to their own automobile insurance policy Underinsured Motorist, or UIM, endorsement, which steps in to pay remaining compensatory damages when the at-fault driver coverage is exhausted.

How to Pursue a Civil Lawsuit Against an Impaired Driver

Follow these strategic legal and diagnostic steps to build an undeniable personal injury lawsuit against an impaired motorist following a collision.

  1. Obtain the Complete Police Crash and Impairment Investigation File

    Secure certified copies of the official police collision report, including field sobriety test notes, preliminary breath test records, and officer observations of impairment.

  2. Undergo Immediate Medical Care and Maintain Treatment Records

    Seek comprehensive emergency room evaluation immediately following the crash, complying strictly with all physical therapy, specialist consultations, and surgical recommendations.

  3. Issue Formal Spoliation Letters to Preserve Electronic Evidence

    Have your attorney issue immediate legal spoliation notices to the driver, tow yards, and commercial establishments to preserve dashcam videos, event data recorders, and bar receipts.

  4. Investigate Insurance Coverage and Third-Party Commercial Liability

    Identify all available insurance policies, including the at-fault driver bodily injury liability, commercial dram shop hospitality policies, and your own underinsured motorist coverage.

  5. File a Civil Complaint Seeking Compensatory and Punitive Damages

    Draft and file a formal civil summons and complaint in county court alleging gross negligence, demanding full compensatory damages and statutory punitive awards.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sue an impaired driver if their criminal charges are dismissed?

Yes, you can still sue and win. Civil court uses a lower standard of proof known as preponderance of the evidence (51% likelihood), whereas criminal court requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Dismissal of criminal charges does not bar a civil lawsuit.

Q2: What are punitive damages and can I receive them in a collision lawsuit?

Punitive damages are monetary penalties awarded above compensatory damages to punish egregious misconduct and deter future reckless behavior. Because driving while intoxicated constitutes gross negligence, juries routinely award punitive damages in these cases.

Q3: Will the at-fault driver insurance company pay for my injuries?

Yes, the driver auto liability insurance is responsible for paying compensatory damages up to the policy bodily injury limits. However, insurance carriers often exclude punitive damage payments, requiring collection from the driver personal assets.

Q4: Can the impaired driver declare bankruptcy to avoid paying a civil judgment?

No. Under the federal United States Bankruptcy Code Section 523(a)(9), civil judgments and debts arising from death or personal injury caused by the debtor operation of a motor vehicle while unlawfully intoxicated cannot be discharged in bankruptcy.

Q5: Can I sue the bar or restaurant that served the driver?

Yes, under state dram shop acts, you can sue commercial establishments that continued serving intoxicating drinks to a visibly intoxicated customer who subsequently drove and caused an accident. Bars maintain substantial commercial insurance coverage.

Q6: What if the impaired driver only has minimum state insurance coverage?

If the at-fault driver policy limits are insufficient to cover your medical bills, your attorney will pursue third-party commercial dram shop claims and file an Underinsured Motorist claim against your own auto insurance policy.

Q7: How long do I have to file a lawsuit against an impaired driver?

The statute of limitations for personal injury lawsuits generally ranges from one to three years from the date of the collision, depending on the state. Missing this legal deadline permanently forfeits your right to seek financial recovery in court.

Q8: Do I still need a personal injury lawyer if the criminal court ordered restitution?

Yes, criminal restitution covers only documented out-of-pocket medical bills and basic property damage. Restitution does not provide compensation for physical pain, emotional trauma, lost future earning potential, or punitive damages.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Filing a civil lawsuit against an impaired driver is both a righteous pursuit of justice and an essential mechanism for securing the financial resources required to heal from traumatic injuries. Unlike ordinary fender benders, collisions caused by intoxicated motorists involve elevated legal standards of gross negligence, potential punitive damages, and third-party commercial liability. By preserving police accident reports, gathering chemical test results, tracking all medical losses, and pursuing every available insurance policy, injured victims can achieve full accountability and comprehensive financial recovery.
