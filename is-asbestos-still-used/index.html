Is Asbestos Still Used?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Is asbestos still used? The comprehensive answer is both yes and no, depending on the specific international jurisdiction and industrial sector examined. In more than sixty-seven industrialized nations—including the entire European Union, the United Kingdom, Australia, Japan, and Canada—the importation, manufacture, sale, and use of all asbestos minerals are strictly and completely banned. In the United States, a widespread public misconception persists that asbestos was outlawed decades ago; however, while consumer sprays, pipe insulation, and flooring were restricted in the 1970s and 1980s, commercial imports of chrysotile asbestos continued for specialized industrial applications until the US EPA finalized a comprehensive prohibition on chrysotile in March 2024. Furthermore, in major developing economies including Russia, China, India, and Kazakhstan, asbestos continues to be mined and utilized in massive quantities for construction materials.

The Status of Asbestos in the United States and the 2024 EPA Ban

The regulatory trajectory of asbestos in the United States has been marked by decades of legal contention and policy reversals. In 1989, the Environmental Protection Agency issued the landmark Asbestos Ban and Phase-Out Rule under the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA), designed to outlaw virtually all asbestos-containing products over a seven-year period. However, in the 1991 landmark federal appellate ruling Corrosion Proof Fittings v. EPA, the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals overturned the vast majority of the ban, determining that the EPA had failed to prove asbestos was the 'least burdensome' regulatory alternative, thereby allowing dozens of legacy product categories to remain legal.

Consequently, the United States continued importing hundreds of metric tons of raw chrysotile asbestos annually for decades, primarily consumed by the domestic chlor-alkali chemical manufacturing industry. Chlor-alkali facilities utilized raw chrysotile to fabricate permeable diaphragms within electrolytic cells used to produce chlorine, sodium hydroxide (caustic soda), and bleach. Following amendments to TSCA in 2016 (the Lautenberg Act), the EPA conducted rigorous multi-year risk evaluations, culminating in a historic final rule issued on March 18, 2024, that completely bans the importation, processing, and distribution of chrysotile asbestos with structured phase-out timelines for chemical plants.

Global Landscape: Banned Nations versus Active Markets

Globally, a stark geopolitical divide governs modern asbestos consumption. Over sixty-seven developed countries enforce zero-tolerance prohibitions that ban the mining, manufacturing, importing, and exporting of all six asbestos minerals. The European Union implemented a comprehensive directive in 2005 that outlawed all chrysotile uses across its member states, while nations such as Australia and the United Kingdom maintain strict border controls that confiscate imported goods, machinery parts, or automotive components containing even trace asbestos contamination.

Conversely, in several developing nations and prominent raw mineral producers, asbestos remains an active, multi-billion-dollar industrial commodity. Russia represents the world's largest producer and consumer of chrysotile asbestos, operating massive open-pit mines in the Ural Mountains city of Asbest, followed closely by Kazakhstan, China, and Zimbabwe. In major consumer nations across South Asia, Southeast Asia, and South America—most notably India, Indonesia, and Vietnam—inexpensive corrugated asbestos-cement roofing sheets and water pipes are widely manufactured and installed across low-income residential and agricultural communities.

Legacy Asbestos in Existing Infrastructure and Modern Risks

Even in nations where asbestos manufacturing and importation are completely outlawed, asbestos remains actively present in built environments. Millions of commercial office towers, public schools, military bases, hospitals, and residential homes constructed between 1920 and 1985 still contain thousands of tons of legacy asbestos products. Popcorn ceilings, vinyl floor tiles, transite cement siding shingles, and boiler pipe lagging remain safely encapsulated in many structures, but pose severe respiratory hazards whenever aging, weather damage, or uncontained renovation occurs.

Modern exposure risks have shifted from primary industrial factory workers to secondary building trades and DIY homeowners. Electricians, plumbers, HVAC technicians, firefighters, and residential remodelers frequently encounter and accidentally disturb legacy asbestos when cutting into walls, accessing ceiling plenums, or performing emergency repairs on older piping systems. Consequently, environmental agencies emphasize that the battle against asbestos is far from over: managing, encapsulating, and safely abating the legacy materials already installed inside our buildings remains a paramount public health priority for decades to come.

How to Protect Yourself from Asbestos Still in Use Today

Follow these five protective guidelines to avoid accidental exposure to legacy asbestos materials and imported contaminated goods.

  1. Verify the Age of Your Building

    Check building construction records; any property built before 1985 has a high statistical probability of containing legacy asbestos materials.

  2. Treat Suspicious Materials as Hazardous

    Presume that textured ceilings, nine-inch floor tiles, and pipe wraps contain asbestos until proven otherwise by certified laboratory testing.

  3. Avoid Unregulated Imported Goods

    Beware of inexpensive imported friction parts, ceramic goods, or building materials sourced from countries without asbestos bans.

  4. Commission Pre-Renovation Laboratory Testing

    Always hire a licensed asbestos inspector to take controlled bulk core samples before performing plumbing, electrical, or structural remodeling.

  5. Hire Certified Abatement for Demolition

    If asbestos-containing materials must be removed, exclusively contract licensed environmental abatement firms utilizing negative-air HEPA systems.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is asbestos completely banned in the United States?

The EPA issued a final rule banning chrysotile asbestos in March 2024, closing the last legal loopholes for industrial chlor-alkali plants.

Q2: Which countries still mine and use asbestos today?

Russia, Kazakhstan, and China remain major producers, while India, Indonesia, and Vietnam are major consumers of chrysotile asbestos.

Q3: How many countries have banned asbestos completely?

More than sixty-seven nations have enacted complete bans on the mining, manufacture, importation, and use of all asbestos forms.

Q4: Why did the US continue using asbestos until 2024?

A 1991 federal court ruling overturned the EPA's 1989 ban, allowing chemical chlor-alkali plants to continue importing chrysotile diaphragms.

Q5: Can imported products still contain asbestos in the US?

While illegal, imported automotive brake pads, gaskets, and vintage building materials from non-ban countries occasionally test positive.

Q6: Is there asbestos in modern drywall and joint compound?

No, modern drywall and joint compound sold in the US have been completely asbestos-free since federal bans took effect in the late 1970s.

Q7: Is legacy asbestos still inside older homes and schools?

Yes, millions of homes and schools built before 1985 still contain intact asbestos flooring, ceiling textures, insulation, and siding.

Q8: What is the safest alternative to asbestos used today?

Modern engineering uses safe synthetic substitutes like aramid fibers (Kevlar), spun basalt mineral wool, fiberglass, and expanded perlite.
