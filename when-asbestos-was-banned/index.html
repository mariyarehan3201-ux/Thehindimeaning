When Asbestos Was Banned?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

When asbestos was banned is one of the most frequently asked yet widely misunderstood regulatory questions in North American environmental law. Many consumers mistakenly believe that the United States enacted a sweeping, total ban on asbestos during the 1970s or 1980s. In truth, the regulatory timeline of asbestos prohibition has been a contentious, half-century legal battle characterized by targeted product restrictions, federal court reversals, and a landmark final prohibition on chrysotile asbestos completed only in March 2024.

Early Targeted Restrictions: 1973 to 1978

The initial federal regulatory interventions against asbestos occurred during the 1970s under the auspices of newly created environmental protection agencies. In 1973, the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), operating under the Clean Air Act's National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP), banned spray-applied asbestos-containing materials used for structural fireproofing and thermal insulation in high-rise buildings.

Following this action, the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) stepped in during 1977 to ban asbestos in consumer drywall patching compounds and artificial emberizing ash used in gas fireplaces. In 1978, the EPA broadened its NESHAP ban to prohibit decorative spray-on acoustic plaster and textured 'popcorn' ceiling applications. However, these early regulations targeted only specific friable consumer products, leaving thousands of industrial, automotive, and commercial building materials completely unrestricted.

The 1989 EPA Phaseout Rule and the 1991 Court Overturn

The pivotal moment in American asbestos policy occurred in July 1989, when the EPA, under Section 6 of the Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA), promulgated the Asbestos Ban and Phaseout Rule. This historic rule was designed to systematically prohibit the manufacture, processing, commercial distribution, and importation of almost all asbestos-containing products over a seven-year phased schedule.

However, commercial asbestos manufacturers and international trade associations filed a fierce lawsuit against the agency. In the landmark 1991 decision Corrosion Proof Fittings v. EPA, the US Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit overturned the vast majority of the ban. The court ruled that the EPA had failed to demonstrate that the ban was the 'least burdensome' alternative required under the statutory language of TSCA at that time. As a result of this ruling, asbestos remained legal in the US in automotive brake pads, roofing coatings, gaskets, and chlor-alkali diaphragm membranes for the next three decades.

The Landmark March 2024 EPA Ban and Global Landscape

Following decades of legislative gridlock, the federal regulatory landscape shifted dramatically with the 2016 passage of the Frank R. Lautenberg Chemical Safety for the 21st Century Act, which modernized TSCA and eliminated the restrictive 'least burdensome' requirement. Armed with this updated statutory authority, the EPA finalized a landmark rule on March 18, 2024, announcing a complete and comprehensive ban on chrysotile asbestos—the sole variety of asbestos imported or processed in modern American commerce.

The 2024 final rule immediately prohibits raw chrysotile imports and establishes phased compliance deadlines for the chemical chlor-alkali sector to transition to membrane cell technology, while immediately banning asbestos-containing friction products, gaskets, and aftermarket automotive parts. Globally, over 60 countries—including the United Kingdom, Australia, Japan, and all European Union members—enacted comprehensive asbestos bans years earlier, making the 2024 US rule a long-awaited milestone in international public health.

How to Verify If a Material Was Installed Before or After Asbestos Restrictions

A systematic approach to evaluating product installation dates against federal ban timelines.

  1. Identify the Specific Material and Application

    Determine whether the building material is spray-applied fireproofing (banned 1973), popcorn texture (banned 1978), or commercial mastic.

  2. Check Architectural Permits and Invoices

    Examine architectural drawings, construction permits, or contractor billing invoices to pinpoint the exact installation year.

  3. Account for Existing Manufacturer Stockpiles

    Remember that federal bans prohibited manufacturing, but allowed retailers to sell off existing stockpiles for several years post-ban.

  4. Do Not Rely Exclusively on Construction Age

    Recognize that because friction products and roof coatings remained legal until 2024, age alone does not guarantee a material is asbestos-free.

  5. Submit Physical Samples to a Certified Laboratory

    The only definitive method to verify asbestos content is submitting a bulk sample to an NVLAP-accredited laboratory for PLM analysis.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is asbestos 100% banned in the United States today?

Yes. In March 2024, the EPA finalized a comprehensive ban under TSCA on chrysotile asbestos, the last variety imported into the United States.

Q2: Why wasn't asbestos completely banned in 1989?

In 1991, the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals overturned the EPA's 1989 total phaseout rule, ruling the agency did not prove it was the least burdensome option.

Q3: When were popcorn ceilings banned?

The EPA banned the manufacturing of asbestos-containing textured popcorn ceiling sprays in 1978 under the Clean Air Act.

Q4: When was asbestos banned in automotive brake pads?

While domestic car makers largely phased them out in the late 1990s, aftermarket imports remained legal until the EPA's comprehensive ban in 2024.

Q5: Did the UK ban asbestos before the United States?

Yes. The United Kingdom banned blue and brown asbestos in 1985 and enacted a total ban on all white chrysotile asbestos in 1999.

Q6: How many countries have banned asbestos worldwide?

More than 60 nations across the European Union, the Americas, and Asia-Pacific have enacted total, comprehensive bans on all forms of asbestos.

Q7: Can contractors still use old asbestos materials found in storage?

No. Federal and state regulations strictly prohibit the distribution, re-installation, or reuse of asbestos-containing building materials.

Q8: Does the 2024 EPA ban mean all buildings are now safe?

No. The 2024 ban stops new imports and uses, but millions of older structures erected prior to 1980 still contain intact legacy asbestos.
