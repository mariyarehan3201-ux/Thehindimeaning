Will a Bad Capacitor Cause AC to Not Cool?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

A failing or completely defective capacitor is one of the most common reasons a central air conditioning system suddenly stops blowing cold air and begins circulating warm ambient indoor air. In modern residential split systems, dual run capacitors store electrical charge and provide the necessary starting torque and continuous phase-shifted current to operate two critical outdoor motors: the hermetic compressor and the condenser fan motor. When the capacitor degrades or suffers an internal dielectric breakdown, neither motor can function properly, completely halting the refrigeration cycle while the indoor air handler continues to run deceptively.

The Electrical Role of Dual Run Capacitors in Refrigeration

Residential split air conditioning condensers rely on single-phase alternating current supplied by household electrical panels. However, single-phase induction motors, including the hermetic scroll compressor and the outdoor condenser fan motor, cannot generate a rotating magnetic field on their own. To initiate and maintain smooth rotation, these motors require two separate stator windings: a main run winding and an auxiliary start winding. The dual run capacitor is wired in series with the start winding, creating an artificial ninety-degree phase displacement that produces the mechanical starting torque and rotational momentum necessary for continuous operation.

Most outdoor condensing units utilize a single dual run capacitor housed inside the electrical service panel. This cylindrical metal component features three distinct sets of spade terminals: Common (marked C), Hermetic Compressor (marked HERM), and Condenser Fan Motor (marked FAN). The capacitor contains layers of conductive aluminum foil separated by a thin dielectric polymer film submerged in synthetic insulating oil. Capacitance is measured in microfarads (represented by the symbol µF or MFD), with ratings such as 35/5 µF, 40/5 µF, or 45/5 µF, indicating the separate capacitance values allocated to the compressor and fan circuits respectively.

When the dielectric material inside the capacitor degrades due to continuous summer heat exposure, high electrical voltage spikes, or sustained operating hours, its microfarad rating drops precipitously. Once capacitance drifts below manufacturer tolerances, the motor windings receive inadequate current phase shift, rendering the compressor or condenser fan incapable of spinning. Mechanical Symptoms: Motor Stalls, Compressor Hum, and Overload Trips

When a homeowner notices that the indoor air handler is blowing warm air despite the thermostat calling for cooling, the outdoor condenser is almost always the origin of the problem. If the HERM side of the dual capacitor has failed, the compressor attempts to start when the contactor closes, but without the phase-shifted boost, the motor rotor remains physically locked. This draws massive locked rotor amperage (LRA), often five to seven times the normal running current. The locked motor generates an unmistakable deep electrical humming or buzzing vibration every few minutes.

Within eight to fifteen seconds of failing to start, the locked rotor generates intense resistive heat within the hermetic copper windings. To prevent a catastrophic electrical fire or winding burnout, an internal bimetallic thermal overload protector opens, cutting power to the compressor motor. The compressor then sits dormant and scorching hot for thirty to sixty minutes until the internal thermal disc cools and snaps closed again, restarting the cycle of humming, failing to rotate, and tripping on thermal overload. If this cycle repeats unabated over several days, the extreme thermal stress can permanently ruin the compressor insulation, resulting in a multi-thousand-dollar replacement.

In other instances, the FAN side of the dual capacitor fails while the HERM side remains operational. In this scenario, the compressor successfully starts and pumps high-pressure refrigerant through the condenser coil, but the top fan blade remains completely motionless. Without forced airflow across the condenser fins, the system cannot reject heat to the outdoor atmosphere. Condensing pressure and refrigerant temperature skyrocket within two minutes, causing the compressor internal high-pressure relief valve to bypass or tripping the high-pressure safety switch, bringing cooling to a complete halt.

Physical Inspection, Electrical Testing, and Replacement Standards

Visual inspection often provides immediate confirmation of capacitor death. Run capacitors incorporate an internal pressure-relief mechanism designed to expand under excessive heat and gas pressure, disconnecting the internal foil leads before the metal can can violently rupture. A defective capacitor frequently exhibits a swollen, domed, or bulged top terminal disc, resembling an over-pressurized soda can. Additionally, oily dielectric fluid leaking around the terminal posts or dark rust streaks running down the casing are definitive signs of internal structural failure.

However, many failing capacitors look pristine externally while their internal capacitance has drifted far below the allowable plus-or-minus five or six percent operating tolerance. Accurately confirming capacitor integrity requires using a digital multimeter capable of measuring capacitance in microfarads. After disconnecting high-voltage power and safely discharging the stored current using an insulated resistor, the technician detaches the wires and measures capacitance between the Common terminal and the HERM terminal, followed by Common to FAN. If either reading falls outside the specified percentage stamped on the label, the capacitor must be replaced.

When selecting a replacement capacitor, the microfarad rating must match the equipment manufacturer specifications exactly. Installing an incorrect microfarad rating alters the phase angle, causing excessive heat buildup in the motor start windings. Conversely, voltage ratings can safely be upgraded: installing a 440-volt capacitor in place of a factory 370-volt unit is an industry-recommended practice that provides greater dielectric endurance against summer voltage fluctuations.

How to Safely Test and Identify a Faulty AC Run Capacitor

Follow these electrical diagnostic procedures using a digital multimeter to safely discharge, remove, and measure the microfarad capacitance of an air conditioner run capacitor.

  1. Shut Down Electrical Power at the High-Voltage Service Disconnect

    Locate the metal disconnect box mounted on the exterior wall near the outdoor condensing unit, pull out the handle or flip the breaker to completely disconnect electrical power, and switch off the indoor breaker panel switch.

  2. Remove the Outdoor Condenser Electrical Control Access Panel

    Use a hex-head driver or screwdriver to remove the mounting screws securing the corner electrical access cover on the outdoor condensing unit, setting the panel aside to expose the capacitor, contactor, and wiring.

  3. Safely Discharge Stored High-Voltage Current Across All Terminals

    Place an insulated-handle screwdriver with a twenty-thousand-ohm resistor or span an insulated metal shaft across Common to HERM and Common to FAN terminals to safely dissipate any lethal stored electrical voltage.

  4. Label and Detach Terminal Wires from Common, Herm, and Fan Terminals

    Photograph the existing wiring configuration with your phone, mark each wire with masking tape, and use needle-nose pliers to carefully pull the spade connectors off the C, HERM, and FAN terminal posts.

  5. Measure Capacitance Values Across Terminals Using a Digital Multimeter

    Switch your digital multimeter to the capacitance setting (marked MFD or µF), place one test probe on Common and the other on HERM, then repeat for Common to FAN, comparing the readings against the printed label ratings.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How do I know if my AC is not cooling due to a bad capacitor?

Common indicators include your indoor vents blowing room-temperature air, the outdoor unit emitting a deep electrical humming or buzzing noise without the compressor starting, the outdoor fan blade failing to spin, or visible bulging on the top of the capacitor.

Q2: Can a bad capacitor permanently damage my AC compressor?

Yes. When a capacitor fails, the compressor rotor remains locked while attempting to start, drawing massive locked-rotor current. If the system repeatedly attempts to start and trips the thermal overload protector over days, the extreme heat can destroy the internal motor windings.

Q3: What does a failing AC capacitor sound like?

A failing capacitor typically causes the outdoor condensing unit to emit a loud, rhythmic humming, buzzing, or clicking sound every few minutes as the compressor attempts to start without sufficient phase torque, followed by silence when the thermal overload trips.

Q4: Can I push-start my condenser fan motor if the capacitor is bad?

You can sometimes start a stalled condenser fan blade by carefully nudging it clockwise with a long wooden or plastic stick. While this confirms the capacitor start circuit has failed, it is strictly a diagnostic test; the motor will overheat and stall again once power cycles.

Q5: How long does a typical AC dual run capacitor last?

Most residential AC run capacitors last between five and ten years. However, prolonged exposure to extreme summer heat above ninety-five degrees Fahrenheit, frequent power surges, and continuous system cycling can degrade internal dielectric films within three to five years.

Q6: Can I replace a 370 volt capacitor with a 440 volt capacitor?

Yes. You can safely replace a 370-volt capacitor with a 440-volt capacitor of the exact same microfarad rating. A higher voltage rating provides greater dielectric insulation strength against line surges. However, you must never replace a 440-volt capacitor with a lower 370-volt unit.

Q7: What happens if I install a capacitor with the wrong microfarad rating?

Installing an incorrect microfarad rating alters the phase angle of the electrical current feeding the motor start winding. An incorrect rating causes the motor to run hotter, operate at suboptimal speed, consume excess power, and suffer premature winding failure.

Q8: Why does my indoor blower fan blow warm air when the capacitor fails?

The indoor air handler contains its own independent blower motor that continues circulating air through your ductwork. Because the outdoor capacitor failure keeps the compressor offline, no chilled refrigerant reaches the indoor evaporator coil, resulting in unconditioned room-temperature airflow.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

A bad capacitor will unequivocally cause your central air conditioning system to stop cooling, converting an efficient refrigeration system into an ineffective air circulator. Recognizing early warning signs such as outdoor compressor humming, sluggish condenser fan blades, or brief clicking sounds can save you from catastrophic compressor burnout. Because dual run capacitors are inexpensive and straightforward to replace, addressing a degraded capacitor at the first sign of cooling failure is one of the most cost-effective repairs in residential HVAC maintenance.
