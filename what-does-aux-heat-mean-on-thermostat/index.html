What Does Aux Heat Mean on a Thermostat?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 17, 2026

When freezing winter temperatures arrive and you see the indicator Aux Heat or Auxiliary Heat illuminate on your digital thermostat screen, it signifies that your heat pump has activated a secondary heating stage to keep your home warm. Heat pumps operate by absorbing ambient heat energy from outside air and transferring it indoors through refrigerant lines. However, when outdoor temperatures drop below freezing, typically around thirty-five to thirty degrees Fahrenheit, the quantity of thermal heat available in outdoor air decreases significantly. To prevent indoor temperatures from dropping, your HVAC system automatically engages auxiliary heating elements.

Understanding auxiliary heat operation helps homeowners maintain indoor comfort without incurring shockingly high monthly utility bills. Unlike your primary heat pump, which moves heat efficiently, auxiliary heat relies on high-resistance electric heating strips or a secondary gas burner. These supplemental heat sources consume substantially more electricity than the compressor. Knowing when auxiliary heat is operating normally versus when it indicates an underlying mechanical problem gives you complete control over your home heating system.

The mechanical efficiency of a residential heat pump is defined by its Coefficient of Performance (COP). In mild weather between forty-five and fifty degrees Fahrenheit, a modern variable-speed heat pump delivers three to four units of thermal heat energy for every unit of electricity consumed. However, thermodynamic laws dictate that as ambient outdoor air grows colder, heat extraction becomes more labor-intensive. When the heat pump output drops below the home heat loss rate, auxiliary heat bridges the thermal deficit.

Understanding how primary heat pump operation compares with auxiliary heat strips clarifies why electric consumption spikes during harsh cold snaps. The table below illustrates the technical and economic differences between primary compressor heating and auxiliary backup heat.

Many homeowners confuse Auxiliary Heat with Emergency Heat (often labeled EM Heat on thermostat interfaces). While both heating stages utilize the exact same electric resistance coils or secondary fuel source, the activation logic is fundamentally different. Auxiliary Heat is an automatic second stage that works concurrently alongside the running compressor. In contrast, selecting Emergency Heat manually shuts off the outdoor unit entirely, relying solely on electric resistance coils even if the outdoor compressor is functioning normally.

To reduce auxiliary heat runtimes, practice steady thermostat management during winter months. Setting your thermostat to a consistent temperature rather than making steep multiple-degree adjustments allows the heat pump to maintain warmth steadily without triggering high-draw heating elements.

How to Troubleshoot and Optimize Auxiliary Heat on Your Thermostat

  1. Monitor the Outdoor Temperature Threshold

    Check outdoor thermometer readings; if outside temperatures are above forty degrees Fahrenheit and Aux Heat runs continuously, suspect a system issue.

  2. Verify Thermostat Temperature Differentials

    Avoid raising your indoor setpoint by more than two or three degrees at once, as large manual adjustments automatically trigger auxiliary heat strips.

  3. Inspect and Replace Air Filters Regularly

    Check return air filters monthly; clogged filters restrict airflow over indoor coils, reducing heat pump efficiency and forcing auxiliary heat engagement.

  4. Check Outdoor Condenser for Ice Buildup

    Inspect the outdoor unit for thick ice coating on the aluminum fins, which forces the heat pump into defrost mode and activates auxiliary backup heat.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is Aux Heat bad for my HVAC system?

No, auxiliary heat is a normal, engineered feature designed to assist your heat pump during periods of sub-freezing weather or rapid temperature recovery.

Q2: Why is my electric bill so high when Aux Heat turns on?

Electric auxiliary heat strips function like giant toaster coils, drawing between 10 to 20 kilowatts of electricity per hour compared to 3 kilowatts for a compressor.

Q3: What is the difference between Aux Heat and Emergency Heat?

Aux Heat engages automatically as secondary backup when needed, whereas Emergency Heat manually locks out the outdoor compressor and runs only expensive heating strips.

Q4: Should Aux Heat come on in the morning?

Yes, if your programmable thermostat adjusts from a cool overnight setting like 62 degrees to a morning setting of 68 degrees, Aux Heat assists the rapid climb.

Q5: Can I turn off Aux Heat to save money?

You cannot directly disable automatic Aux Heat on most thermostats without reprogramming staging settings, but avoiding large temperature jumps minimizes its runtime.

Q6: Why does Aux Heat turn on during defrost mode?

When the outdoor unit defrosts, the heat pump reverses into cooling mode; Aux Heat energizes indoors to temper chilled air so your vents do not blow cold drafts.

Q7: How long should Aux Heat stay on during a cycle?

Auxiliary heat typically runs for 15 to 30 minutes until the indoor temperature reaches within one degree of your desired setpoint.

Q8: What does it mean if Aux Heat stays on constantly?

Continuous Aux Heat during mild weather indicates low refrigerant charge, failing compressor valves, a stuck reversing valve, or a faulty outdoor defrost board.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Auxiliary heat is a built-in safety net that ensures your home remains cozy and warm when extreme winter freezes challenge your heat pump capabilities. While auxiliary electric strips consume more power than the outdoor compressor, their automated use during sub-freezing weather is completely normal. By maintaining clean filters, avoiding sharp setpoint swings, and leaving Emergency Heat reserved strictly for true mechanical breakdowns, you can optimize comfort and energy savings all winter long.
