Asbestos on Navy Ships

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

The historical presence of asbestos on Navy ships represents one of the most concentrated and devastating occupational toxic exposures in American naval history. From the build-up of the Two-Ocean Navy prior to World War II through the height of the Cold War, the United States Navy mandated the extensive incorporation of asbestos-containing thermal insulation, gaskets, packing, and electrical systems across virtually every vessel in the fleet. Because warships operated in enclosed, high-temperature, and battle-critical maritime environments, thousands of tons of friable asbestos products were packed into propulsion spaces, exposing generations of sailors to lethal mineral dust.

Military Specifications (Mil-Specs) and Heavy Naval Reliance

The massive utilization of asbestos on naval vessels was directly driven by rigorous military specifications (Mil-Specs) enacted by the US Bureau of Ships (BuShips) and the Department of the Navy. Following catastrophic fires aboard combat vessels in early twentieth-century naval warfare, the Navy sought materials with supreme thermal resistance, acoustic deadening, and fireproofing capabilities to prevent engine room explosions and contain damage. Military procurement mandates required that steam piping, high-pressure boilers, and bulkhead partitions incorporate dense chrysotile and amosite asbestos composites.

As a result of these specifications, private shipbuilders—including Newport News Shipbuilding, Bath Iron Works, Electric Boat, and naval shipyards across the nation—installed hundreds of tons of asbestos onto newly constructed warships. An Essex-class aircraft carrier contained over two hundred thousand pounds of asbestos insulation, while Fletcher-class destroyers and cruisers carried tens of thousands of pounds. Thermal block insulation, known commercially as Unibestos (made of amosite) and Kaylo, lined main propulsion turbines, steam lines, and reduction gears, creating an inescapable toxic environment throughout shipboard machinery spaces.

High-Risk Shipboard Ratings, Machinery Spaces, and Daily Exposure

Exposure risks aboard Navy ships were strongly tied to specific enlisted naval ratings and assigned shipboard watch stations. Personnel working below the waterline in the propulsion plant—including Boiler Technicians (BT), Machinist Mates (MM), Firemen (FN), and Enginemen (EN)—faced direct, daily occupational exposure. These sailors routinely cut pre-formed pipe coverings, mixed dry asbestos insulating cement powders with water in open buckets, repaired blown steam gaskets using wire brushes, and repacked leaking valve stems with braided asbestos rope.

The physical environment of a naval warship drastically magnified the danger. Machinery spaces were cramped, poorly ventilated compartments operating at ambient temperatures frequently exceeding one hundred degrees Fahrenheit. Constant mechanical vibration from powerful marine turbines and rough ocean swells loosened aging, friable pipe lagging, causing microscopic fibers to flake off into recirculated compartment air. Furthermore, off-duty crew members in berthing compartments slept in triple-tiered bunks directly beneath overhead steam lines wrapped in vibrating asbestos insulation, ensuring continuous round-the-clock inhalation.

Overhauls, Combat Damage, and Veteran Compensation Rights

Shipboard exposure reached catastrophic peaks during periodic shipyard overhauls and drydock modernizations. When warships entered naval shipyards, crew members and civilian yard workers were ordered to conduct complete 'rip-outs' of thermal systems. Using pneumatic chisels, utility knives, and hand scrapers, workers violently tore old, brittle asbestos insulation off miles of piping, filling ship compartments with dense clouds of white dust that looked like falling snow. In combat environments, artillery hits, torpedo impacts, and explosions pulverized insulation, exposing damage control teams to immense fiber surges.

Today, Navy veterans exposed to asbestos on ships have comprehensive rights to financial recovery and specialized healthcare. The Department of Veterans Affairs awards a 100 percent service-connected disability rating for malignant mesothelioma, providing monthly tax-free disability compensation and priority treatment at specialized VA cancer centers. In addition, because the Feres doctrine prevents suing the Navy directly, veterans can pursue substantial private compensation by filing claims against civilian equipment manufacturers that sold asbestos products to the Navy without warnings.

How Navy Veterans Can File an Asbestos Claim

Follow these five vital steps to document your naval service exposure and claim full VA and civilian asbestos benefits.

  1. Obtain Definitive Medical Pathology Confirmation

    Secure certified pathology reports confirming mesothelioma, asbestosis, or asbestos lung cancer from a qualified oncologist.

  2. Compile Complete Military Service Records

    Locate your DD-214, service personnel file, ship assignments, duty logs, and specialty rating designations.

  3. Draft a Shipboard Asbestos Exposure Statement

    Write a detailed personal narrative identifying specific ships, engine rooms, equipment models, and repair overhauls you performed.

  4. Obtain a Formal VA Medical Nexus Letter

    Have a physician write an official medical nexus letter stating your asbestos disease was caused by military service.

  5. Consult a Specialized Navy Asbestos Attorney

    Work with an experienced toxic tort lawyer to submit VA Form 21-526EZ and file private bankruptcy trust claims.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Why was there so much asbestos on Navy ships?

The US Navy mandated asbestos on ships through military specifications because of its unmatched fireproofing, heat resistance, and thermal insulation capabilities in combat spaces.

Q2: Which Navy shipboard jobs had the highest asbestos exposure?

Boiler Technicians (BT), Machinist Mates (MM), Pipefitters, Damage Controlmen (DC), and Electrician Mates (EM) faced the highest exposure working in propulsion spaces.

Q3: Did sailors sleeping in berthing areas get exposed to asbestos?

Yes, berthing compartments had overhead steam lines wrapped in asbestos that constantly shed microscopic fibers due to ship vibration and rough seas.

Q4: What were naval shipyard rip-outs?

Shipyard rip-outs were overhaul operations where sailors and shipyard workers stripped old, damaged asbestos insulation off pipes and boilers, creating thick airborne dust clouds.

Q5: What VA disability rating does a Navy veteran get for mesothelioma?

The Department of Veterans Affairs assigns an automatic 100 percent service-connected disability rating for malignant mesothelioma, paying maximum tax-free monthly benefits.

Q6: Can a Navy veteran sue the US Navy for asbestos exposure?

No, the Feres doctrine prevents military personnel from suing the military or government, but veterans can sue the private civilian companies that manufactured the products.

Q7: When did the Navy stop using asbestos on ships?

The Navy began phasing out asbestos in the early 1970s and officially banned new asbestos installations on warships by the late 1970s, though older ships retained it for decades.

Q8: Can surviving spouses of Navy veterans receive VA benefits?

Yes, surviving spouses are entitled to monthly Dependency and Indemnity Compensation (DIC), survivor healthcare, and burial benefits through the VA.
