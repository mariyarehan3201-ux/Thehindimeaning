Asbestos Roof Shingles

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Asbestos roof shingles, historically marketed under proprietary trade names such as Eternit, Transite, and Careystone, were widely installed across residential homes, barns, and commercial structures from the early 1900s through the late 1970s. Fabricated by combining Portland cement with twenty to forty percent chrysotile or amosite asbestos fibers under extreme hydraulic compression, asbestos cement roof shingles were prized for their complete fireproof nature, immunity to rot and insect damage, and extraordinary fifty-to-eighty-year service lifespans. Classified as non-friable materials that lock mineral fibers securely within a dense cement matrix, intact asbestos roof shingles pose minimal health hazards under normal weather exposure; however, power washing, sawing, or uncontained demolition creates dangerous airborne contamination.

Architectural Profiles: Dutch Lap, Hexagonal, and Corrugated

The engineering design of asbestos roof shingles provided twentieth-century architects with a lightweight, fireproof alternative to heavy natural quarried slate. Invented by Austrian industrialist Ludwig Hatschek in 1900, the Hatschek wet-slurry process allowed manufacturers to produce thin, rigid cementitious tiles measuring between one-eighth and three-sixteenths of an inch thick. The high tensile strength of embedded asbestos fibers acted as structural micro-rebar, preventing brittle Portland cement from shattering under thermal expansion, hail strikes, or heavy winter snow loads.

Manufacturers produced asbestos roofing shingles in several standardized geometric configurations designed to complement diverse architectural styles. Dutch lap and American method shingles featured overlapping rectangular tiles with straight or wavy edges, often embossed with faux slate textures. French method and hexagonal shingles featured interlocking diamond configurations with pre-punched copper nail holes, creating distinctive geometric rooflines. For agricultural and industrial roofs, large corrugated sheets measuring forty-two inches wide provided rapid, seamless weatherproofing across vast spans.

Aging, Weathering, and Environmental Hazards

While asbestos roof shingles earned a reputation for near-indestructibility, decades of continuous exposure to harsh natural elements eventually causes surface deterioration. Atmospheric acid rain, ultraviolet solar radiation, and cyclic freeze-thaw expansion slowly leach away the outer Portland cement binder, leaving bare, weathered asbestos fibers exposed on aged shingle surfaces. Biological colonization by moss, lichen, and fungi accelerates this breakdown, as microscopic root structures pry apart the cementitious matrix, shedding fine mineral particulates during heavy rainstorms.

The single greatest environmental hazard associated with asbestos roofing shingles is improper maintenance. Homeowners and roofing contractors frequently make the catastrophic mistake of using high-pressure power washers to clean moss or discoloration from aging roofs. Water pressure exceeding two thousand PSI instantly strips weathered cement, pulverizing the shingle face and generating a toxic airborne mist containing millions of respirable asbestos fibers. The runoff water carries friable asbestos slurry into rain gutters, downspouts, and perimeter soil, where it dries into a dangerous, easily dispersible powder.

Replacement Strategies: Roof Overlay versus Certified Abatement

Property owners addressing an aging asbestos shingle roof choose between structural roof overlay and complete hazardous abatement. Roof overlay—often called re-roofing or encapsulation—is widely recognized as a safe, cost-effective alternative when shingles remain structurally sound. In this approach, professional roofers install vertical wooden strapping or rigid foam insulation boards directly over the existing asbestos shingles, fastening new metal standing-seam roofing, architectural asphalt shingles, or modern fiber-cement panels over top. This entombs the old shingles without creating hazardous airborne dust.

Complete roof abatement becomes necessary when shingles are severely brittle, when roof decking rots, or when the structure cannot support the weight of a secondary roof layer. Certified abatement contractors wet the roof thoroughly with surfactant water sprays, carefully pull individual nails with specialized shingle ripper tools to remove panels whole without breaking them, and lower shingles to the ground in covered disposal chutes. Technicians wear full-body Tyvek suits and P100 respirators, double-bagging the waste in labeled 6-mil plastic bags for transport to certified hazardous landfills.

How to Safely Handle an Asbestos Shingle Roof

Follow these five certified procedural steps to inspect, maintain, or replace an aging asbestos shingle roof safely.

  1. Confirm Composition with Laboratory Testing

    Collect a small broken corner scrap from the ground or hire a consultant to confirm chrysotile content via NVLAP microscopy.

  2. Strictly Prohibit High-Pressure Washing

    Never power wash asbestos shingles; remove moss using gentle chemical biocides and low-pressure garden hose rinses.

  3. Apply Elastomeric Roof Encapsulant

    Seal weathered shingles by spraying high-build acrylic or silicone roof coatings to bind exposed fibers and prevent moisture leaks.

  4. Evaluate Metal or Shingle Roof Overlay

    Install wooden purlins and a new standing-seam metal roof directly over the existing shingles to entomb them safely.

  5. Contract Licensed Abatement for Tear-Off

    If removing the roof, hire a state-licensed abatement company utilizing wet-removal techniques and hazardous disposal manifests.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How can I tell if my roof shingles contain asbestos?

Rigid, thin cement shingles installed before 1980 that resemble slate with pre-punched nail holes are likely asbestos cement.

Q2: Are asbestos roof shingles dangerous to live under?

No, intact asbestos roof shingles bind fibers in a hard cement matrix, posing no breathing hazard to occupants inside the home.

Q3: Can you put a new roof over asbestos shingles?

Yes, installing wooden strapping and a new metal roof or asphalt shingles directly over intact asbestos shingles is a safe encapsulation method.

Q4: Can I power wash my asbestos shingle roof?

Never power wash asbestos shingles; high pressure pulverizes the cement face, blasting toxic fibers into the air and surrounding soil.

Q5: How long do asbestos roof shingles last?

Asbestos cement shingles are exceptionally durable, frequently lasting sixty to eighty years or longer with minimal maintenance.

Q6: What year were asbestos roof shingles banned?

Manufacturing of asbestos-cement roofing shingles in the United States concluded between 1978 and 1982.

Q7: How much does it cost to remove an asbestos shingle roof?

Professional tear-off and disposal typically costs between $9 and $20 per square foot depending on roof pitch and height.

Q8: Do asbestos roof shingles burn?

No, asbestos cement roof shingles are completely non-combustible and carry Class A fire-resistance ratings.
