Selling a Home in California Taxes

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Selling residential real estate in the Golden State involves navigating one of the most multifaceted and demanding tax environments in the United States. Between soaring property appreciations and aggressive state revenue enforcement, California property owners frequently encounter substantial capital gains liabilities upon closing a transaction. While federal tax provisions like the Internal Revenue Code Section 121 exclusion offer significant tax relief for qualifying primary residences, California imposes state income tax rates reaching as high as 13.3 percent with zero preferential rates for long-term capital gains. Successfully preserving your equity demands a meticulous understanding of cost basis calculations, Franchise Tax Board withholding regulations, documentary transfer taxes, and proactive filing strategies.

Federal Section 121 Exclusion vs California Capital Gains Treatment

When selling a residential property in California, tax liability is calculated based on realized capital gain—the net difference between the adjusted gross sales price and the seller's adjusted cost basis. At the federal level, homeowners benefit from Internal Revenue Code (IRC) Section 121, which allows single taxpayers to exclude up to $250,000, and married couples filing jointly to exclude up to $500,000 of capital gain from taxable income. To qualify, the homeowner must satisfy the ownership and use tests by owning the home and occupying it as their principal residence for at least two of the five years immediately preceding the date of sale.

California conforms to the federal Section 121 principal residence exclusion guidelines, allowing qualifying sellers to deduct that same $250,000 or $500,000 on their state tax return. However, a critical divergence exists in how remaining taxable gains are taxed. While the Internal Revenue Service taxes long-term capital gains on assets held longer than one year at preferential federal rates of 0%, 15%, or 20% (plus a potential 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax surtax), the California Franchise Tax Board (FTB) does not offer preferential rates for long-term capital gains. In California, all net real estate profits are taxed as ordinary personal income, with marginal state tax brackets ranging from 1% up to 12.3%, plus an additional 1% Mental Health Services tax on taxable income exceeding $1,000,000, resulting in a top state marginal rate of 13.3%.

Understanding these tax disparities is particularly crucial for sellers liquidating highly appreciated properties, inherited estates, or rental conversions. When preparing for selling California residential property, factoring state ordinary tax brackets into your closing estimates prevents devastating surprises when April tax returns are filed. Documentary Transfer Taxes, County Levies, and City "Mansion Taxes"

In addition to state and federal income taxes on net capital gains, home sellers in California are responsible for county and municipal documentary transfer taxes levied upon the recording of the grant deed at closing. The California Revenue and Taxation Code establishes a baseline county documentary transfer tax rate of $1.10 per $1,000 of property valuation ($0.55 per $500). While this county tax applies uniformly across the state, California charter cities possess the legal authority to enact supplemental municipal transfer taxes that substantially inflate transaction overhead.

In recent years, several major California municipalities have passed progressive transfer taxes commonly labeled mansion taxes. The most prominent example is the City of Los Angeles Measure ULA, enacted in April 2023. Under Measure ULA, residential property sales exceeding $5,150,000 (adjusted periodically for inflation) incur a 4.0% supplemental transfer tax, while sales exceeding $10,300,000 face a 5.5% tax. Crucially, Measure ULA is assessed against the gross sales price rather than the net profit, meaning a homeowner selling a multi-million-dollar property at a financial loss is still legally required to pay hundreds of thousands of dollars in transfer tax at the close of escrow. Navigating these city-specific transfer taxes often overlaps with complex estate distribution issues like executing a California estate and property transfer.

To calculate true net proceeds, sellers must construct an adjusted cost basis model that accounts for the original purchase price, documented capital improvements, and allowable selling expenses. Routine maintenance—such as repainting bedrooms or fixing leaky faucets—cannot be added to basis, but substantial capital additions like capital home improvement deductions, installing solar arrays, or replacing the roof directly increase basis and reduce taxable gain. California Form 593 Real Estate Withholding and Escrow Mechanics

To ensure home sellers do not leave the state without satisfying their tax liabilities, the California Franchise Tax Board enforces strict mandatory withholding rules at the close of escrow. Under California Revenue and Taxation Code Section 18662, escrow closing officers are legally required to withhold funds from the sale proceeds and remit them directly to the FTB within twenty days following the end of the month in which escrow closes. The default statutory withholding rate is 3.33 percent of the total gross sales price, which on a $1,500,000 home sale equals a massive $49,950 withheld at closing.

However, sellers can avoid this large cash holdback by completing California Form 593 (Real Estate Withholding Statement). If the transaction qualifies for a full statutory exemption—most commonly by certifying that the property was the seller's principal residence under Section 121 rules, or that the property is being sold at a net financial loss—the seller checks the appropriate box on Part III of Form 593, completely exempting the sale from escrow withholding.

For investment properties that do not qualify for the principal residence exclusion, Form 593 allows sellers to elect an alternative withholding calculation based on the actual estimated gain rather than the gross sales price. By calculating estimated net gain and multiplying it by the maximum individual tax rate of 12.3% (or 8.84% for corporations), sellers frequently preserve substantial liquidity compared to the blunt 3.33% gross withholding mandate. Any withheld funds are credited against the seller's final California state income tax return filed the following year.

How to Calculate and Minimize Taxes When Selling California Real Estate

Follow this five-step financial protocol to compute your tax liability, complete escrow disclosures, and claim all allowable exclusions when selling property in California.

  1. Establish Your Adjusted Cost Basis and Gather Receipts

    Locate your original purchase settlement closing statement and aggregate all invoices, receipts, and permits for capital home improvements installed during your ownership.

  2. Verify Qualification for the Section 121 Tax Exclusion

    Confirm that you owned and occupied the property as your principal primary residence for at least two out of the five years preceding the scheduled close of escrow.

  3. Complete California Form 593 Real Estate Withholding Certificate

    Fill out FTB Form 593 with your escrow officer, certifying your principal residence exemption or electing the alternative net gain calculation to prevent excess withholding.

  4. Calculate Local City and County Documentary Transfer Taxes

    Check county recorder rates ($1.10 per $1,000) and investigate local municipal transfer taxes, such as Los Angeles Measure ULA, to budget closing costs accurately.

  5. File Year-End Returns with the IRS and California Franchise Tax Board

    Work with a certified public accountant to report the home sale on federal Schedule D and Form 8949, reconciling any state withholding credits on California Form 540.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Does California offer a lower tax rate for long-term capital gains on home sales?

No. Unlike the federal government which offers preferential rates of 0%, 15%, or 20% on long-term capital gains, California taxes all capital gains as ordinary state personal income, with marginal brackets reaching up to 12.3% plus an additional 1% Mental Health Services tax on income over $1,000,000.

Q2: How does the Section 121 exclusion work on California state tax returns?

California fully conforms to federal IRC Section 121 guidelines. If you meet the two-out-of-five-year ownership and primary residency tests, you can exclude up to $250,000 of capital gain if filing single, or up to $500,000 if married filing jointly, from both federal and California taxable income.

Q3: What is California Form 593 and why does escrow require it?

California Form 593 is the Real Estate Withholding Statement mandated by the Franchise Tax Board. California law requires escrow agents to withhold 3.33% of the gross sale price unless the seller certifies an exemption, such as the sale of a primary residence or a sale at a loss, on Form 593.

Q4: What home improvements can be added to my California cost basis?

Capital improvements that add permanent value, prolong the home's useful life, or adapt it to new uses can be added to your basis. Examples include room additions, new roofs, kitchen remodels, swimming pools, HVAC replacements, and seismic retrofits. Routine repairs like painting do not qualify.

Q5: What is the Los Angeles Measure ULA mansion tax on property sales?

Measure ULA is a supplemental documentary transfer tax enacted by the City of Los Angeles. It assesses a 4.0% tax on real estate sales exceeding $5,150,000 and a 5.5% tax on sales exceeding $10,300,000. It is calculated on the gross selling price regardless of whether the seller made a profit.

Q6: Can I use a 1031 exchange to defer capital gains on my primary residence in California?

No. Section 1031 like-kind exchanges are strictly limited to properties held for productive use in a trade, business, or investment, such as rental houses or commercial buildings. You cannot execute a 1031 exchange on your personal primary residence.

Q7: Do I have to pay California taxes if I moved to another state before selling my home?

Yes. Real estate is sourced to the physical state where the land and building are located. Even if you established residency in a zero-income-tax state like Nevada, Florida, or Texas, any taxable gain on the sale of California real property remains fully taxable by the California FTB.

Q8: Who pays the documentary transfer tax in California home sales?

By default and local custom in Southern California, the seller traditionally pays the county and city documentary transfer taxes, whereas in parts of Northern California, the buyer and seller frequently split the cost. However, transfer tax responsibility is negotiable in the purchase contract.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Selling a home in California represents a major financial liquidity event that carries profound state and federal tax obligations. Because California treats capital gains as ordinary income with marginal rates reaching up to 13.3 percent, failing to plan proactively can result in substantial equity erosion. By meticulously maintaining records of capital improvements to increase your adjusted basis, correctly claiming the Section 121 primary residence exclusion, properly completing Form 593 to control escrow withholding, and accounting for local municipal transfer taxes, you can safeguard your hard-earned real estate wealth.
