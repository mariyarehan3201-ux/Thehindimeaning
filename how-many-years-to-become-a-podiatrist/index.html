How Many Years to Become a Podiatrist?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how many years to become a podiatrist reveals that completing the medical education and surgical training required to practice as a licensed Doctor of Podiatric Medicine (DPM) takes precisely 11 years following high school graduation. This intensive medical pipeline consists of 4 years of undergraduate college study, 4 years of accredited podiatric medical school, and 3 years of a comprehensive hospital-based surgical residency (PMSR/RRA).

Stage One: Undergraduate Pre-Med Degree (4 Years)

The path to becoming a podiatric foot and ankle surgeon begins with a 4-year bachelor's degree at an accredited university. While aspiring podiatrists can major in any academic discipline, the vast majority major in the biological sciences, chemistry, or pre-medical health sciences to fulfill mandatory medical school prerequisites.

Prerequisite coursework includes general biology, human anatomy and physiology, general chemistry, organic chemistry, physics, biochemistry, and statistics, all accompanied by laboratory science components. During their junior year, students study for and take the Medical College Admission Test (MCAT), gaining shadowing experience with practicing DPMs before applying through the American Association of Colleges of Podiatric Medicine Application Service (AACPMAS).

Stage Two: Podiatric Medical School (4 Years)

Upon admission to one of the nine accredited podiatric medical colleges in the United States, students embark on four years of medical study. The curriculum closely parallels that of MD and DO allopathic medical schools. Years 1 and 2 focus heavily on foundational biomedical sciences: gross anatomy (including full human cadaver dissection), histology, pharmacology, pathology, immunology, and microbiology, alongside dedicated coursework in lower extremity biomechanics and gait analysis.

Years 3 and 4 transition students into clinical hospital clerkships. Podiatry students complete rotations in general internal medicine, emergency medicine, general surgery, infectious disease, orthopedic surgery, and vascular surgery. During medical school, students sit for Part 1 and Part 2 of the American Podiatric Medical Licensing Examination (APMLE), graduating with the Doctor of Podiatric Medicine (DPM) degree.

Stage Three: The 3-Year Surgical Residency (PMSR/RRA)

Graduating from podiatric medical school with a DPM does not permit you to practice independently. Every modern podiatrist must complete a standardized 3-year hospital-based Podiatric Medicine and Surgery Residency with the added credential in Reconstructive Rearfoot and Ankle Surgery (PMSR/RRA). Residents work 60 to 80 hours per week managing emergency trauma, inpatient admissions, and performing hundreds of complex surgical operations.

Under the Council on Podiatric Medical Education (CPME), residents must log a minimum of 300 surgical cases—including bunion corrections, flatfoot reconstructions, tendon transfers, Charcot foot reconstructions, and complex ankle fractures. After completing residency, passing APMLE Part 3, and securing state medical licensure, many podiatrists pursue board certification through the American Board of Foot and Ankle Surgery (ABFAS), cementing their status as premier lower extremity surgical specialists.

How to Become a Licensed Podiatrist

Follow these five chronological milestones to navigate the 11-year podiatric medical career pipeline.

  1. Complete a Pre-Med Bachelor's Degree with Strong GPA

    Maintain a 3.4+ GPA in biology, chemistry, and physics, and score competitively on the MCAT.

  2. Graduate from an Accredited 4-Year DPM Medical School

    Complete 2 years of biomedical sciences and 2 years of hospital clerkships to earn your Doctor of Podiatric Medicine.

  3. Pass Parts 1 and 2 of the APMLE National Board Exams

    Score passing marks on the American Podiatric Medical Licensing Examinations during your 2nd and 4th years.

  4. Complete a 3-Year Hospital Surgical Residency (PMSR/RRA)

    Log hundreds of complex reconstructive foot and ankle surgeries across trauma, diabetic wound care, and orthopedics.

  5. Secure State Medical Licensure and ABFAS Board Certification

    Obtain state medical board credentials and achieve board certification in foot surgery and reconstructive rearfoot/ankle surgery.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How many total years does it take to become a podiatrist?

It takes precisely 11 years after high school: 4 years of college, 4 years of podiatry school, and 3 years of residency.

Q2: Is a podiatrist a medical doctor?

A podiatrist is a specialized physician holding a DPM (Doctor of Podiatric Medicine), licensed to perform surgery and prescribe medications.

Q3: What is the MCAT score needed for podiatry school?

Most podiatric medical colleges look for MCAT scores between 495 and 505, along with a science GPA of 3.2 or higher.

Q4: How long is a podiatry residency?

In the United States, all accredited podiatric surgical residencies (PMSR/RRA) last exactly 3 years.

Q5: Can podiatrists perform ankle surgery?

Yes, modern DPMs completing PMSR/RRA residencies are fully credentialed to perform complex reconstructive rearfoot and ankle surgeries.

Q6: Do podiatrists do amputations?

Yes, podiatrists perform partial foot, toe, and below-knee amputations, particularly in severe diabetic limb salvage cases.

Q7: Is podiatry school as hard as MD medical school?

Yes, the first two years of biomedical sciences and board exams closely mirror MD and DO allopathic medical curricula.

Q8: Can a podiatrist prescribe narcotics and medications?

Yes, licensed podiatrists hold DEA registrations and prescribe antibiotics, pain medications, anti-inflammatories, and physical therapy.
