How to Get Rid of Asbestos Tile?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 24, 2026

To get rid of asbestos tile safely, property owners must choose between two approved approaches: encapsulating the floor beneath a new finished flooring system or executing full professional abatement using wet removal methods. Vintage 9x9-inch or 12x12-inch asphalt and vinyl composition tiles (VCT) produced before 1980 contain chrysotile asbestos bound in a vinyl matrix. If tiles are intact, encapsulation is often the safest, most economical path; if broken or water-damaged, controlled removal is essential.

Encapsulation vs Removal: Evaluating the Safest Floor Strategies

Vintage resilient flooring manufactured prior to 1980—including asphalt-asbestos tile and vinyl composition tile (frequently sized in distinct nine-by-nine-inch dimensions)—was installed in millions of residential basements, kitchens, and commercial buildings. Under federal EPA and OSHA guidelines, intact vinyl asbestos tile is categorized as Category I non-friable asbestos-containing material. Because the mineral fibers are embedded tightly within a dense synthetic vinyl or asphalt resin binder, undisturbed tiles do not release fibers into the air during normal foot traffic. Consequently, environmental agencies strongly recommend non-destructive encapsulation whenever the original floor remains structurally sound.

Encapsulation involves sealing the asbestos tile beneath a modern, durable flooring assembly without disturbing the original substrate. Homeowners can install floating luxury vinyl plank (LVP), interlocking laminate, sheet vinyl, or wall-to-wall carpeting with dense pad underlayments directly over the existing tiles. Alternatively, applying a specialized primer followed by a high-strength self-leveling cementitious underlayment creates a seamless, reinforced subfloor that completely encases the asbestos tiles and provides an ideal surface for ceramic tile or engineered hardwood. Encapsulation completely avoids the high costs, environmental permits, and airborne risks associated with physical demolition.

Professional Abatement Procedures and Black Mastic Mitigation

When asbestos floor tiles are severely degraded, lifting, or interfering with foundation waterproofing and structural plumbing work, complete physical removal becomes necessary. Abatement must adhere to OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1101 Class II work protocols. The remediation team begins by sealing all HVAC supply and return registers, doorways, and electrical fixtures with six-mil polyethylene plastic to isolate the room. Workers wear personal protective equipment, including half-mask or full-face air-purifying respirators fitted with NIOSH-certified P100 particulate filters and disposable protective coveralls.

Safe physical tile removal utilizes wet manual scraping or specialized radiant heat equipment. Technicians apply surfactant-amended water to keep tiles continuously damp, then gently slide flat spade scrapers or stiff-bladed putty knives beneath the tiles to pop them off the subfloor intact. Beneath the tiles lies the original black cutback mastic, an asphalt-based adhesive that often contains high concentrations of chrysotile asbestos. OSHA regulations strictly forbid the mechanical dry grinding, rotary wire brushing, or belt sanding of black mastic. Instead, certified abatement crews apply low-odor chemical mastic emulsifiers (such as soy-based or citrus-based solvents) that liquefy the mastic into a manageable slurry that is absorbed with inert clay or squeegeed into disposal containers.

All removed tiles, mastic sludge, and contaminated containment plastic must be treated as regulated hazardous waste. Debris is placed into heavy, six-mil polyethylene bags carrying official OSHA asbestos danger warnings, securely goose-neck tied with duct tape, and placed into a second labeled outer bag. The waste cannot be disposed of with municipal trash; it must be transported by a certified hazardous waste hauler under a uniform hazardous waste manifest to a state-permitted landfill licensed to accept asbestos materials.

How to Safely Handle and Get Rid of Asbestos Floor Tiles

Step-by-step procedure for assessing, encapsulating, or abating asbestos floor tiles.

  1. Confirm Asbestos Content Through Certified Laboratory PLM Testing

    Collect a small representative corner fragment of the tile and black mastic using wet sampling techniques and submit it to an NVLAP-accredited laboratory for Polarized Light Microscopy testing.

  2. Assess Tile Integrity for Cracking, Friability, and Water Damage

    Examine the floor surface to determine whether tiles are intact and firmly adhered, or if loose tiles, chipping, and moisture issues necessitate full physical removal.

  3. Choose Between In-Place Encapsulation or Professional Abatement

    Select encapsulation with luxury vinyl plank or self-leveling cement underlayment for sound floors, or hire a licensed abatement contractor if removal is unavoidable.

  4. Apply Chemical Softening Agents and Wet Manual Scraping

    If removing, use surfactant wetting sprays or infrared heating to soften the tile adhesive, prying tiles loose manually with flat scrapers without fracturing them.

  5. Package Waste in Double 6-Mil Poly Bags for Manifested Disposal

    Seal all wet tile debris and mastic sludge in double 6-mil polyethylene bags marked with OSHA asbestos danger labels and arrange transport to a permitted hazardous landfill.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I remove asbestos floor tiles myself?

While some jurisdictions allow single-family homeowners to perform work, DIY removal is strongly discouraged because improper dry prying or sanding can spread toxic fibers throughout your home.

Q2: What are the common dimensions of vintage asbestos floor tiles?

The classic format for asbestos-containing asphalt and vinyl composition tile is nine inches by nine inches (9x9), though many early twelve-by-twelve-inch (12x12) tiles also contained asbestos.

Q3: Is black cutback mastic beneath floor tiles dangerous?

Black cutback mastic frequently contains chrysotile asbestos; while non-friable when undisturbed, dry sanding or grinding releases heavy concentrations of toxic airborne fibers.

Q4: What is the best modern flooring to install over asbestos tiles?

Floating luxury vinyl plank (LVP), interlocking laminate, or engineered wood with an attached acoustic underlayment are ideal because they install directly without adhesive or mechanical fasteners.

Q5: How much does professional asbestos tile removal cost?

Professional abatement of asbestos tile and mastic typically costs between $6 and $15 per square foot, including containment, wetting, HEPA filtration, and hazardous disposal.

Q6: Can I use power sanders to remove the black glue from concrete?

No, power sanding or grinding black cutback mastic is strictly illegal under OSHA regulations because mechanical abrasion pulverizes the adhesive into respirable dust.

Q7: Does undisturbed asbestos floor tile pose a health risk?

No, undisturbed, intact asbestos floor tile in good physical condition poses zero health hazard because the microscopic fibers are locked tightly inside the vinyl matrix.

Q8: How do I legally dispose of removed asbestos floor tiles?

Asbestos tiles must be double-bagged in 6-mil labeled hazardous waste bags and hauled by a licensed hazardous transporter to a landfill permitted to accept asbestos waste.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Getting rid of asbestos floor tiles requires careful risk assessment rather than immediate panic. If your vintage vinyl or asphalt tiles are intact and firmly bonded, encapsulating them beneath a new modern floor covering is the safest, most practical, and cost-effective approach. If complete removal is essential due to structural alterations or severe deterioration, always engage certified asbestos abatement professionals who utilize wet manual methods, negative air filtration, and lawful hazardous waste disposal.
