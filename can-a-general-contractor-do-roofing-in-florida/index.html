Can a General Contractor Do Roofing in Florida?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Navigating the statutory boundaries of construction trade licensing in Florida requires a thorough understanding of state laws governing structural integrity, hurricane building codes, and professional scope. Property owners frequently wonder whether a licensed Certified General Contractor (CGC) or Registered General Contractor (RGC) has the legal authority to install, repair, or replace a roof. Under Chapter 489 of the Florida Statutes and administrative rules established by the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) Construction Industry Licensing Board (CILB), the answer depends heavily on whether the roofing work is part of a newly built structure or an existing standalone roof replacement.

Florida Statute Chapter 489 and the Scope of General Contractors

Florida enforces some of the most rigorous construction licensing laws in the United States, shaped by decades of severe tropical weather and the Florida Building Code High-Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ) standards. Under Section 489.105(3)(a) of the Florida Statutes, a Certified General Contractor (CGC) is authorized to construct, repair, alter, remodel, or demolish any commercial, residential, or industrial structure without height or size limitations. When a general contractor is constructing a brand-new building from the ground up, the statutory scope of work allows the general contractor to self-perform or subcontract all phases of construction, including structural roof trusses, roof decking, secondary water barriers, and final roof coverings such as asphalt shingles, tiles, or metal panels.

However, the legal landscape changes dramatically when the project involves an existing building requiring a standalone roof replacement, re-roofing, or roof leak repairs. Under Florida law, a general contractor cannot contract for, pull a permit for, or self-perform standalone roofing work on an existing structure unless they also hold an active Certified Roofing Contractor (CCC) license or Registered Roofing Contractor (RCC) license. If a general contractor takes on an extensive whole-home structural renovation that includes a roof replacement, they may act as the primary prime contractor on the master building permit, but they are statutorily required to subcontract the actual roofing portion to a licensed roofing contractor. Many building professionals studying contractor business growth strategies recognize that obtaining dual licensing (CGC and CCC) provides a significant competitive edge across the state.

This strict division of trade scope was established by the Florida Legislature and the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) to protect property owners from structural failures during major hurricanes. Installing roof coverings requires specialized trade expertise, wind uplift engineering calculations, approved fastener patterns, and detailed knowledge of self-adhering underlayment membranes. Subcontracting Rules, Permitting Requirements, and Homeowner Protections

Municipal and county building departments across Florida—from Miami-Dade and Broward to Orange, Hillsborough, and Duval—strictly scrutinize permit applications to enforce trade scope limitations. When a property owner seeks a permit for a standalone roof replacement, the local building official will reject an application submitted by a general contractor unless the GC provides proof of a concurrent roofing contractor license. In multi-trade remodeling permits where roofing constitutes an ancillary trade, the building official requires the general contractor to formally register the licensed roofing subcontractor on the permit jacket before roofing inspections will be scheduled or passed.

This regulatory enforcement directly affects homeowner safety, building code compliance, and insurance claims. Florida homeowners must ensure that any contractor replacing or altering a roof adheres to local drainage and rainwater management mandates. Proper installation of drip edges, flashing, and gutters prevents catastrophic water intrusion behind fascia boards and wall cladding. Property owners evaluating roof upgrades often explore whether metal roof drainage systems are necessary, and they review guidelines for installing rain gutters on metal roofs to guarantee complete water shedding during severe tropical downpours.

Hiring an improperly licensed contractor carries severe legal and financial risks for Florida property owners. Under Florida Statute Section 489.128, contracts entered into with unlicensed contractors are legally unenforceable by the contractor, yet homeowners may find their manufacturer material warranties completely voided by shingle or tile manufacturers if installation was not executed by a certified roofer. Furthermore, property insurance carriers investigating post-storm water damage can deny claims if unpermitted or improperly licensed roofing work compromised the roof envelope. How Homeowners Should Verify Florida Contractor Credentials

Before executing any contract or paying a deposit for roofing work in the state of Florida, property owners must take proactive steps to confirm the professional credentials of the builder. The Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation maintains a publicly accessible online license verification portal at myfloridalicense.com. Homeowners can enter the contractor's six-digit license number or personal name to inspect license status, expiration dates, workers' compensation compliance, and any public disciplinary history or consumer complaints.

Homeowners should also verify that the contractor carries active commercial general liability insurance and workers' compensation coverage that specifically covers roofing operations. Roofing carries significantly higher workers' compensation rates than general carpentry or drywall installation. Unscrupulous contractors sometimes classify their employees under general construction codes to evade high insurance premiums, leaving homeowners exposed to personal injury lawsuits if a laborer falls from the roof. Requesting a Certificate of Insurance issued directly from the contractor's insurance broker listing the homeowner as a certificate holder ensures genuine coverage.

How to Verify Contractor Licensing for Roofing Work in Florida

Follow this verification process to confirm your contractor has the lawful Florida DBPR credentials to complete or oversee roofing projects.

  1. Obtain the Full Legal Name and License Number of the Contractor

    Ask the contractor for their state license number, which will begin with CGC for Certified General Contractor or CCC for Certified Roofing Contractor.

  2. Check the Florida DBPR License Search Portal Online

    Visit myfloridalicense.com and verify that the license status is Current and Active, with zero unresolved administrative complaints or disciplinary suspensions.

  3. Determine Whether the Project Involves New Construction or Existing Reroofing

    If your project is a standalone roof replacement on an existing building, ensure the contracting entity holds a CCC license or has named a licensed CCC roofing subcontractor.

  4. Verify the Subcontractor Agreement and Permit Application Details

    Review the permit paperwork filed with your city or county building department to ensure the roofing trade is officially permitted under a licensed roofer.

  5. Confirm Active Workers' Compensation and General Liability Policies

    Request a Certificate of Insurance directly from the contractor's insurer verifying active liability and roofing-specific workers' compensation coverage.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can a Florida general contractor replace an existing roof on my home?

A Florida Certified General Contractor cannot legally self-perform a standalone roof replacement on an existing home unless they also hold a Certified Roofing Contractor (CCC) license. However, a general contractor can contract for the job as part of a larger multi-trade remodel if they subcontract the roofing work to a licensed CCC.

Q2: What is the difference between a CGC and a CCC license in Florida?

A CGC (Certified General Contractor) license authorizes a professional to build, alter, or demolish entire structural buildings statewide. A CCC (Certified Roofing Contractor) license specifically authorizes a contractor to install, repair, and maintain roof coverings, waterproofing systems, and roof drainage envelopes.

Q3: Can a general contractor pull a roofing permit in Florida?

In Florida, local building departments will not allow a general contractor to pull a standalone roofing permit unless they hold an active CCC license. On larger multi-trade building permits, the GC can pull the master permit but must list a licensed roofing subcontractor to pull the roofing trade permit.

Q4: What happens if a general contractor does roofing without a roofing license?

Performing roofing work outside of one's statutory license scope constitutes unlicensed contracting under Florida Statute Chapter 489. Violators face DBPR administrative fines up to $10,000, suspension or revocation of their general contractor license, and potential misdemeanor criminal charges.

Q5: Can a general contractor install roof shingles on a new home they are building?

Yes. When building a new structure from the ground up, Florida Statute Section 489.105(3)(a) permits a Certified General Contractor to self-perform all phases of construction, including framing, decking, and installing final roof coverings like asphalt shingles, metal, or tiles.

Q6: Does my homeowners insurance cover roof damage if installed by an unlicensed contractor?

Property insurance carriers in Florida can deny roof leak or storm damage claims if the roof was installed without mandatory municipal building permits or by an unlicensed contractor, as policy terms generally require all structural work to comply with building codes and lawful licensing.

Q7: Can a building contractor (CBC) or residential contractor (CRC) do roofing in Florida?

Like general contractors (CGC), Certified Building Contractors (CBC) and Certified Residential Contractors (CRC) may only self-perform roofing on new structures within their scope. For existing structures, standalone roofing work strictly requires a Certified Roofing Contractor (CCC).

Q8: How do I check if my Florida contractor is properly licensed for roofing?

You can check any contractor's licensing status online by visiting the Florida Department of Business and Professional Regulation website at myfloridalicense.com and searching by contractor name or license number to confirm an active CCC roofing license.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Understanding whether a general contractor can do roofing in Florida hinges on the nature of the project. While a Certified General Contractor possesses broad authority to construct an entire building and self-perform the roof on new construction, Florida law strictly prohibits general contractors from performing standalone roof replacements or repairs on existing homes unless they also possess a Certified Roofing Contractor license. By verifying DBPR license classifications, ensuring proper trade permits are pulled, and confirming adequate workers' compensation coverage, homeowners can protect their properties, preserve roof warranties, and maintain full insurance compliance.
