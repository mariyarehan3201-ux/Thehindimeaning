Statute of Limitations on DUI?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, there is a statute of limitations on DUI offenses in the United States. For standard misdemeanor DUI charges, state statutes of limitations typically require prosecutors to formally file criminal charges within one to three years from the date of the arrest. For felony DUIs involving serious injury or death, deadlines extend to three to six years, or have no statutory limit at all.

The Constitutional and Statutory Function of Limitations Periods

In American criminal jurisprudence, the Statute of Limitations serves as an indispensable constitutional safeguard rooted in the Sixth Amendment right to a speedy trial and Fourteenth Amendment procedural due process. A statute of limitations is a legislative time clock that establishes the maximum legal window within which the state government (the prosecutor) must formally commence criminal proceedings against a defendant.

The philosophical and evidentiary rationale behind limiting DUI prosecutions is sound. As years pass, human memories fade, civilian eyewitnesses move away or pass away, police dashcam and bodycam video footage is routinely purged, and chemical blood samples degrade in laboratory storage.

If the state fails to file formal criminal charges (typically via an Information, Indictment, or criminal Complaint) before the statutory time clock runs out, the defendant possesses an absolute legal right to have the case dismissed with prejudice. Once dismissed under the statute of limitations, the defendant can never be prosecuted for that specific offense. However, understanding how the DUI limitation clock operates requires distinguishing between criminal charges, administrative DMV actions, and arrest warrants. The table below delineates the statutory time limits for DUI offenses across representative jurisdictions.

DUI Offense Severity Level Typical State Statutory Deadline State-Specific Code Examples Legal Effect If Deadline Missed
First-Offense Misdemeanor DUI 1 to 2 Years from arrest date California: 1 year (PC 802); Texas: 2 years (CCP 12.02) Case dismissed permanently; cannot be refiled
Misdemeanor DUI with Blood Draw Delays 1 to 3 Years from arrest date Florida: 2 years (§ 775.15); Washington: 2 years Lab delays cannot legally extend statutory deadline
Felony DUI (Repeat Offender / Injury) 3 to 6 Years from crash date Texas: 3 years (CCP 12.01); California: 3 years Extended filing window for felony grand jury indictments
Vehicular Homicide / Manslaughter 5 Years to No Statute of Limitations Florida: No SOL on capital/life; Texas: No SOL Prosecutors can file charges decades after fatal crash

The Tolling Trap: When the Criminal Time Clock Stops

While the statute of limitations establishes a firm statutory deadline, defendants frequently fall into a dangerous legal misconception assuming that simply hiding or moving out of state will run out the clock.

In criminal law, this misconception fails due to the doctrine of Tolling. When a statute of limitations is 'tolled,' the legal time clock temporarily stops ticking. Under virtually all state penal codes, if a person flees the state, becomes a fugitive from justice, resides outside the court's jurisdiction, or conceals their physical identity, the statutory limitations period is paused for the entire duration of their absence.

For example, if you are arrested for a misdemeanor DUI in California (which carries a strict one-year statute of limitations) and you move to Texas two months later, the clock freezes at two months. The remaining ten months do not elapse while you reside out of state. The moment you return to California, the time clock resumes exactly where it stopped. The table below illustrates how tolling affects criminal prosecution deadlines.

Defendant Action / Circumstance Status of DUI Statutory Clock Legal Justification Under Criminal Law
Continuously residing in state at known address Clock ticks continuously toward expiration State has full legal opportunity to locate and charge
Moving out of state or fleeing jurisdiction Clock is completely tolled (stopped) Defendant cannot benefit from evading court process
Living under false name or alias Clock is completely tolled (stopped) Active concealment pauses statutory time periods
State files formal Complaint / Indictment in court Clock is permanently satisfied and halted Filing charges stops the limitations clock forever

Arrest Warrants and DMV Administrative Deadlines Never Expire

The single most catastrophic error individuals make regarding DUI statutes of limitations is confusing the deadline to file charges with an outstanding Bench Warrant or Arrest Warrant.

The statute of limitations applies exclusively to the time period between the date of the alleged crime and the date prosecutors formally file charges with the court. Once the prosecutor files an Information or Complaint within the statutory window, the statute of limitations is satisfied forever. If the court issues an Arrest Warrant or Failure to Appear (FTA) Bench Warrant because you did not show up for your arraignment, that warrant NEVER expires. An active DUI bench warrant remains valid for 10, 20, or 40 years until you are physically arrested or an attorney files a motion to quash it.

Furthermore, administrative driver license suspensions enforced by the state Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) operate under entirely separate administrative timelines. If you fail to request a formal administrative review hearing within your state's strict 10 to 14-day window following your arrest, your driver license is automatically suspended, completely independent of whatever happens with the criminal statute of limitations.

How to Assert a Statute of Limitations Defense for DUI

Step-by-step criminal litigation protocol to dismiss an untimely DUI prosecution.

  1. Determine the Exact Date of the Alleged Incident

    Review the initial police citation or arrest booking sheet to identify the precise calendar date and time of the alleged driving offense.

  2. Examine Court Docket for Date Criminal Charges Were Filed

    Have your defense attorney check the county clerk of court docket to verify the exact filing date of the formal Information or Complaint.

  3. Verify You Maintained Continuous In-State Residence

    Gather utility bills, residential leases, and state tax returns proving you lived openly in the state so prosecutors cannot claim tolling.

  4. File a Formal Motion to Dismiss Based on Statute of Limitations

    If the filing date exceeds the statutory period (e.g., over 1 year in CA or 2 years in TX), file a motion to dismiss the case permanently with prejudice.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: How long can a prosecutor wait to charge you with DUI?

For standard misdemeanor DUIs, prosecutors typically have 1 to 2 years from the date of the incident to formally file criminal charges.

Q2: Do DUI arrest warrants ever expire?

No. Once a court issues a bench warrant for your arrest, it never expires; it remains active in national police databases until cleared.

Q3: Can blood test delays cause a DUI to miss the statute of limitations?

Yes! If state toxicology labs take 14 to 18 months to process blood and the statute of limitations is 1 year, the case must be dismissed.

Q4: What happens if the statute of limitations expires on my DUI?

Your attorney files a Motion to Dismiss, and the judge will dismiss the criminal charges permanently; the state can never refile the case.

Q5: Does leaving the state pause the DUI statute of limitations?

Yes. Under criminal tolling laws, moving out of state or concealing your whereabouts stops the statutory time clock.

Q6: Is there a statute of limitations on felony DUI?

Felony DUIs typically carry longer limits of 3 to 6 years, and fatal vehicular homicide cases often carry no statute of limitations at all.

Q7: Does the statute of limitations affect my driver license suspension?

No. DMV administrative license suspensions are separate civil proceedings that must be contested within 10 to 14 days of arrest.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

There is a definitive statute of limitations on DUI offenses, requiring prosecutors to file misdemeanor charges within one to two years and felony charges within three to six years. However, tolling rules freeze the clock if you leave the state, and active arrest warrants never expire. If you were arrested for DUI months ago and have not received court dates, retaining an experienced criminal defense attorney to monitor court filings ensures you capitalize on missed prosecution deadlines and secure full dismissals.
