Linguistic Roots: Unpacking 'Praotes' (πραΰτης) and 'Praus' (πραΰς)

In ancient and biblical Koine Greek, the English word 'gentleness' (frequently translated in historic versions as 'meekness') derives from the Greek noun praotes (πραΰτης) and its root adjective praus (πραΰς). While modern English speakers often equate gentleness or meekness with passivity, timidity, or physical weakness, the original Greek concept represents the exact opposite: strength brought under perfect control.

In ancient Greek culture, praotes described a majestic quality of emotional balance, composure, and deliberate restraint. It is celebrated prominently in biblical literature as one of the nine Fruit of the Spirit in Galatians 5:23 and in the Beatitudes of Jesus ('Blessed are the meek [praus], for they shall inherit the earth'). To understand praotes is to discover a virtue of profound moral power governed by self-restraint.

Classical Metaphors: The Warhorse and Aristotle's Golden Mean

To grasp the vivid cultural imagery of praus, ancient Greek writers looked to equestrian training. The military historian Xenophon used the term praus to describe a wild, muscular warhorse that had been thoroughly trained and bridled. The stallion had lost none of its speed, ferocity, or explosive physical strength; rather, its immense power had been submitted completely to the whisper and gentle rein of the rider.

Philosophically, Aristotle articulated praotes in his Nicomachean Ethics as the ideal golden mean between two destructive extremes: the vice of uncontrollable rage (orgilotes) on one hand, and the vice of spineless apathy (aorgesia) on the other. A person possessing praotes knows how to be angry at the right time, for the right reason, against the right evil, while never losing mastery over their spirit.

Rediscovering praotes reclaims gentleness as an inspiring standard of disciplined strength, moral nobility, and purposeful peace.

How to Cultivate and Embody Greek 'Praotes' in Modern Life

Integrate the classical and biblical virtue of disciplined gentleness into daily personal relationships.

  1. Pause and Breathe Before Reacting in Anger

    Practice the Aristotelian pause: allow emotional adrenaline to subside so reason guides your response rather than blind temper.

  2. Remember the Warhorse Metaphor of Restrained Power

    Recognize that possessing authority, sharp intellect, or physical strength carries the moral duty to bridle it for constructive good.

  3. Listen with Teachable Humility During Disagreements

    Embody praotes by considering other perspectives calmly rather than bulldozing conversations with defensive pride.

  4. Advocate for Others Firmly Without Cruelty

    Defend the vulnerable with courageous conviction while refusing to adopt the hostile, demeaning tactics of adversaries.

  5. Yield Personal Pride to Foster Communal Harmony

    Practice epieikeia (gracious forbearance) by letting go of petty grievances that serve only to inflame household or workplace strife.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: What does the word gentleness mean in Greek?

In Greek, gentleness translates to 'praotes' (πραΰτης), which signifies strength under control, mildness, and disciplined restraint rather than weakness.

Q2: Is biblical gentleness the same as being weak?

No. In ancient Greek, a gentle or 'meek' person (praus) possessed tremendous strength and courage, but kept their passions bridled under complete self-control.

Q3: What is the warhorse metaphor for praotes?

Ancient Greeks used 'praus' to describe a powerful warhorse trained to obey the rider's rein—retaining all its strength while submitting to discipline.

Q4: How did Aristotle define gentleness (praotes)?

Aristotle defined praotes as the virtuous mean between flying into irrational rages and being completely indifferent to wrongdoing.

Q5: Where is praotes found in the Bible?

Praotes appears prominently in Galatians 5:23 as a Fruit of the Spirit, in Matthew 5:5 ('Blessed are the meek'), and in 1 Peter 3:15.

Q6: What is the difference between praotes and epieikeia?

Praotes refers to inner mildness and disciplined emotions, whereas epieikeia emphasizes outward fairness, forbearance, and gracious equity in dealings.

Q7: Why did early English translations use the word 'meekness'?

In seventeenth-century English, 'meek' meant calm, humble strength; over centuries, common English drifted to associate meekness with timidity.
