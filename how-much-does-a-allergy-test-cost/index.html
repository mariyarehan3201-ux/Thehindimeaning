How Much Does a Allergy Test Cost?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how much does a allergy test cost requires distinguishing between the testing methodology employed (skin prick test, intradermal test, or blood IgE antibody test) and whether you have health insurance. In the United States, an allergy skin prick testing panel costs between $200 and $1,000 without insurance, plus an initial allergist consultation fee of $150 to $350. However, for patients with commercial health insurance or Medicare, allergy testing is typically covered as an essential diagnostic medical service, leaving patients responsible for standard specialist copays ($30 to $75) or deductible coinsurance (10% to 20%).

Testing Types and Price Breakdown: Skin Prick vs. Blood RAST/IgE Tests

In clinical immunology, allergists employ two primary testing methodologies: in vivo percutaneous skin tests and in vitro blood serum tests. The gold standard for rapid, accurate allergy diagnosis is the skin prick test (also called a scratch test). A medical technician applies tiny drops of concentrated allergen extracts (environmental pollens, dust mites, pet dander, mold spores, or food proteins) to your forearm or back and gently scratches the epidermal surface with a sterile plastic lancet.

Skin prick panels are priced based on the number of individual allergens tested, typically ranging from $5 to $15 per individual allergen prick. A standard 40-to-60-allergen environmental or food panel costs between $300 and $600 for the test itself. Blood allergy testing (specific IgE antibody test, formerly known as RAST) measures circulating immunoglobulin E antibodies in a blood sample. Blood panels cost between $200 and $800 out-of-pocket and are preferred for patients with severe eczema, active hives, or those who cannot safely discontinue antihistamine medications.

Billing Components: Initial Consultation, Testing, and Follow-Up

When budgeting for allergy testing, patients should recognize that medical invoices comprise several separate billing components. You cannot walk into an allergist's office and immediately receive skin prick testing; medical ethics and insurance billing rules mandate an initial comprehensive medical consultation (CPT codes 99203 or 99204).

During this 45-minute consultation ($150 to $350 cash value), the board-certified allergist evaluates your clinical symptom history, family genetics, seasonal triggers, and physical respiratory airways. Testing is performed subsequently (CPT code 95004 billed per scratch). Following the test, the physician interprets the wheal-and-flare reactions, formulates an avoidance plan, and discusses immunotherapy treatment options (allergy shots or sublingual drops), which incurs a follow-up consultation fee ($100 to $200).

Insurance Coverage and Allergen Immunotherapy Costs

The vast majority of commercial health insurance plans (Blue Cross, Aetna, Cigna, UnitedHealthcare) and Medicare cover allergy testing when medically necessary to diagnose allergic rhinitis, asthma, or anaphylaxis. However, patients on high-deductible health plans (HDHPs) must pay the negotiated contracted rate out-of-pocket until their annual deductible is satisfied (typically $300 to $600 for the full appointment).

Once allergies are confirmed, patients often explore allergen immunotherapy (allergy shots or SLIT sublingual drops) for long-term desensitization. Allergy shots require weekly injections during a 6-month 'buildup phase' followed by monthly maintenance shots for 3 to 5 years, costing $1,500 to $3,500 annually before insurance coverage.

How to Prepare for an Allergy Skin Prick Test

Follow these five preparation steps to ensure accurate test results and prevent false negatives.

  1. Stop All Antihistamines 5 to 7 Days Before the Test

    Discontinue oral antihistamines (Zyrtec, Claritin, Allegra, Benadryl) as they block histamine receptors and produce false negative results.

  2. Stop Over-the-Counter Sleep Aids and Cold Meds

    Check labels on nighttime cold medicines (like NyQuil); many contain hidden diphenhydramine or doxylamine antihistamines.

  3. Continue Taking Your Prescribed Asthma Inhalers

    Do not stop daily maintenance asthma inhalers (like Flovent or Advair) or emergency albuterol without consulting your doctor.

  4. Wear a Short-Sleeved Shirt for Easy Arm Access

    Wear comfortable clothing that allows the medical staff to access your forearms or upper back easily.

  5. Allow 1.5 to 2 Hours for Your Full Appointment

    Plan for a 45-minute consultation, 20 minutes for skin reactions to develop, and 30 minutes for doctor interpretation.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How much does an allergy test cost without insurance?

Without insurance, an allergy skin prick test typically costs between $300 and $800, plus a $150 to $350 initial doctor consultation fee.

Q2: Does health insurance cover allergy testing?

Yes, health insurance almost always covers diagnostic allergy testing, leaving patients with standard specialist copays or deductible coinsurance.

Q3: Does a skin prick allergy test hurt?

It does not feel like a shot; the plastic lancet barely scratches the skin surface, feeling like a mild prick followed by localized mosquito-bite itching.

Q4: How long does an allergy test take to show results?

Skin prick results are visible in just 15 to 20 minutes; blood tests require 2 to 5 business days for laboratory processing.

Q5: Why do you have to stop antihistamines before testing?

Antihistamines chemically block your skin's histamine reaction, causing false negative results even if you are severely allergic.

Q6: How many allergies can be tested at once?

A standard comprehensive environmental panel tests 40 to 60 common tree, grass, weed, mold, dust, and animal allergens simultaneously.

Q7: Can you do an at-home allergy test?

At-home finger-prick blood kits cost $100 to $250, but allergists consider in-office skin prick testing significantly more accurate.

Q8: What is an oral food challenge?

It is a medical test where a patient eats tiny, increasing doses of a suspected food allergen under continuous medical supervision to rule out allergies.
