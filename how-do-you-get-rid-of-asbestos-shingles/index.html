How Do You Get Rid of Asbestos Shingles?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 24, 2026

How do you get rid of asbestos shingles? Disposing of vintage asbestos-cement exterior shingles requires choosing between professional abatement or lawful encapsulation. Because these transite panels contain fifteen to twenty-five percent chrysotile fibers locked in dense Portland cement, safe management requires preventing panel breakage, applying continuous wet-mist suppression, utilizing HEPA-filtered equipment, and transporting waste under hazardous manifests to designated monofill landfills.

Strategic Options: Total Removal vs. Encapsulation

Property owners dealing with aged exterior asbestos-cement shingles generally face two primary paths: complete physical removal or structural encapsulation. Complete abatement permanently eliminates the hazardous material from the property, removing future disclosure liabilities during real estate sales and eliminating safety concerns during subsequent structural alterations. However, total removal requires substantial financial outlay, careful staging, and hazardous waste logistics.

Encapsulation, on the other hand, involves leaving intact shingles securely fastened to the exterior sheathing and installing rigid insulation foam board directly over them, followed by new modern vinyl, fiber cement, or engineered wood siding. Because undisturbed transite shingles are classified as non-friable Category II materials, encapsulation maintains structural integrity while completely sealing the fibers behind a durable protective barrier, often saving homeowners between fifty and seventy percent compared to full removal costs.

Safe Handling Protocols and Regulatory Compliance

When complete removal is chosen, strict engineering protocols must govern every stage of the teardown. Under federal NESHAP (National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants) guidelines and municipal environmental codes, workers must never use power tools, high-pressure washers, circular saws, or abrasive sanding discs on asbestos shingles. Any high-energy mechanical agitation pulverizes the cementitious binder, converting non-friable siding into lethal airborne friable dust.

Instead, workers utilize manual pry bars to extract the mounting fasteners directly or shear nail heads using specialized siding removal hooks. Ground surfaces below the work area must be lined with two layers of six-mil polyethylene sheeting extending at least ten feet from the foundation walls to catch any chips or stray debris. All removed panels must be lowered to the ground manually or via mechanical hoist buckets rather than dropped into open dumpsters.

Careful disposal manifest tracking is essential to avoid hefty civil penalties from environmental regulators. Hazardous waste disposal facilities require signed manifests verifying that waste originated from a certified project and was transported by an authorized carrier.

When encapsulation is chosen instead, contractors must affix clear warning labels to the building's sheathing. This prevents future tradespeople, such as electricians or window installers, from unknowingly drilling through covered transite panels during subsequent remodeling projects.

How to Safely Remove Exterior Asbestos Shingles

Step-by-step procedure for removing transite cement shingles while preventing fiber release.

  1. File Environmental Notifications and Permits

    Submit required notification forms to regional clean air agencies and municipal building departments at least ten business days in advance.

  2. Lay Ground Polyethylene Containment

    Spread two layers of six-mil polyethylene sheeting across the ground extending ten feet from building walls to catch any loose chips.

  3. Wet-Mist Shingles with Surfactant Spray

    Saturate the shingle courses thoroughly with water mixed with surfactant using a pump sprayer to suppress airborne dust during removal.

  4. Pry Fasteners Without Breaking Panels

    Use a flat shingle ripper tool to gently lever or cut nail heads beneath each panel, carefully lifting intact shingles into lined containers.

  5. Package in Labeled Six-Mil Hazmat Bags

    Stack intact panels into certified six-mil asbestos disposal bags, tape with gooseneck seals, and manifest for transport to an authorized landfill.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I legally remove asbestos shingles myself?

Federal law allows single-family homeowners to remove siding in many regions, but state and municipal codes frequently mandate licensed abatement contractors.

Q2: What respirator is required for removing asbestos shingles?

Workers must wear a NIOSH-approved half-mask or full-face tight-fitting respirator equipped with P100 particulate (HEPA) filter cartridges.

Q3: Can you install new vinyl siding over asbestos shingles?

Yes, installing rigid foam board over intact shingles followed by new siding is an approved encapsulation method that avoids costly abatement.

Q4: How do you dispose of removed asbestos siding shingles?

Shingles must be double-bagged in six-mil poly bags with OSHA warning labels or shrink-wrapped on pallets and transported to an authorized landfill.

Q5: Can you power wash asbestos siding before painting?

No, power washing asbestos shingles is strictly prohibited because high-pressure water pulverizes the cement and atomizes millions of hazardous fibers.

Q6: How much does it cost to remove asbestos siding shingles?

Professional removal typically costs between $7 and $15 per square foot, averaging $8,000 to $18,000 for a complete single-family residence.

Q7: Do asbestos shingles pose health risks if intact?

No, intact and undamaged shingles pose virtually no health risk because the fibers are tightly bound inside a dense Portland cement matrix.

Q8: What is a shingle ripper tool?

A shingle ripper is a thin, hooked steel pry tool designed to slide beneath shingles to pull or shear mounting nails without shattering the brittle panel.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Deciding how to get rid of asbestos shingles depends on budget, exterior condition, and long-term property renovation plans. While encapsulation provides an economical and safe short-term barrier, professional abatement permanently frees properties from hazardous materials under strict environmental compliance.
