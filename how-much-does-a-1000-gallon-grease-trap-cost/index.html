How Much Does a 1000 Gallon Grease Trap Cost?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how much does a 1000 gallon grease trap cost requires distinguishing between the equipment purchase price and the extensive civil engineering, excavation, and plumbing expenses required for underground installation. In commercial restaurant construction, purchasing a precast concrete or heavy-duty polyethylene 1,000-gallon gravity grease interceptor costs between $3,500 and $7,500 for the tank unit alone. However, full turnkey underground installation—including engineering plans, municipal permitting, excavation, piping tie-ins, traffic-rated cast iron risers, and asphalt paving—typically ranges from $12,000 to $25,000.

Material Costs: Precast Concrete vs. Polyethylene (Fiberglass)

In commercial kitchen wastewater design, a 1,000-gallon exterior gravity grease interceptor (GGI) is mandatory for medium-to-large restaurants, bakeries, school cafeterias, and hotel banquet kitchens to prevent fats, oils, and grease (FOG) from clogging municipal sewer mains. When specifying a 1,000-gallon unit, engineers choose between precast reinforced concrete and modern rotationally molded polyethylene.

Traditional precast concrete grease traps are the most economical equipment choice upfront, costing between $3,500 and $5,500. Concrete tanks are exceptionally heavy (weighing 10,000 to 14,000 pounds), which eliminates the risk of tank buoyancy in high groundwater tables. However, raw concrete is vulnerable to microbial-induced sulfuric acid corrosion from decomposing food wastes, typically lasting 15 to 25 years. In contrast, advanced polyethylene or fiberglass tanks (such as Schier or Striem units) cost between $6,000 and $9,000, but are completely impervious to acid corrosion and feature lifetime structural warranties.

Site Work and Turnkey Installation Breakdown

The equipment purchase price represents less than 40% of the total project expenditure. Installing a 1,000-gallon grease interceptor requires heavy civil site contracting. The installation process begins with an excavator digging a 10x12-foot pit that is 8 to 10 feet deep in the restaurant's parking lot or rear alleyway, costing $3,000 to $6,000 for earthmoving, soil hauling, and crushed stone bedding.

A commercial crane or boom truck hoists the multi-ton tank into the excavation ($1,000 to $2,000). Commercial utility plumbers run 4-inch SDR-35 or cast iron gravity drain pipes connecting the commercial kitchen's three-compartment sink, floor drains, and tilt kettles through the trap before tying into the city sewer main, which costs $4,000 to $8,000. Finally, H-20 traffic-rated cast iron manhole risers are installed flush with grade, followed by backfilling, compaction, and fresh asphalt or concrete repaving ($2,500 to $5,000).

Ongoing Maintenance Costs: Pumping and Regulatory Compliance

Beyond installation expenses, restaurant owners must budget for continuous operational maintenance. Municipal wastewater authorities enforce the '25 Percent Rule': when the combined volume of floating grease and settled bottom solids exceeds 25% of the trap's total liquid capacity (250 gallons in a 1,000-gallon tank), the interceptor must be pumped out completely.

Pumping out a 1,000-gallon grease trap requires a commercial vacuum pump truck and typically costs between $350 and $650 per service call, including municipal wastewater disposal fees. Depending on your kitchen's grease output, municipal health codes mandate pumping schedules every 30, 60, or 90 days, resulting in annual ongoing maintenance expenses of $1,500 to $4,000.

How to Plan and Execute a Commercial Grease Trap Installation

Follow these five strategic milestones to navigate engineering, municipal permitting, and installation.

  1. Calculate Required Interceptor Sizing via UPC / IPC Plumbing Codes

    Work with a MEP engineer to calculate sizing based on kitchen fixture counts, drain GPM, and a 30-minute retention time.

  2. Secure Municipal Health and Sewer Department Permits

    Submit civil site utility plans and structural engineering drawings to city building authorities for plan approval.

  3. Verify H-20 Traffic Rating for Parking Lot Placement

    Ensure the tank, risers, and manhole lids are H-20 certified to withstand heavy delivery truck drive-over loads.

  4. Schedule Underground Excavation and Plumbing Inspections

    Ensure city plumbing inspectors witness the 10-foot water static head pressure test before backfilling trenches.

  5. Establish a Preventative Pumping Contract with a Licensed Hauler

    Set up scheduled 60-to-90-day pump-out contracts to comply with the municipal 25% FOG rule and prevent sewer fines.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How much does a 1,000-gallon grease trap cost installed?

Turnkey installation typically costs between $12,000 and $25,000, including tank, excavation, plumbing, and paving.

Q2: How much does just the 1,000-gallon tank cost?

The tank alone costs between $3,500 and $5,500 for precast concrete, and $6,000 to $9,000 for polyethylene.

Q3: Why is a 1,000-gallon grease trap required for restaurants?

Municipal codes require exterior 750 to 1,500-gallon gravity interceptors to prevent commercial grease from congealing in city sewers.

Q4: How often does a 1,000-gallon grease trap need to be pumped?

Most commercial restaurants must pump their 1,000-gallon trap every 1 to 3 months to stay below the 25% FOG threshold.

Q5: How much does it cost to pump out a 1,000-gallon grease trap?

Professional vacuum pump truck service typically costs between $350 and $650 per pumping.

Q6: What is the difference between an indoor grease trap and a 1,000-gallon interceptor?

Indoor hydromechanical traps hold 20-50 gallons under sinks (light use), while 1,000-gallon gravity traps sit buried outside (heavy use).

Q7: Can a grease trap be installed inside a basement?

Generally no; a 1,000-gallon unit is massive and must be accessible outdoors for high-volume pumper vacuum trucks.

Q8: What happens if a restaurant doesn't clean its grease trap?

Fats overflow into city sewers, causing massive municipal fines ($1,000+/day), raw sewage backups, and emergency restaurant closure.
