Defining OAC: The Owner-Architect-Contractor Triad

In commercial construction management, real estate development, and project engineering, OAC is an essential project administration acronym that stands for Owner, Architect, Contractor (frequently referred to as the weekly OAC Meeting).

The OAC triad represents the three primary legal and operational stakeholders required to design and build any commercial structure: the Owner (or Owner Representative/Developer who funds the project), the Architect (and engineering design consultants who drew the blueprints), and the General Contractor (who oversees field construction trades). An OAC meeting is a mandatory, recurring weekly management meeting where these three leadership pillars collaborate to review job progress, resolve design conflicts, and approve financial change orders.

Review the distinct responsibilities of the three OAC project stakeholders:

OAC Stakeholder Primary Legal & Contractual Role Core Responsibilities in OAC Meeting Key Decision Authority
The Owner (or Owner Rep) Project financier and property developer Monitors budget, reviews schedule milestones, approves scope changes Final executive approval on all financial change orders and design additions
The Architect (A/E Design Team) Lead architectural designer and engineer of record Answers Requests for Information (RFIs), reviews submittals, clarifies blueprints Ensures building design aesthetic and structural life-safety codes are upheld
The General Contractor (GC) Prime builder managing on-site construction execution Presents weekly schedules, reports trade progress, submits cost changes Manages job site safety, subcontractor trades, material deliveries, and physical build

What Gets Solved in a Weekly OAC Meeting: RFIs, Submittals, and Budgets

The weekly OAC meeting is the engine that drives a commercial construction project forward. Without regular OAC meetings, field construction grinds to a halt whenever job site conditions conflict with architectural blueprints.

During an OAC meeting, the General Contractor Project Manager reviews the critical path schedule, presenting three-week look-ahead schedules to show upcoming concrete pours, structural steel erection, and drywall framing. The team tackles pending Requests for Information (RFIs)—formal questions where field workers discovered plumbing pipes colliding with structural beams. The architect provides immediate design clarifications, while the owner reviews Proposed Change Orders (PCOs) to assess cost impacts. Thoroughly documenting OAC meeting minutes creates a legally binding paper trail that protects all three parties in the event of future construction defect or delay litigation.

Compare key administrative documents reviewed and managed during OAC meetings:

Submittal Package Submittal Shop drawings, product data, and material samples for approval Architect verifies windows, steel, and tiles match design specs
Proposed Change Order PCO / COR Detailed cost and time estimate for out-of-scope work Owner approves or negotiates budget adjustments before work begins
Three-Week Look-Ahead Look-Ahead Detailed day-by-day schedule of upcoming subcontractor work Coordinates trade handoffs (e.g., framing before rough plumbing)
OAC Meeting Minutes Minutes Official written summary of all discussions and commitments made Legal documentation preventing disputes over who agreed to what

A well-structured OAC meeting eliminates adversarial posturing and aligns the team around delivering a safe, high-quality, on-budget building.

How General Contractors Run a High-Impact Weekly OAC Meeting

Ensure your construction project stays aligned using this standardized OAC meeting structure.

  1. Establish a Consistent Weekly Meeting Rhythm

    Schedule OAC meetings on the identical day and time every week (e.g., Tuesdays at 10:00 AM) in the job site trailer or via video conference.

  2. Distribute the Formal Agenda 24 Hours in Advance

    Circulate an agenda covering: Safety, Master Schedule, RFI Log, Submittal Log, Financial Change Orders, and Field Issues.

  3. Begin with a Job Site Safety and Quality Review

    Start every meeting reviewing on-site safety statistics, municipal building inspections passed, and quality control audits.

  4. Review Open RFIs and Submittal Bottlenecks

    Focus on critical-path RFIs; secure verbal architect commitments on pending engineering design submittals impacting next week trades.

  5. Publish and Distribute Meeting Minutes Within 24 Hours

    The GC project engineer must email finalized OAC meeting minutes with action items and responsible party deadlines within one business day.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What does OAC mean in construction?

OAC stands for Owner, Architect, Contractor, referring to the core three-party project leadership team and their weekly progress meetings.

Q2: Who attends an OAC meeting?

Attendees include the property owner (or owner rep), lead architects and engineers, the general contractor project manager and superintendent, and major consultants.

Q3: Why are OAC meetings important?

They ensure the project finishes on time and within budget by resolving blueprint design questions (RFIs), approving change orders, and coordinating schedules.

Q4: What is an RFI in an OAC meeting?

An RFI (Request for Information) is a formal document where the builder asks the architect to clarify an ambiguous or conflicting blueprint detail.

Q5: Who writes the OAC meeting minutes?

The General Contractor project manager or project engineer typically writes and distributes the official meeting minutes to all stakeholders.

Q6: What is an Owner Rep in construction?

An Owner Representative is an experienced construction consultant hired by the property owner to oversee the architect and builder and protect owner financial interests.

Q7: What is a PCO in an OAC meeting?

A PCO (Proposed Change Order) is a contractor estimate for additional work outside the original contract scope, submitted to the owner for approval.

Q8: How long do OAC meetings usually last?

Standard commercial OAC meetings typically last between 60 and 90 minutes, depending on the project complexity and stage of construction.
