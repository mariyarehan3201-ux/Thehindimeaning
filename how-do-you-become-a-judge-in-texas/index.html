How Do You Become a Judge in Texas?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how do you become a judge in texas requires navigating the Lone Star State's distinctive, constitutionally mandated judicial selection framework. Unlike the federal judiciary where judges receive lifetime presidential appointments, Texas is one of the few states that selects nearly all of its judges through partisan political elections. Becoming a Texas judge demands meeting strict statutory legal qualifications, building deep litigation experience as a licensed Texas attorney, and mounting successful political campaigns across county primaries and general elections.

Texas Court Structure and Constitutional Qualifications

The Texas judicial system is one of the largest and most intricate in the United States, structured into two supreme appellate courts (the Supreme Court of Texas for civil matters and the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals for criminal matters), fourteen regional intermediate Courts of Appeals, hundreds of state District Courts, and numerous county and municipal trial courts.

Constitutional eligibility requirements vary substantially across different court tiers. Under Article V of the Texas Constitution and the Texas Government Code, judges presiding over higher courts must possess substantial legal credentials. However, uniquely in Texas, constitutional county judges (who head county government commissioners courts) and Justices of the Peace (JPs) presiding over small claims and traffic courts are not constitutionally required to be licensed attorneys, though they must complete mandatory judicial education.

Recent Legislative Reforms: House Bill 2384 Qualification Changes

In recent years, the Texas Legislature enacted landmark judicial reform legislation. House Bill 2384, enacted to raise standards for the Texas judiciary, significantly increased experience thresholds for district and appellate judges taking office on or after September 1, 2023. Under HB 2384, candidates for state District Judge must have practiced law in Texas for at least eight years (up from the previous four-year rule) and must be practicing trial lawyers or judges in good standing.

For Texas Courts of Appeals and high appellate courts (Texas Supreme Court and Texas Court of Criminal Appeals), candidates must now possess at least ten to twelve years of legal practice experience, with at least eight of those years practicing in Texas courts. Furthermore, candidates must have zero public disciplinary sanctions or disbarments from the State Bar of Texas during the ten years preceding their election or appointment.

The Campaign Process: Partisan Primaries, Fundraising, and Gubernatorial Appointments

Because Texas utilizes partisan judicial elections, aspiring judges must formally affiliate with a recognized political party (Republican or Democratic). The judicial election campaign begins with filing formal candidate petitions or paying filing fees ($2,500 to $3,750) with the state or county party executive committee before the December filing deadline. Candidates must wage robust primary campaigns in March, speaking at political clubs, securing endorsements from local bar associations and law enforcement unions, and running voter education drives.

The alternative pathway to the Texas bench is through Gubernatorial Appointment. When a sitting judge resigns, retires, or passes away mid-term, or when the Texas Legislature creates a new specialty district court, the Governor of Texas holds constitutional authority to appoint an interim judge to fill the vacancy until the next general election. Gubernatorial appointees must undergo rigorous background vetting through the Governor's Appointments Office and receive formal confirmation by a two-thirds vote of the Texas Senate.

How to Become a Judge in Texas

Follow these five strategic steps to gain legal qualifications, build judicial credentials, and campaign for the Texas bench.

  1. Earn a Juris Doctor and Pass the Texas Bar Exam

    Graduate from an ABA-accredited law school and earn admission to the State Bar of Texas as an attorney in good standing.

  2. Build Substantial Trial Litigation Experience

    Accumulate 8 to 12 years of trial or appellate courtroom experience to satisfy Texas HB 2384 statutory eligibility rules.

  3. Participate Actively in Local Bar and Civic Groups

    Join local bar association committees, publish legal articles, and cultivate a reputation for integrity and fairness.

  4. File for Party Primary Candidacy

    Submit candidate application petitions and filing fees with the political party of your choice during open winter filing periods.

  5. Wage a Partisan Campaign or Apply for Appointment

    Campaign across the judicial district to win the general election, or petition the Governor's Office for an interim vacancy appointment.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Are judges in Texas appointed or elected?

Nearly all Texas judges are chosen through partisan political elections, though the Governor appoints judges to fill mid-term vacancies.

Q2: Do you have to be a lawyer to be a judge in Texas?

District, statutory county, and appellate judges must be licensed lawyers; Justices of the Peace and constitutional county judges are not required to hold a law degree.

Q3: What are the new qualifications under Texas HB 2384?

Enacted in 2023, HB 2384 requires district judges to have 8+ years of legal experience (up from 4) and appellate judges to have 10-12 years with clean disciplinary records.

Q4: How long is a judge's term in Texas?

District, county, and Justice of the Peace judges serve 4-year terms; appellate and supreme court justices serve 6-year terms.

Q5: What is the mandatory retirement age for Texas judges?

Under the Texas Constitution, judges must retire at age 75 (or complete their term if they turn 75 during the first four years of a six-year term).

Q6: Can a Texas judge switch political parties while on the bench?

Yes, sitting judges can legally switch political party affiliations, though they must run under their new party banner in the subsequent primary election.

Q7: How much do Texas district judges make?

Texas district judges receive a state base salary of $153,000, which can reach over $180,000 with local county salary supplements.

Q8: Who investigates judicial misconduct in Texas?

The State Commission on Judicial Conduct (SCJC) is the independent state agency that investigates complaints against Texas judges.
