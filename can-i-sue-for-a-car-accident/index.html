Can I Sue for a Car Accident?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, you can sue for a car accident if another party's negligence caused the collision and you suffered physical injuries, financial losses, or vehicle property damage. However, your legal right to file a lawsuit depends on whether your accident occurred in a traditional tort state or a no-fault state with strict verbal injury thresholds.

The Legal Right to Sue: Tort States vs. No-Fault States

Experiencing an automobile collision is an overwhelming event that brings physical pain, emotional trauma, and unexpected financial disruption. When insurance adjusters fail to offer adequate compensation, accident victims universally ask: "Can I sue for a car accident?" In the American civil justice system, the answer is an affirmative yes?"provided you satisfy specific statutory prerequisites governing personal injury law in your state.

The primary legal determinant of your right to sue is whether the accident occurred in an At-Fault (Tort) State or a No-Fault State. In traditional tort states (which comprise approximately 38 US states, including Texas, California, and Illinois), you possess the immediate legal right to file a lawsuit against the at-fault driver for all damages, including past and future medical expenses, lost wages, and non-economic pain and suffering, without having to meet arbitrary injury thresholds.

Conversely, in the 12 No-Fault states (such as New York, Florida, Michigan, and New Jersey), the law restricts the right to sue for non-economic damages. In no-fault jurisdictions, each motorist must first turn to their own mandatory Personal Injury Protection (PIP) insurance to cover initial medical bills and wage losses. To sue the at-fault driver for pain and suffering, your injuries must cross a statutory "Serious Injury Threshold" (often called a verbal threshold)?"such as permanent disfigurement, bone fractures, permanent loss of a bodily function, or death. The Four Legal Elements of Car Accident Negligence

To successfully sue another motorist and obtain a financial judgment in civil court, your attorney must prove four distinct legal elements of negligence by a preponderance of the evidence (meaning it is more likely than not that your claim is true). If any one of these four pillars fails, your lawsuit will be dismissed.

1. Duty of Care: The plaintiff must establish that the defendant owed them a legal duty of care. Every licensed motorist on public roadways owes a statutory legal duty to all other drivers, pedestrians, and passengers to operate their vehicle with reasonable care, obey traffic signals, and maintain a vigilant lookout.

2. Breach of Duty: The plaintiff must prove that the defendant breached that duty of care by acting carelessly or recklessly. Common breaches include speeding, running a red light, texting while driving, following too closely, or driving while intoxicated.

3. Proximate Causation: The breach of duty must be the direct, proximate cause of the collision and resulting injuries. The defense will lose if the evidence shows that the accident would not have occurred "but for" the defendant careless actions.

4. Legally Cognizable Damages: The plaintiff must prove that they suffered measurable financial, physical, or emotional harm as a direct consequence of the collision. Without actual damages (such as medical invoices, lost paychecks, or physical pain), you cannot maintain a lawsuit even if the other driver ran a stop sign. Comparative Fault Rules and Financial Recovery Limits

A major consideration before filing a car accident lawsuit is evaluating whether you contributed in any way to the collision. State laws handle shared fault through three distinct comparative fault legal doctrines: Pure Comparative Fault, Modified Comparative Fault, and Pure Contributory Negligence.

Under Pure Comparative Fault (e.g., California, Florida), you can recover damages even if you were 90 percent at fault, but your recovery is reduced by your fault percentage. Under Modified Comparative Fault (the majority of states, including Texas and Ohio), you can recover damages only if you were 50 percent or less at fault (or 51 percent in some states); if you cross that threshold, you recover zero. In the five strict Contributory Negligence jurisdictions (including North Carolina, Virginia, and Maryland), if you contributed even one percent to the crash, you are legally barred from recovering a single penny. Retaining skilled legal counsel ensures that defense adjusters cannot falsely inflate your fault percentage.

How to File a Successful Car Accident Lawsuit

A step-by-step practical guide for accident victims to evaluate their legal claim, retain trial counsel, and file a civil lawsuit.

  1. Verify the Statute of Limitations Deadline in Your State

    Check your state personal injury statute of limitations (typically 1 to 4 years from the accident date). Confirm that your claim is well within the mandatory filing window.

  2. Obtain Complete Medical Records and Billing Invoices

    Gather certified copies of all emergency room records, diagnostic MRI scans, surgical reports, physical therapy notes, and pharmacy receipts proving your injuries.

  3. Retain a Contingency-Fee Personal Injury Trial Attorney

    Interview personal injury attorneys with proven courtroom trial experience. Sign a standard contingency fee agreement ensuring zero upfront legal costs.

  4. Draft and File the Formal Summons and Complaint

    Your attorney will draft a formal civil Complaint outlining the four elements of negligence and itemizing your damages, filing it with the appropriate county court clerk.

  5. Execute Formal Service of Process on All At-Fault Drivers

    Hire a licensed process server to personally serve the summons and complaint upon all at-fault drivers and corporate employers to initiate formal discovery.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sue for a car accident if I was not hurt?

You can sue for vehicle property damage, towing bills, and rental car costs. However, you cannot sue for pain and suffering or personal injury if you suffered zero physical injuries.

Q2: How long after a car accident can you sue?

The deadline is governed by your state's statute of limitations, which typically ranges from 1 to 4 years from the crash date (with 2 years being the most common standard across states).

Q3: What is the difference between an insurance claim and a lawsuit?

An insurance claim is an informal out-of-court negotiation with an insurance adjuster. A lawsuit is a formal legal action filed in a court of law governed by judicial procedure and judges.

Q4: Can I sue for a car accident in a no-fault state?

Yes, but only if your physical injuries meet the state's 'serious injury threshold' (such as bone fractures, permanent impairment, or disfigurement) or if medical bills exceed statutory caps.

Q5: How much money can you sue for after a car accident?

There is no fixed limit. You can sue for all economic damages (past and future medical bills, surgeries, lost wages) and non-economic damages (physical pain, emotional trauma, disability).

Q6: Can I sue if I was partially at fault for the crash?

In comparative negligence states, yes. In modified comparative fault states, you can sue as long as you were 50% or less at fault, though your financial award is reduced by your fault percentage.

Q7: Will I have to go to court if I sue after a car accident?

Rarely. Over 90 to 95 percent of filed car accident lawsuits reach a negotiated settlement during pre-trial discovery or court-mandated mediation before ever reaching a jury trial.

Q8: How much does it cost to hire a lawyer to sue for a car accident?

Personal injury lawyers work on a contingency fee basis (typically 33.3% pre-trial), meaning you pay zero upfront costs and pay nothing unless your attorney wins a financial recovery.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

You have an absolute constitutional right to sue for a car accident if another driver's carelessness disrupted your life, inflicted physical pain, or caused financial harm. While the overwhelming majority of car accident claims settle out of court during pre-trial negotiations, filing a formal lawsuit provides the essential legal leverage required to force insurance companies to offer full, fair compensation. Consulting with an experienced personal injury attorney allows you to understand your state specific laws, navigate comparative fault rules, and secure the financial recovery you deserve.
