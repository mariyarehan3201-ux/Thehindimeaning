Can Someone Sue You Personally After a Car Accident?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Yes, someone can sue you personally after a motor vehicle collision if they sustain bodily injuries or property damage caused by your negligence. Under civil tort law, you are individually responsible for the physical and financial harm resulting from careless conduct behind the wheel. While mandatory automobile liability policies provide the primary defense and payout mechanism up to statutory policy maximums, an injured claimant retains the legal right to name you as a personal defendant and pursue your private savings, real estate equity, and personal assets if their damages surpass policy limits.

The Anatomy of Personal Civil Lawsuits in Motor Vehicle Collisions

When an automobile accident occurs, drivers often assume their automobile insurance policy creates an impenetrable legal barrier that insulates them from direct personal contact with the judicial system. In reality, automobile liability policies do not prevent lawsuits from being filed against you directly; rather, they obligate the insurance carrier to defend you and indemnify your covered financial losses up to the specific dollar maximums outlined in your policy contract. Under civil litigation procedure, when an injured claimant retains legal counsel, the actual defendant named on the court summons and complaint is you, the individual driver, rather than your insurance company.

Every motor vehicle tort claim is grounded in the legal concept of negligence. To prevail in a civil lawsuit, an injured plaintiff must demonstrate that you owed a duty of reasonable care while operating a vehicle, that you breached that duty through an act such as speeding, running a red signal, or distracted driving, and that this breach directly caused demonstrable bodily injuries or property losses. In cases involving moderate damages, the vast majority of claims are resolved smoothly through settlement negotiations between the plaintiff's attorney and your insurer's claims adjuster. However, when evaluating how much someone can sue for a car accident, serious collisions involving catastrophic trauma can quickly outstrip minimum statutory insurance policies.

When catastrophic harm occurs—such as severe traumatic brain injuries, permanent spinal paralysis, amputations, or wrongful death—medical bills, life-care planning costs, and lost future income can easily reach hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars. If an at-fault motorist carries only state minimum bodily injury liability limits, such as twenty-five thousand dollars per person, the claimant's attorney will investigate the driver's private financial profile. If substantial personal wealth or unencumbered real estate is identified, the plaintiff will refuse to settle for policy limits alone, electing to take the case before a civil jury to secure a personal excess verdict against the driver.

Excess Verdicts, Stowers Doctrine, and Bad Faith Settlement Refusals

When an injured claimant files a personal lawsuit for damages that exceed your liability policy limits, a complex legal dynamic unfolds between you, your assigned defense attorney, and your insurance carrier. In standard insurance contracts, the insurer retains exclusive authority to negotiate, settle, or defend the claim. However, because the insurance company's direct financial liability is strictly capped at your policy limit, an insurer might be tempted to gamble at trial rather than settle, since their worst-case payout is limited to the policy maximum, while you bear all the risk of an enormous excess judgment.

To curb this conflict of interest, state common law established legal principles such as the Stowers Doctrine and broader insurance bad faith jurisprudence. Under these doctrines, if the injured plaintiff's attorney tenders a formal, time-limited settlement demand offering to release you from all personal liability in exchange for your policy limits, your insurer has a legal duty to accept the offer if an ordinarily prudent insurer would do so given the clear evidence of fault and the severity of injuries. If the insurance carrier unreasonably refuses to pay the policy limits and takes the case to trial, resulting in an excess verdict against you, the carrier may be held liable for the entire judgment—even the amount far above your policy limit—due to insurer bad faith.

In many real-world cases where an excess verdict is entered, the defendant driver enters into an agreement with the plaintiff. The driver assigns their bad faith legal claim against the insurance carrier to the plaintiff, and in exchange, the plaintiff signs a binding covenant not to execute the judgment against the driver's personal assets. However, if the insurance carrier handled the claim reasonably and the plaintiff simply refused all settlement offers to pursue your private wealth, the excess verdict stands as an enforceable personal judgment entered directly against your name in county records.

Once a civil court enters a money judgment against you, the plaintiff becomes a judgment creditor with powerful legal tools to locate and seize unexempt assets. Enforcing Judgments: Homestead Exemptions, Wage Garnishment, and Bankruptcy

If a plaintiff secures an excess money judgment against you, they cannot simply confiscate your life savings overnight. Post-judgment collection is governed by strict statutory rules that balance creditor recovery rights against fundamental debtor protections. Judgment creditors begin by recording a certified abstract of judgment in every county where you own real property, which immediately creates a cloud on title and prevents you from selling or refinancing real estate without satisfying the debt.

However, state homestead exemption laws provide substantial protection for your primary dwelling. In states such as Florida, Texas, Kansas, and Iowa, the state constitution grants an unlimited dollar homestead exemption for your primary residence on designated acreage. In those states, a judgment creditor cannot force the sale of your primary home regardless of the judgment's size. In other states, such as California, New York, or Illinois, homestead exemptions protect only a capped amount of equity, ranging from thirty thousand to several hundred thousand dollars, leaving surplus equity potentially exposed to judicial foreclosure.

For financial assets, the level of protection depends heavily on how funds are held. Qualified employer-sponsored retirement plans, such as 401(k)s, 403(b)s, and defined-benefit pensions, enjoy absolute federal protection under the Employee Retirement Income Security Act (ERISA) and cannot be seized by civil judgment creditors. Individual Retirement Accounts (IRAs) also enjoy broad protections under federal bankruptcy and state exemption statutes. In contrast, standard checking accounts, savings accounts, and stock trading portfolios have minimal protection and can be quickly frozen and drained through a writ of garnishment.

When it comes to paychecks, the federal Consumer Credit Protection Act caps continuous wage garnishment at twenty-five percent of your disposable earnings, or the amount by which weekly earnings exceed thirty times the federal minimum wage. Furthermore, several states—including Texas, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, and South Carolina—prohibit wage garnishment entirely for ordinary civil accident judgments. If an insurmountable excess judgment threatens financial ruin, filing for Chapter 7 or Chapter 13 bankruptcy provides a final safety net, allowing individuals to discharge civil accident judgments resulting from ordinary negligence. Understanding these realities underscores the importance of implementing proactive strategies to protect your assets after a car accident and monitoring the statute of limitations for auto lawsuits.

How to Respond and Protect Yourself If Sued Personally After a Car Accident

Follow these critical procedural and legal steps when served with a personal summons and complaint following an automobile collision.

  1. Notify Your Insurance Carrier Immediately Upon Being Served

    Contact your automobile insurance company the same day you receive court papers and transmit digital copies of the summons and complaint to initiate your defense.

  2. Confirm Assigned Defense Counsel and Duty to Defend

    Verify that your insurance carrier has assigned a licensed defense attorney to represent you at no out-of-pocket cost under their contractual duty to defend.

  3. Demand in Writing That Your Insurer Settle Within Policy Limits

    Instruct your assigned defense counsel to send a formal Stowers letter demanding that your insurer accept any reasonable settlement offer within your policy caps.

  4. Consult an Independent Personal Asset Protection Attorney

    If the claimed damages substantially exceed your policy limits, retain private counsel to advise you on state asset exemptions and evaluate potential insurer bad faith.

  5. Audit and Safeguard Your Exempt Personal Assets

    Review your home homestead status, maintain retirement savings within ERISA-qualified accounts, and avoid making fraudulent conveyances that could compromise asset protection.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can someone sue me for more than my car insurance policy limits?

Yes. An injured party has the legal right to sue you personally for the full extent of their medical expenses, lost wages, and pain and suffering. If a jury awards more than your policy limits, you are personally liable for the excess amount.

Q2: What happens if a jury verdict exceeds my automobile insurance coverage?

Your insurance carrier pays up to your policy limit, and the court enters an excess judgment against you for the remaining balance. The plaintiff can attempt to collect that balance from your non-exempt savings, investments, or personal assets.

Q3: Can a car accident judgment creditor take my house?

It depends on state homestead exemption laws. In states like Florida and Texas, primary residences have unlimited homestead protection and cannot be seized. In states with capped exemptions, surplus home equity could be vulnerable.

Q4: Are my retirement accounts safe from an auto accident lawsuit?

Yes. Qualified employer retirement plans such as 401(k)s and pensions are 100 percent shielded from civil lawsuit judgment creditors under federal ERISA law. Traditional and Roth IRAs also receive broad state and federal statutory exemptions.

Q5: Can my paycheck be garnished to pay for a car accident judgment?

Under federal law, wage garnishment is capped at 25 percent of disposable weekly earnings. In addition, several states including Texas, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, and South Carolina completely ban wage garnishment for ordinary civil judgments.

Q6: What is an insurer bad faith claim regarding an excess verdict?

If your insurer unreasonably rejects a settlement demand within your policy limits despite clear liability, and you get hit with an excess verdict, the insurer may be forced to pay the entire excess amount under state bad faith law.

Q7: Can an auto accident judgment be wiped out in personal bankruptcy?

Yes. Civil judgments resulting from ordinary car accident negligence can be discharged through Chapter 7 or Chapter 13 bankruptcy. However, judgments resulting from intoxication or willful malicious conduct cannot be discharged.

Q8: Will hiring a personal defense attorney help if the damages exceed my policy?

Yes. While your insurance company provides an attorney to defend the collision claim, hiring an independent personal counsel ensures your private assets are protected and pressures the insurer to settle within policy limits.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Facing the prospect of being sued personally following a car accident is undeniably stressful, but understanding the legal reality provides crucial perspective. In the vast majority of motor vehicle collisions, claims resolve peacefully within the limits of standard liability insurance policies through negotiated settlements. Even when severe damages create excess exposure, common law bad faith doctrines, state homestead exemptions, federal retirement protections, and wage garnishment caps provide powerful shields for your essential livelihood. By maintaining healthy bodily injury coverage limits, purchasing affordable personal umbrella insurance, and promptly demanding that your insurer settle within policy caps, you can confidently protect your personal wealth against unexpected roadway liabilities.
