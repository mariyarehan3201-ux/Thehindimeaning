How Do You Get Rid of Asbestos?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Discovering asbestos in your home, commercial facility, or renovation site inevitably raises an urgent question: how do you get rid of asbestos safely, legally, and permanently? Getting rid of asbestos is not simply a matter of stripping out materials with crowbars and tossing debris into curbside trash cans. Under federal EPA, OSHA, and state environmental laws, asbestos-containing materials are classified as hazardous air pollutants requiring specialized containment, negative-pressure air filtration, wet dismantling techniques, and certified landfill disposal. Whether dealing with acoustic popcorn ceilings, basement pipe insulation, vinyl floor tiles, or exterior siding, mastering the approved pathways of abatement and encapsulation ensures the hazard is eliminated without poisoning your indoor air.

The Decision: Professional Abatement vs. In-Place Encapsulation

When evaluating how to address asbestos, property owners must understand that getting rid of asbestos does not always necessitate physical removal. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) explicitly advises that intact, undamaged asbestos materials in sound condition are often best managed in place. Physical removal carries an inherent risk of disturbing dormant fibers and dispersing them into indoor air. Encapsulation—sealing the material with a durable elastomeric coating—or enclosure—building an airtight physical barrier like drywall or subflooring over the material—is frequently safer, faster, and thousands of dollars less expensive.

However, complete physical abatement becomes mandatory under specific structural conditions. If asbestos materials are severely damaged, water-rotted, crumbling, or located in an active renovation zone scheduled for structural demolition or utility retrofitting, complete removal is unavoidable. When full removal is required, hiring a state-licensed, certified asbestos abatement contractor ensures the work complies with federal National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP) regulations and local clean air mandates.

The Abatement Process: Engineering Controls and Containment

When licensed contractors execute complete asbestos removal, they construct an elaborate environmental containment envelope designed to isolate the hazard completely. Technicians seal all HVAC vents, windows, doors, and electrical fixtures using critical barriers of two layers of six-mil polyethylene sheeting secured with industrial duct tape. The team establishes a multi-stage decontamination unit—comprising clean, shower, and dirty airlock chambers—to prevent workers from tracking fibers outside the containment envelope.

To ensure fibers cannot escape into adjacent rooms, contractors deploy industrial negative-pressure air machines equipped with certified HEPA filtration. These air scrubbers run continuously, drawing air inward through the containment zone and exhausting filtered air outdoors. Throughout the demolition phase, workers wear NIOSH-approved respirators (such as powered air-purifying respirators, or PAPRs) and disposable Tyvek coveralls, misting materials continuously with amended water containing surfactant to saturate dust at the exact moment of detachment.

Proper Packaging, Transportation, and Certified Landfill Disposal

Getting rid of asbestos safely requires strict compliance with hazardous waste transport and disposal regulations. Asbestos waste cannot be discarded in standard municipal roll-off dumpsters, curbside trash bins, or standard construction landfills. Extracted materials must be placed while wet directly into pre-labeled, leak-tight six-mil polyethylene disposal bags or lined fiber drums. Each bag is goose-necked, twisted, and taped with duct tape, and wiped clean with a damp cloth inside the decontamination unit before being loaded into a secondary sealed disposal bag.

Waste packages must bear explicit OSHA and EPA warning placards declaring carcinogenic danger and identifying the generator's name and project site. The contractor must retain an EPA-certified hazardous waste hauler who transports the material under an official Uniform Hazardous Waste Manifest. The waste is delivered exclusively to a permitted solid waste landfill licensed to receive friable or non-friable asbestos, where heavy equipment immediately covers the waste with clean soil to prevent airborne fiber dispersion.

How to Get Rid of Asbestos Compliantly and Safely

Follow these five operational steps to plan, execute, and verify the safe removal and disposal of asbestos materials.

  1. Obtain an Independent Pre-Abatement Survey

    Hire an AHERA-certified inspector to test materials and produce a scope-of-work specification document for bidding.

  2. Solicit Bids from Licensed Abatement Contractors

    Interview licensed, insured abatement contractors and verify active state environmental licensing and worker certifications.

  3. Submit Advance Regulatory Notifications

    Ensure the contractor submits required 10-day advance notification to the state environmental agency and municipal building department.

  4. Execute Controlled Removal Under Negative Pressure

    Verify that work proceeds within sealed poly containment under continuous negative-air HEPA filtration and wet methods.

  5. Conduct Third-Party Air Clearance Testing

    Retain an independent consultant to perform visual inspection and PCM/TEM air clearance testing before tearing down containment.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can a homeowner get rid of asbestos by themselves?

In many states, single-family homeowners are legally permitted to remove asbestos from their primary residence, but they must adhere to strict wet methods, wear P100 PPE, and transport waste to a licensed landfill.

Q2: Can I put asbestos in my regular household garbage?

No, putting asbestos in curbside trash or standard construction dumpsters is strictly illegal under federal, state, and local laws and carries severe financial penalties and criminal fines.

Q3: How much does it cost to get rid of asbestos professionally?

Professional abatement typically costs between $1,500 and $4,500 for small projects (such as popcorn ceilings or pipe wrap) and $5,000 to $15,000+ for whole-home or commercial remediation.

Q4: What is the cheapest way to deal with asbestos?

In-place encapsulation—such as painting over pipe wrap with specialized lagging mastic or installing new flooring over intact vinyl tile—is the most cost-effective and safest option.

Q5: Where does removed asbestos go once it leaves the house?

Asbestos is transported under a formal hazardous waste manifest by a licensed hauler to a state-permitted landfill authorized to bury asbestos beneath layers of clean soil.

Q6: What is air clearance testing after asbestos removal?

An independent environmental consultant collects aggressive air samples inside the containment zone, analyzing them via PCM or TEM to certify fiber levels are safe before containment is removed.

Q7: How do I find a certified asbestos disposal landfill?

Contact your municipal solid waste management district or state Department of Environmental Protection to obtain a list of licensed landfills permitted to accept asbestos waste.

Q8: Does homeowners insurance pay to get rid of asbestos?

Standard homeowners insurance policies contain explicit asbestos exclusion clauses and do not cover routine removal, although coverage may apply if damage results from a covered pipe burst or fire.
