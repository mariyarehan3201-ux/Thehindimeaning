Can You Sue a Deceased Person?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

You cannot legally sue a deceased person directly because a deceased individual loses legal personhood and capacity to be sued upon death. Instead, under probate and tort law, you must direct your lawsuit against the Deceased Person's Estate by naming the appointed Personal Representative, Executor, or Administrator as the legal party defendant.

The Legal Reality: Loss of Legal Personhood at Death

In American jurisprudence, legal personhood terminates the instant a human being passes away. A deceased person cannot be served with legal process, cannot retain an attorney, cannot testify in court, and cannot hold legal title to property. Consequently, if you file a civil summons and complaint naming a deceased individual as the defendant, the court will immediately dismiss the action for lack of subject matter and personal jurisdiction.

However, the death of a tortfeasor (the person whose negligence, malpractice, or breach of contract injured you) does not extinguish their underlying civil liabilities. Under state Survival Statutes (such as California Code of Civil Procedure § 377.20 or Florida Statutes § 46.021), valid legal causes of action survive the death of the wrongdoer.

To pursue financial compensation, the lawsuit must be filed against the Decedent's Estate. In formal pleadings, the defendant is officially captioned as: 'Jane Doe, as Personal Representative of the Estate of John Smith, Deceased.' The judgment is then satisfied out of the decedent's probate assets or paid directly by the decedent's liability insurance policies. The table below compares suing a living individual versus pursuing a claim against an estate.

Litigation Parameter Lawsuit Against Living Defendant Lawsuit Against Decedent's Estate
Named Party Defendant The individual person directly Personal Representative / Executor of Estate
Service of Process Method Personal in-hand service on individual Formal service on the court-appointed Executor
Filing Time Limits (Deadlines) Standard statute of limitations (2 to 4 years) Severely shortened Probate Non-Claim Statutes (3 to 12 months)
Mandatory Pre-Lawsuit Prerequisite None (Direct civil filing permitted) Must file formal Creditor's Claim in probate court
Primary Source of Recovery Personal bank accounts, wages, insurance Estate probate assets and liability insurance policies

The Trap of Probate Non-Claim Statutes and Filing Deadlines

The single greatest hazard in pursuing a legal claim against a deceased wrongdoer is the brutal timeline imposed by state Probate Non-Claim Statutes.

While an ordinary personal injury lawsuit (such as a car accident or slip and fall) generally permits two to three years under general state statutes of limitations, the death of the defendant completely resets and drastically accelerates the legal clock. State probate codes are engineered to settle estates rapidly so that inheritances can be distributed to heirs with finality.

When an estate opens in probate court, the personal representative publishes an official Notice to Creditors in a local newspaper. Under state laws, creditors—including personal injury tort victims—must file a formal Creditor's Claim in the probate proceeding within a narrow window, typically three to four months from the date of publication, or within one year of the decedent's death regardless of notice (e.g., California CCP § 366.2). If you miss this deadline, your claim against the estate's probate assets is permanently barred and extinguished forever. The table below delineates typical probate claim windows.

The Crucial Exception: Suing up to Applicable Insurance Limits

What happens if the decedent had no money, left no probate estate, or the probate non-claim deadline has already passed? In motor vehicle collisions and premises liability accidents, there is a powerful statutory exception: Liability Insurance.

Under statutory exceptions recognized across many states (such as California Probate Code §§ 550 through 554), an injured claimant can file a lawsuit against a deceased person's estate without filing a probate creditor's claim, provided the claimant explicitly limits their financial recovery solely to the proceeds of the decedent's liability insurance policy.

In these actions, the auto insurance carrier (such as State Farm or Geico) has an affirmative contractual and statutory duty to defend the action, hire defense counsel, and pay any settlement or jury verdict up to the policy coverage limits. If no probate estate was opened by the family, the plaintiff's attorney can petition the court to appoint a Special Administrator or Guardian ad Litem specifically to accept service of process on behalf of the estate so the lawsuit against the insurance policy can proceed to judgment.

How to Pursue a Lawsuit Against a Deceased Person's Estate

Master procedural legal guide to filing a formal claim and lawsuit against a deceased wrongdoer.

  1. Confirm Date of Death and Locate the Probate Case

    Search local county probate court records to determine if an estate has been opened and identify the court-appointed Personal Representative.

  2. File a Formal Creditor's Claim in Probate Court

    Draft and submit an itemized Statement of Claim with the probate court clerk before the strict state non-claim deadline expires.

  3. Petition to Open an Estate if Heirs Refuse to Do So

    If the family does not open probate, petition the court as an interested creditor to appoint a Public Administrator or Special Administrator.

  4. Serve the Summons and Caption the Estate in Civil Court

    File the civil complaint naming the Personal Representative as defendant, or proceed under insurance-limitation statutes against the insurer.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I take money from the deceased person's children or heirs?

No. Children and surviving heirs are not personally liable for the debts or torts of their deceased parents; recovery is limited to estate assets.

Q2: What happens if a defendant dies while a lawsuit is already pending?

Your attorney files a formal 'Motion for Substitution of Party' to replace the deceased defendant with their court-appointed estate executor.

Q3: Does life insurance pay out for a lawsuit against a deceased person?

Generally no. Life insurance proceeds pass directly to named beneficiaries outside of probate and are exempt from estate creditor claims.

Q4: Can I sue the deceased driver if I was in a car crash with them?

Yes. You sue their estate, and the deceased driver's auto liability insurance company defends the case and pays the settlement up to policy limits.

Q5: What is a 'Probate Non-Claim Statute'?

A state law establishing a strict, accelerated deadline (typically 3 to 12 months) by which all creditors must submit claims against an estate.

Q6: Can I sue an estate if there is no money in probate?

If the decedent carried liability insurance (such as auto or homeowner insurance), you can still sue to collect the insurance policy limits.

Q7: What happens if the estate executor rejects my creditor claim?

If the executor rejects your claim, you have a limited statutory window (often 60 to 90 days) to file a formal civil lawsuit in circuit court.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

While you cannot sue a deceased individual directly, the legal justice system provides clear mechanisms to recover damages by bringing action against the decedent's estate or their liability insurance provider. Navigating these claims requires swift action to comply with unforgiving probate creditor claim deadlines and procedural requirements for appointing estate administrators. If an individual who injured you passes away, consult an experienced litigation attorney immediately to preserve your claims before statutory probate bar dates permanently extinguish your financial recovery.
