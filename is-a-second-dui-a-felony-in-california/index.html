Is a Second DUI a Felony in California?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: October 1, 2026

In California, a second driving under the influence (DUI) offense within a ten-year lookback period is generally charged as a misdemeanor rather than an automatic felony. Under California Vehicle Code Section 23152, standard impaired driving offenses without aggravating circumstances remain misdemeanor violations. However, a second DUI will be elevated to a severe felony prosecution if the incident caused physical bodily injury or death to another individual, if the defendant has a prior felony DUI conviction on their criminal record, or if gross negligence manslaughter charges are filed by district prosecutors.

California Vehicle Code Framework: Misdemeanor vs Felony Classification

California enforces rigorous impaired driving laws governed primarily under California Vehicle Code (VC) Section 23152(a) and Section 23152(b). When an individual is arrested for operating a motor vehicle while impaired or with a blood chemical concentration measuring 0.08 percent or greater, prosecutors audit the driver's criminal record within a ten-year lookback window. If exactly one prior conviction for impaired driving or reckless driving under VC 23103.5 (wet reckless) occurred within the preceding ten years, the new arrest is formally classified as a second-offense misdemeanor DUI.

Although classified as a misdemeanor, the statutory criminal penalties for a second offense escalate substantially compared to a first arrest. California judges are legally required to impose mandatory jail time, extensive probation, significant court fines, eighteen to thirty months of licensed treatment schooling, and installation of a certified ignition interlock device (IID) on all vehicles operated by the offender. When facing substantial court hearings, individuals frequently evaluate hiring a California impaired driving defense attorney to navigate DMV administrative per se hearings, evaluate breath testing calibration records, and seek reduced charges.

Statutory Penalties and Sentencing for a Second Misdemeanor DUI

Even when a second offense avoids felony filing, misdemeanor sentencing in California is severe. Under California Vehicle Code Section 23540 and Section 23542, judges must impose a minimum of ninety-six continuous hours of county jail custody, with sentences often extending from ten days to several months depending on local county court guidelines. In certain counties, alternative sentencing programs such as supervised electronic home detention, work furlough, or community service work crews may be granted at judicial discretion.

Financial assessments are equally demanding. Base court fines range from $390 to $1,000, but mandatory state penalty assessments, courthouse construction fees, and restitution funds routinely push total court costs between $1,800 and $3,500. Additionally, offenders are sentenced to informal summary probation lasting between three and five years. Key conditions of probation include complete abstinence from operating a vehicle with any measurable chemical concentration, consenting to preliminary roadside breath screenings, and refraining from committing any further criminal violations.

Aggravating Factors and Strategic Legal Defense Options

District attorneys aggressively scrutinize second arrest files for aggravating statutory enhancements. Operating a vehicle at excessive speeds (thirty miles per hour over the speed limit on a freeway), having a child under fourteen years old inside the passenger cabin (VC 23572), refusing a lawful chemical test (VC 23577), or registering an exceptionally elevated concentration exceeding 0.15 percent will result in mandatory additional jail time enhancements that cannot be suspended by the judge.

Skilled defense counsel challenge repeat charges on multiple constitutional and technical grounds. Attorneys file suppression motions under Penal Code Section 1538.5 if law enforcement officers lacked reasonable suspicion to initiate the initial vehicle stop. Counsel also subpoena forensic maintenance logs for breath test instruments to identify calibration drift or failure to observe the mandatory fifteen-minute continuous observation period, which can invalidate chemical testing evidence.

How to Navigate a Second DUI Charge in California

Follow these essential legal and administrative steps to protect your driving privileges, comply with California court processes, and prepare your defense.

  1. Request a California DMV Administrative Hearing Within 10 Days

    Contact the California DMV Driver Safety Branch within ten calendar days of your arrest date to formally contest your administrative per se driver license suspension and request a temporary stay.

  2. Retain Experienced Legal Defense Counsel

    Hire a dedicated California criminal defense attorney specializing in impaired driving cases to obtain police dashcam footage, body camera video, and breath instrument maintenance logs.

  3. Enroll Early in an 18-Month Licensed Education Program

    Proactively register for a state-certified SB 38 eighteen-month educational program to demonstrate rehabilitative commitment to the court and expedite eligibility for a restricted IID license.

  4. Arrange Ignition Interlock Device Installation on Your Vehicle

    Schedule an installation appointment with an authorized California IID provider to obtain Form DL 920, allowing you to regain legal driving privileges during your administrative suspension.

  5. Obtain and File an SR-22 Certificate of Financial Responsibility

    Contact your filing carrier or an independent broker to file a California SR-22 certificate with the DMV, maintaining uninterrupted coverage to satisfy license reinstatement conditions.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is a second DUI automatically a felony in California?

No. A second DUI within a ten-year lookback period in California is typically charged as a misdemeanor, provided there are no injuries, fatal accidents, or prior felony DUI convictions on your record.

Q2: What is the mandatory minimum jail time for a second DUI in California?

California state law mandates a minimum of ninety-six hours of county jail for a second misdemeanor DUI conviction, though many county courts impose between ten days and ninety days depending on local policies.

Q3: What is the 10-year lookback period in California?

The ten-year lookback period is calculated from the date of the first arrest to the date of the second arrest. Any prior conviction for impaired driving or wet reckless within that window counts as a prior strike.

Q4: Can I get a restricted driver license after a second DUI conviction?

Yes. Under California law, second-time offenders can typically obtain an ignition interlock device (IID) restricted license immediately or after a short waiting period by installing an IID, filing an SR-22, and paying DMV fees.

Q5: How long is DUI school for a second offense in California?

A second conviction requires enrollment in a licensed eighteen-month multiple offender program (SB 38), which consists of fifty-two hours of group counseling, twelve hours of education, and individual interviews.

Q6: When does a second DUI become a felony in California?

A second DUI becomes a felony if another person suffered great bodily injury or death in an accident, or if the driver has a prior felony DUI conviction on their criminal record.

Q7: Can a second DUI charge be reduced to a wet reckless in California?

While difficult, experienced defense attorneys can sometimes negotiate a second-offense wet reckless reduction if there are flaws in police procedures, uncalibrated chemical testing equipment, or marginal chemical readings.

Q8: How much does a second DUI conviction cost overall in California?

Between court fines, penalty assessments, eighteen months of educational classes, IID installation and monitoring, legal defense fees, and increased responsibility filings rates, total costs frequently range from $12,000 to $25,000.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

While a second DUI in California is normally charged as a misdemeanor rather than a felony, it represents a grave criminal charge carrying mandatory jail custody, license suspension, thousands of dollars in fines, and prolonged educational requirements. If aggravating circumstances such as third-party injuries or prior felony convictions are present, the state will aggressively pursue felony state prison sentences. Consulting experienced criminal defense counsel immediately after arrest is vital to safeguard constitutional rights, preserve driving privileges at DMV administrative hearings, and build a strategic legal defense.
