Asbestos Shingles on Roof

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Asbestos shingles on roof structures, commonly known as asbestos-cement shingles or Transite roofing, are rigid exterior roof coverings composed of Portland cement reinforced with chrysotile asbestos fibers. Installed on residential homes, barns, and commercial structures from the 1920s through the late 1970s, these shingles delivered exceptional fireproof protection, rot immunity, and multi-decade weatherproofing. While non-friable when intact, understanding maintenance boundaries, roof-over options, and safe removal guidelines is critical for homeowners.

Composition, Profiles, and Historical Manufacturing

Asbestos-cement roof shingles were engineered by blending roughly fifteen to thirty percent refined chrysotile asbestos fibers with seventy to eighty-five percent hydraulic Portland cement. This dense slurry was formed under extreme hydraulic pressing and steam autoclave curing, producing thin, extraordinarily hard, stone-like shingles that withstood hail strikes, driving rain, and gale-force winds without deteriorating.

Major building manufacturers—including Johns-Manville, Keasbey & Mattison, Flintkote, and GAF—produced these shingles in a variety of architectural profiles. Common styles included interlocking diamond-shaped shingles, rectangular simulated Dutch lap shakes with embossed wood-grain textures, and hexagonal French-method tiles, often pre-colored with mineral oxide pigments in earthy tones of slate gray, terra cotta red, moss green, and weathered brown.

Friability, Weathering, and Environmental Hazards

Under environmental regulations, asbestos roof shingles are classified as Category II non-friable asbestos-containing materials. Because the mineral fibers are permanently locked within a solid, hardened Portland cement matrix, normal outdoor weathering, rain, and sun exposure do not release significant concentrations of fibers into the ambient air. An intact, undamaged asbestos roof presents virtually zero respiratory risk to the home's occupants.

The health hazard emerges when roof shingles are subjected to mechanical forces that crush, pulverize, or abrade the cement matrix. Walking carelessly on brittle vintage shingles, power washing with high-pressure water streams, cutting tiles with power saws, or scraping moss with wire brushes shatters the cement, generating clouds of respirable crystalline dust. Severe hail damage, falling tree limbs, or structural fires can also fracture tiles, leaving exposed edges that shed fibers over time.

Roof-Over Encapsulation versus Complete Abatement

Homeowners seeking to address an aging asbestos roof generally choose between two primary engineering approaches: roof-over encapsulation or complete professional tear-off. Roof-over encapsulation involves installing vertical and horizontal wooden or metal furring strips directly over the existing shingles, filling the gaps with rigid foam insulation, and installing modern standing-seam metal roofing or asphalt shingles on top. This approach completely seals the asbestos, eliminates hazardous tear-off dust, and saves thousands of dollars in disposal fees.

However, if the roof deck is rotting, structural framing cannot bear the weight of a second roof, or extensive tiles are missing, full professional abatement is required. Certified roofing abatement teams erect perimeter scaffolding, line the ground with double-layer six-mil polyethylene sheeting, continuously wet the shingles with amended water, and carefully unfasten each shingle using pry bars without breaking them, lowering them in sealed containers to the ground.

How to Safely Manage Asbestos Shingles on a Roof

Follow these five tactical steps to evaluate, maintain, or replace vintage asbestos roof shingles safely.

  1. Perform a Ground-Level Inspection

    Use binoculars from the ground to check for cracked, slipped, or missing shingles, strictly avoiding walking on the roof.

  2. Never Power Wash the Roof

    Do not use pressure washers or abrasive scrubbers to clean moss or lichen, as this blasts toxic fibers into the yard and air.

  3. Apply Biocidal Moss Sprays Gently

    Treat moss growth using a garden hose sprayer with gentle zinc or copper-based biocidal wash solutions without scrubbing.

  4. Evaluate a Metal Roof-Over

    Consult with a licensed roofing contractor to determine if your roof framing can accommodate furring strips and a standing-seam metal roof overlay.

  5. Hire Licensed Abatement for Tear-Off

    If removal is necessary, contract a state-licensed abatement firm equipped with ground containment and certified hazardous disposal.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How long do asbestos roof shingles last?

Asbestos-cement roof shingles are exceptionally durable and can easily last 50 to 80 years or more with minimal maintenance if left undamaged.

Q2: Can you walk on an asbestos shingle roof?

You should never walk directly on an asbestos shingle roof; the vintage cement tiles are brittle and will easily crack under foot traffic, creating leaks and hazards.

Q3: Can you put a metal roof over asbestos shingles?

Yes, installing furring strips, rigid foam insulation, and standing-seam metal roofing directly over asbestos shingles is an approved, highly popular encapsulation method.

Q4: How can I tell if my roof shingles contain asbestos?

Asbestos shingles look like hard, rigid cement tiles, often with a diamond or wood-grain shape, sound ceramic when tapped, and were installed mostly between 1920 and 1978.

Q5: How much does it cost to remove an asbestos roof?

Professional removal typically costs between $8 and $18 per square foot (averaging $12,000 to $25,000 for a whole house), not including the cost of installing the new roof.

Q6: Can you pressure wash an asbestos roof to clean it?

No, pressure washing an asbestos roof is illegal under environmental regulations because high-pressure water abrades the cement, dispersing billions of toxic fibers into the air.

Q7: Can I replace a few broken asbestos roof shingles?

Yes, you can replace individual broken shingles using modern non-asbestos fiber-cement replacement tiles, such as GAF Weatherside or salvaged tiles.

Q8: Do home insurance companies insure homes with asbestos roofs?

Most standard insurers will cover homes with intact asbestos roofs, though some carriers may exclude hail damage or require replacement if tiles are deteriorating.
