How Much Is a Police Report?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 16, 2026

Understanding how much is a police report requires examining municipal police department administrative policies, third-party digital record portal convenience fees, certification surcharges, and state public records disclosure statutes. Across the United States, obtaining an official copy of a police accident report or criminal incident report typically costs between $5 and $25. While in-person administrative pickups at local municipal police headquarters are occasionally free or carry a nominal $2 to $10 clerical copying fee, purchasing digital collision reports online through statewide aggregators (such as BuyCrash or LexisNexis) routinely costs between $10 and $22.

In-Person Requests vs. Online Third-Party Database Portals

The fee charged for an official police report depends heavily on the request channel utilized. The traditional method involves visiting the records division or records window of the municipal police department, county sheriff's office, or state highway patrol barracks that investigated the event. At municipal headquarters, paper copies of standard incident reports (such as theft, burglary, or vandalism) generally cost between $5 and $15. In many progressive jurisdictions, direct crime victims listed in the report are entitled to receive one complimentary copy free of charge upon presenting valid government identification.

However, in modern law enforcement administration, the vast majority of motor vehicle crash reports are outsourced to private digital database contractors such as LexisNexis BuyCrash, CRASHDOCS by Carfax, or Docview. These centralized web portals allow motorists, auto insurance adjusters, and attorneys to search for and download collision reports 24/7 without visiting a police station. These private vendors charge convenience processing fees ranging from $10 to $22 per digital PDF download, splitting a portion of the revenue back to the contracting police agency.

Certified Copies, FOIA Public Records, and Redaction Fees

For standard auto insurance claims, a basic downloaded PDF copy of a collision report is completely sufficient; insurance adjusters only need the investigating officer's diagram, insurance policy numbers, and citation notes. However, if your police report is required as formal legal evidence in a civil personal injury lawsuit, criminal trial, or administrative hearing, you will require a 'Certified Copy.' A certified police report features a stamped or embossed municipal seal and an official attestation signed by the records custodian, adding an administrative certification surcharge of $5 to $15 above standard copy fees.

When requesting extensive criminal investigative files, body-worn camera footage, or 911 dispatch audio tapes under state open records or Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) laws, pricing structures shift dramatically. Police departments are legally permitted to bill requesters for actual labor costs incurred while locating files, converting video formats, and redacting confidential information (such as juvenile identities, medical history, and social security numbers). Extensive FOIA file packages can easily incur administrative processing fees ranging from $25 to over $100.

Information Requirements and Insurance Company Acquisition

To obtain a police report quickly without administrative delays, you must supply the records clerk or search portal with precise tracking information. Following any motor vehicle accident or crime investigation, the investigating officer hands you an informational receipt card containing an official Police Incident or Case Number. Having this exact case number—along with the date of the incident, exact street location, and the correct legal names of the involved parties—streamlines processing and avoids rejected search queries.

Importantly, personal motorists involved in minor fender benders rarely need to purchase a crash report themselves. When you file a claim with your auto insurance carrier, your assigned claims adjuster will automatically order the official police crash report directly from the reporting law enforcement agency or their third-party database portal at the insurance company's expense. Before spending your own money on an online portal, contact your insurance adjuster to confirm if they have already retrieved the file for your claim.

How to Obtain a Copy of an Official Police Report

Follow these five simple steps to locate your case number, choose the best channel, and retrieve your report.

  1. Locate Your Police Incident or Case Number

    Retrieve the case number card provided by the investigating officer at the scene of the incident or accident.

  2. Check If Your Auto Insurer Already Has It

    Contact your insurance adjuster to verify if they purchased the report directly, saving you time and fees.

  3. Determine the Correct Law Enforcement Agency

    Confirm whether the report was filed by the city police, county sheriff, or state highway patrol.

  4. Visit the Official Online Portal or Records Division

    Access the police department's designated vendor (BuyCrash/CRASHDOCS) or visit the station window.

  5. Pay the Required Fee and Download the Report

    Submit payment via credit card or cash and save a digital PDF copy for your personal records.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How much does a police accident report usually cost?

Most police accident reports cost between $5 and $20, depending on whether you purchase in-person or through an online portal.

Q2: Can you get a police report for free?

Direct victims of crimes can frequently obtain one free copy in person; also, your insurance company often orders it at their expense.

Q3: Why do online police report websites charge extra fees?

Third-party portals like BuyCrash or LexisNexis charge convenience fees ($3 to $7) for 24/7 digital access and database hosting.

Q4: How long after an accident is a police report available?

Collision reports are typically completed, reviewed by supervisors, and approved within 3 to 7 business days.

Q5: What is a certified police report and why does it cost more?

A certified report includes an official embossed seal and custodian signature for court evidence, costing an extra $5 to $15.

Q6: Can anyone look up and buy my police report?

Under state public records laws, basic crash reports are public records, though sensitive personal information like SSNs are redacted.

Q7: What information do I need to get a police report?

You need the official incident/case number, date of the incident, street location, and names of the involved parties.

Q8: How much does a 911 call recording cost?

Obtaining 911 audio recordings under public records laws typically costs between $15 and $40 for copying and redaction labor.
