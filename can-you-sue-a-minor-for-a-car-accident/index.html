Can You Sue a Minor for Car Crash?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, you can sue a minor for a car accident, but civil litigation procedures require naming the minor through a parent or court-appointed Guardian Ad Litem. More importantly, personal injury lawsuits typically target the minor's parents under established legal doctrines—including Parental Vicarious Liability, Negligent Entrustment, and the Family Purpose Doctrine—to access commercial auto insurance coverage.

The Legal Capacity of Minors in Civil Tort Law

Colliding with a vehicle operated by a teenage driver is a harrowing experience. Teenage drivers—statistically classified by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) as the most accident-prone demographic on American roadways—frequently cause catastrophic accidents due to excessive speeding, nighttime driving inexperience, and dangerous smartphone texting distractions.

When an injured adult victim seeks legal accountability, an immediate procedural question arises: Can an individual who is under 18 years old be named as a defendant in a civil lawsuit? In American tort law, the answer is an unambiguous yes. While minors lack the legal capacity to enter into binding business contracts, common law universally holds that minors are legally responsible for their own civil torts, including negligent motor vehicle operation.

However, because a minor is not legally 'sui juris' (possessing full legal capacity), a plaintiff cannot serve a lawsuit directly on a 16-year-old high school student alone. State civil practice rules mandate that the lawsuit must formally name the minor 'by and through their parent, natural guardian, or a court-appointed Guardian Ad Litem.' But because typical teenage drivers possess zero personal assets, bank accounts, or real estate, your attorney's primary strategic focus is establishing Parental Legal Liability. Statutory Parental Liability: The Driver's License Sponsor Trap

In many states across the country, state legislatures have enacted explicit statutory codes that automatically impose Joint and Several Liability on parents who sign their minor child's driver's license application.

For example, under California Vehicle Code § 17707, any civil liability of a minor arising out of their driving a motor vehicle is directly imposed upon the parent or guardian who signed and verified the minor's application for a license. The statute dictates that the parent and the minor are jointly and severally liable. Under Cal. Veh. Code § 17709, statutory financial caps apply to this imputed liability ($15,000 for injury to one person, $30,000 for multiple persons, and $5,000 for property damage), but these limits do not restrict recoveries against the family's auto insurance policy.

Similarly, in states like Florida (Fla. Stat. § 322.09), a parent who signs a minor's learner's permit or driver license application assumes 100% full, unlimited joint civil liability for all damages caused by the minor's negligent driving—with zero statutory monetary caps. If a 17-year-old causes a crash that paralyzes an innocent victim in Florida, the parents' personal real estate, savings, and future wages are directly exposed on equal footing with the teenage driver. Who Pays the Settlement? Family Auto and Umbrella Insurance

When you sue a minor for a car accident, financial recovery does not come from a teenager's part-time summer job savings. Settlements and judgments are funded through the parents' Family Auto Insurance Policy and Personal Umbrella Policy.

Under standard personal auto insurance underwriting, any household family member who resides in the home and operates a family vehicle is an Insured Driver under the policy's omnibus clause. Standard personal auto liability limits typically range from $100,000 to $500,000 per occurrence. Furthermore, affluent parents with teenage drivers frequently maintain Personal Umbrella Policies providing an additional $1,000,000 to $5,000,000 in secondary liability protection.

Your personal injury trial lawyer will file a formal insurance claim and civil complaint naming both the minor and the vehicle's registered owner parents. If the parents' insurer attempts to claim the teen was an 'excluded driver' on the policy, your attorney will investigate whether the parents committed Negligent Entrustment by leaving vehicle keys accessible to an unlicensed or excluded teen, holding the parents directly liable for all damages.

How to File a Claim for an Accident Caused by a Minor

Step-by-step litigation roadmap to establish parental liability and secure auto insurance recovery.

  1. Confirm the Registered Owner of the Striking Vehicle

    Review the police crash report to identify whether the vehicle title is registered in the minor's name or the parents' names.

  2. Identify Who Signed the Minor's Driver License Application

    Have your attorney verify with the state DMV whether a parent signed the driver's license application, establishing statutory joint liability.

  3. Demand Complete Policy Disclosure for Family Auto and Umbrella

    Issue a formal insurance disclosure demand requiring the parents' insurer to disclose all primary, secondary, and umbrella policy limits.

  4. Name Minor Through Parents / Guardian Ad Litem in Lawsuit

    File a civil complaint naming the minor 'by and through' their parents, alongside direct negligence counts against the parents for entrustment.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can a 16-year-old be sued for a car accident?

Yes. Minors are legally liable for their own civil negligence, but the lawsuit must be filed through their parents or a Guardian Ad Litem.

Q2: Are parents automatically responsible for a teen's car crash?

In states like FL and CA, parents who sign license paperwork are automatically liable; in other states, liability requires vehicle ownership or entrustment.

Q3: What is negligent entrustment of a vehicle?

Negligent entrustment occurs when a parent allows a teen to drive despite knowing they are reckless, unlicensed, or have a history of tickets/crashes.

Q4: What if the teen was driving a friend's car?

The car owner's insurance is primary, and the teen's parents' family auto insurance acts as secondary coverage to pay remaining damages.

Q5: Can an insurance company deny coverage for a teenage driver?

Only if the teen was formally listed as an 'excluded driver' on the policy; otherwise, permissive household drivers are covered.

Q6: Can a victim take a parent's house after a teen's car accident?

In extreme catastrophic crashes exceeding insurance limits, a civil judgment can place liens on a parent's non-exempt personal assets.

Q7: What is the Family Purpose Doctrine in car accident law?

A legal doctrine in many states holding the head of a household liable for accidents caused by family members driving household vehicles.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

You can sue a minor for a car accident, and legal doctrines ensure that victims have direct access to the parents' substantial auto insurance policies and personal assets. Through statutory license sponsor liability, the family purpose doctrine, and negligent entrustment, parents are held legally accountable for the harm their minor children inflict on public roads. Seek emergency medical care immediately, obtain the police accident report, preserve scene evidence, and retain an experienced car accident attorney to hold the teen and their parents fully accountable.
