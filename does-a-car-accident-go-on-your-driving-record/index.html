Does an Accident Go on Your Driving Record?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Following a motor vehicle collision, drivers face an overwhelming sequence of practical and financial concerns, from vehicle repairs and medical treatment to insurance rate increases. Among the most pressing long-term questions is whether the collision will be officially documented on your state Motor Vehicle Record (MVR) and how that entry will impact your driving privileges, insurance premiums, and employment opportunities. Whether a car accident appears on your driving record depends on specific criteria, including who was legally at fault, the monetary value of property damage, whether injuries occurred, and whether police citations were issued at the scene. Understanding how departments of motor vehicles (DMVs) process collision reports and how insurers evaluate your MVR helps drivers protect their records and navigate post-accident reporting requirements effectively.

When Does a Car Accident Appear on Your Driving Record?

In the United States, driving records—officially classified as Motor Vehicle Records (MVRs)—are maintained at the state level by the relevant Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV), Bureau of Motor Vehicles (BMV), or state Department of Public Safety. An MVR serves as a comprehensive administrative history of an individual driver's license, detailing moving violation convictions, administrative suspensions, license point accumulations, and reported vehicular collisions. However, not every minor scratch or fender bender results in an automatic entry on your official record. Specific statutory criteria determine whether a collision is transmitted to and permanently indexed on your state driving history.

The primary trigger that causes an accident to appear on an MVR is the formal submission of an official police collision report. When law enforcement officers respond to a crash scene, investigate physical vehicle damage, and interview involved motorists, they generate a state accident report (such as Form SR-1 or equivalent state accident documentation). State laws mandate that police officers file these reports with the state DMV whenever an incident exceeds statutory reporting thresholds. In most states, reporting is required if any party suffers bodily injury, if a fatality occurs, or if total estimated property damage exceeds a specific statutory dollar threshold—typically ranging between five hundred and two thousand dollars depending on the jurisdiction.

Furthermore, in many states, motorists are statutorily required to file an individual accident report directly with the DMV within ten days if law enforcement did not investigate the collision. If an accident involves moving violations—such as speeding, running a red light, reckless driving, or driving under the influence—the corresponding traffic citation conviction will accompany the accident entry on your MVR. Motorists comparing traffic penalties often evaluate how long serious infractions linger, such as researching how long a DWI stays on your driving record compared to standard civil collisions. At-Fault vs. Not-At-Fault Accidents and DMV Demerit Points

A widespread point of confusion among motorists is how legal fault affects driving records. When an accident is reported to the DMV, state records typically log the date, location, involved vehicles, and reporting police agency. In many states, the MVR will record the occurrence of an accident regardless of which driver caused it. However, the legal and financial ramifications differ drastically between at-fault and not-at-fault classifications. State DMV records distinguish between a driver deemed primarily responsible for an accident and a motorist who was an innocent victim of another party's negligence.

Most state licensing authorities operate a driver license point system (demerit points) designed to penalize chronic traffic offenders. Crucially, in the overwhelming majority of states, points are assigned solely for convictions of underlying moving violations—not for the physical collision itself. For example, if you rear-end another vehicle because you were following too closely, the DMV assesses points against your license for the following-too-closely infraction, while the accident itself is simply annotated as an incident marker. If a collision occurred due to severe weather or mechanical failure without an accompanying moving violation, an accident entry may appear without any license penalty points.

For commercial motor vehicle operators holding a Commercial Driver's License (CDL), accident entries carry disproportionately severe consequences. Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) regulations require that all DOT-recordable accidents appear on commercial driver records, impacting safety scores regardless of fault. Commercial operators must manage these entries carefully, reflecting the severe career consequences seen when examining what happens when a truck driver has an accident under federal transportation mandates. When civil liability is fiercely contested, drivers often consult legal counsel to determine can you sue in a car accident to clear disputed fault notations and recover repair costs. Duration on Record, Insurance Surges, and Clearing Your Record

The duration an accident remains visible on your official driving record varies according to individual state motor vehicle codes. In most states—including California, Texas, Florida, and New York—standard at-fault and non-fault collisions remain on your driving history for a minimum of three years, with many jurisdictions retaining records for five years. For collisions involving major criminal traffic offenses, such as vehicular manslaughter, hit-and-run, or driving under the influence, the record duration extends from seven to ten years, and in states like California, certain alcohol-related vehicular offenses remain permanently visible on DMV files.

While state DMVs track driving records for licensing and public safety oversight, auto insurance carriers evaluate risk through private underwriting databases. Even if an accident does not appear on your state MVR, it will almost certainly be recorded on your Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (C.L.U.E.) report or LexisNexis insurance history database if any claim was filed. Insurers review both your state MVR and C.L.U.E. reports when recalculating premiums at policy renewal periods. An at-fault accident entry typically results in a loss of good driver discounts and triggers rate surcharges that persist for thirty-six to sixty months. In contrast, states such as California and Massachusetts prohibit auto insurers from raising rates for accidents where the policyholder was demonstrably not at fault.

Drivers who identify incorrect accident notations on their MVR can take proactive administrative steps to correct their records. If an accident is improperly listed as at fault when the official police report clearly demonstrates that the other motorist was cited and held solely responsible, the driver can file a data dispute with the state DMV. Submitting a certified copy of the final police report, a formal letter of exoneration from the other driver's insurer, or an official court dismissal document will prompt the DMV to amend the record to reflect a not-at-fault status. Additionally, completing a state-approved defensive driving or traffic school course can dismiss associated moving violation points, preventing insurance rate surges and keeping your driving privileges secure.

How to Check and Manage an Accident on Your Driving Record

Follow these structured administrative steps to order your motor vehicle record, verify collision entries, resolve inaccuracies, and mitigate insurance surcharges.

  1. Order an Official Copy of Your Motor Vehicle Record (MVR)

    Visit your state Department of Motor Vehicles website or local field office to request an official certified copy of your driving record to see what collision data is documented.

  2. Cross-Reference the MVR Entry with the Official Police Crash Report

    Compare the MVR listing with the police accident report, checking that fault determinations, dates, vehicle citations, and damage descriptions are accurately reflected.

  3. Submit a Formal DMV Data Dispute for Inaccurate Fault Designations

    If a not-at-fault accident is mistakenly designated as at fault, submit a certified police report and an insurance letter of acceptance from the opposing insurer to the DMV.

  4. Complete an Approved Defensive Driving or Traffic Safety Course

    Enroll in a state-certified defensive driving course to remove penalty points from associated traffic citations and submit the certificate to your insurer for discounts.

  5. Review Your LexisNexis C.L.U.E. Report After Thirty-Six Months

    Request a free annual C.L.U.E. insurance loss report to ensure that accident claims roll off your insurance history once the three to five year surcharge window expires.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Does a minor fender bender go on your driving record if police were not called?

If property damage is below the state statutory reporting threshold (often $1,000), no one was injured, and neither driver called the police or filed a state accident report, the incident will not appear on your state driving record.

Q2: Will a not-at-fault car accident raise your auto insurance rates?

In many states, insurance companies are prohibited by law from raising premiums for accidents where you were not at fault. However, in states without these consumer protections, insurers may reduce certain preferred driver discounts.

Q3: How many points go on your driver license after a car accident?

In most states, points are not assessed for the accident itself. Demerit points are added only if you are issued a traffic citation for an underlying moving violation, such as speeding, following too closely, or reckless driving.

Q4: How long does an at-fault car accident stay on your motor vehicle record?

In most jurisdictions, an at-fault car accident remains on your state driving record for three to five years from the incident date. Serious violations involving alcohol or felony offenses may remain visible for seven to ten years.

Q5: Can you expunge or remove a car accident from your driving record early?

You cannot expunge a legitimate, accurately reported accident. However, if an accident is inaccurately recorded as at-fault or placed on your record due to clerical error, you can submit official police reports to have the DMV amend the entry.

Q6: Do parking lot accidents go on your driving record?

Parking lot accidents on private property generally do not appear on your driving record unless police are called to investigate significant damage exceeding state dollar thresholds, someone is injured, or a criminal citation like hit-and-run is issued.

Q7: What is the difference between an MVR driving record and a C.L.U.E. report?

An MVR is a public administrative record maintained by the state DMV documenting license points, citations, and reported crashes. A C.L.U.E. report is a private insurance industry claims database tracking claims filed with insurance carriers.

Q8: Will an accident in another state show up on your home state driving record?

Yes. Most states participate in the Driver License Compact (DLC) and the National Driver Register (NDR), meaning traffic convictions and major reported accidents from other states are transmitted back to your home state licensing agency.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Whether a car accident goes on your driving record depends on the severity of property damage, the presence of injuries, and whether law enforcement filed an official crash report. While at-fault collisions and associated traffic citations remain on your state MVR for three to five years, not-at-fault accidents do not result in license penalty points and cannot legally trigger insurance surcharges in many states. By obtaining a certified copy of your MVR after any collision, promptly disputing inaccurate fault determinations, and completing approved driver safety courses, you can minimize long-term insurance penalties and preserve a clean driving record.
