Defining OS&D: Over, Short, and Damaged Freight

In commercial trucking, Less-Than-Truckload (LTL) freight logistics, and warehouse inventory management, OS&D stands for Over, Short, and Damaged. It is the standardized industry classification used by receiving docks, motor carriers, and third-party logistics (3PL) providers to document discrepancies between the physical freight delivered and the official delivery receipt.

When a commercial semi-trailer backs into an unloading dock, receiving personnel must inspect the incoming cargo against the carrier Bill of Lading (BOL) and delivery receipt. Any discrepancy falls into one of three specific OS&D categories, initiating an official investigation, inventory quarantine, and formal freight claim process.

Review the three fundamental classifications of OS&D freight exceptions:

OS&D Discrepancy Category Physical Receiving Condition Operational Cause Standard Logistics Resolution
Over (Overage) More freight delivered than listed on the Bill of Lading Cross-dock sorting error; extra pallet misloaded onto trailer Held in carrier quarantine; cross-referenced with lost freight manifests
Short (Shortage) Fewer cartons or pallets delivered than listed on the BOL Freight left behind at terminal; stolen in transit; misrouted Driver and dock manager sign Delivery Receipt noting exact missing piece count
Damaged (Damage) Freight arrives visibly broken, crushed, wet, or tipped over Improper pallet wrapping, forklift puncture, shifting during transit Photographed immediately, damage noted on Delivery Receipt, claim filed
Concealed Damage Outer packaging intact but internal merchandise broken Severe transit vibration, internal drop impact Must be reported in writing within 5 business days of delivery
Refused Freight Consignee refuses delivery due to severe catastrophic damage Freight completely unsalvageable or rejected by quality control Returned to carrier terminal; shipper contacted for salvage or return

The Legal and Financial Weight of the Delivery Receipt (Proof of Delivery)

In freight claims governed by the Carmack Amendment (49 U.S.C. 14706) under United States federal transportation law, the delivery receipt—often called the Proof of Delivery (POD)—is a binding legal document. When a receiving clerk signs the delivery receipt without noting any discrepancies, it is classified as a 'Clean Delivery Receipt'.

Signing a clean receipt legally certifies that all cargo was received in full count and in pristine physical condition. If warehouse workers later unwrap the pallets and discover missing cartons or crushed boxes, filing a freight claim becomes exceedingly difficult. The receiving party must prove that the damage occurred while in the carrier custody rather than during post-delivery warehouse handling. Therefore, noting OS&D exceptions clearly on the delivery receipt before the driver departs is the single most critical task on any receiving dock.

Compare the responsibilities of key supply chain parties during an OS&D exception:

Supply Chain Stakeholder Immediate OS&D Responsibility Financial Liability / Documentation Requirement
Receiving Clerk / Consignee Count pieces, inspect pallet wrap, write detailed exceptions on BOL Must take timestamped photos before breaking down pallets
Delivery Truck Driver Witness inspection, verify piece count, co-sign delivery receipt Transmits exception code to carrier terminal dispatch immediately
Motor Carrier Claims Department Initiates OS&D investigation, reviews dock cross-dock logs Liable for actual freight value under Carmack Amendment rules
Shipper / Manufacturer Verifies original packing slip against shipping dock manifest Issues replacement merchandise or credits consignee invoice
Freight Broker / 3PL Coordinates communication between carrier, shipper, and receiver Facilitates claims documentation and tracks replacement freight

Rigorous OS&D reporting protocols protect companies from absorbing thousands of dollars in lost inventory and shipping damage costs.

How to Properly Document an OS&D Exception at the Receiving Dock

Follow this standardized freight receiving procedure when delivery discrepancies occur.

  1. Count Total Pallets and Cartons Before Signing

    Compare the physical piece count directly against the carrier delivery receipt; never accept a verbal piece count from the delivery driver.

  2. Inspect Pallets for Tipped Loads or Torn Shrink-Wrap

    Look for crushed box corners, broken wooden pallet stringers, wet packaging, or missing manufacturer security tape.

  3. Document Exact Discrepancies on the Delivery Receipt

    Write explicit notes on the paper or digital POD (e.g., 'SHORT 2 CARTONS ON SKID 3' or 'PALLET 1 CRUSHED BY FORKLIFT').

  4. Take High-Resolution Timestamped Photos

    Photograph the damaged freight while it is still inside the truck trailer, showing the trailer number, pro tracking barcode, and damaged cargo.

  5. Obtain Driver Signature on the Exception Note

    Ensure the commercial delivery driver signs and prints their name next to the written exception, retaining a signed physical copy.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What does OS&D mean in freight and trucking?

OS&D stands for Over, Short, and Damaged, the standard industry term for freight delivery discrepancies compared to the Bill of Lading.

Q2: What does 'Over' mean in OS&D?

'Over' means more freight or extra boxes were delivered than what was listed on the official shipping manifest.

Q3: What does 'Short' mean in OS&D?

'Short' means fewer pieces, cartons, or pallets were delivered than what the Bill of Lading indicated should be on the truck.

Q4: What does 'Damaged' mean in OS&D?

'Damaged' means freight arrived broken, crushed, punctured, or compromised due to improper handling or shifting during transit.

Q5: What is a clean bill of lading or clean delivery receipt?

A clean delivery receipt is signed without any noted exceptions, legally certifying that all freight was received in full count and perfect condition.

Q6: What is concealed damage in freight shipping?

Concealed damage occurs when the exterior box or shrink-wrap looks undamaged, but the internal contents are found broken after unpacking.

Q7: How long do you have to report concealed freight damage?

Under standard NMFC rules, concealed damage must be reported to the freight carrier in writing within 5 business days of delivery.

Q8: Can a consignee refuse a damaged freight shipment?

Yes. If freight is completely destroyed and has zero salvage value, the receiver can legally refuse delivery, forcing the carrier to return it.
