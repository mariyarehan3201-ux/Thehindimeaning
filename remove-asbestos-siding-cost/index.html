Remove Asbestos Siding Cost

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Calculating the cost to remove asbestos siding is an essential budgeting step for homeowners and commercial property managers planning exterior renovations or modernizing vintage structures. Asbestos-cement shingles—widely known as transite siding—were prized between the 1920s and 1970s for their fireproof qualities and durability. However, when these rigid shingles become weathered, chipped, or disturbed during remodeling, microscopic mineral fibers can be released into ambient air. Understanding per-square-foot labor charges, hazardous waste disposal fees, and replacement cladding costs allows property owners to budget accurately.

Cost Breakdown and Per-Square-Foot Financial Metrics for Siding Removal

The national average cost to professionally remove exterior asbestos-cement siding ranges from seven to fifteen dollars per square foot for abatement labor, containment, and disposal alone. For a typical single-family home with fifteen hundred to twenty-five hundred square feet of exterior wall area, total removal expenses generally range from eight thousand to twenty-two thousand dollars. In major metropolitan markets with elevated prevailing wages and strict municipal disposal tariffs, pricing can exceed eighteen dollars per square foot.

Multiple variables influence the overall financial scope of an exterior siding abatement project. Multi-story homes requiring extensive scaffolding, staging, or boom lifts incur higher labor overhead than single-story ranch houses. In addition, contractor mobilization, site containment using heavy polyethylene ground drop cloths, continuous misting with surfactant-amended water, and the painstaking manual unfastening of each shingle to prevent cracking all contribute to labor expenses. Encapsulation vs. Complete Removal: Comparative Economic and Risk Analysis

When confronted with the substantial expense of stripping asbestos siding, many property owners evaluate exterior encapsulation as an alternative strategy. Encapsulation typically involves covering the existing transite shingles with high-density rigid foam insulation board and installing modern vinyl, fiber cement, or engineered wood siding directly over the top. This approach circumvents the heavy labor, hazardous waste fees, and disposal permits associated with removal, typically reducing upfront costs by forty to sixty percent.

However, encapsulation carries long-term operational and economic trade-offs. Siding over asbestos leaves hazardous materials embedded in the building envelope, which must be formally disclosed on real estate transfer documents and can deter prospective home buyers. Furthermore, any subsequent exterior carpentry, window replacements, or electrical upgrades will require drilling into or penetrating the underlying asbestos shingles, triggering specialized contractor requirements. In contrast, complete removal permanently eliminates toxic liability, ensures unencumbered property value, and simplifies future structural remodeling.

Regulatory Permitting, Disposal Tipping Fees, and Contractor Selection

Disposing of transite siding requires compliance with federal NESHAP standards and state hazardous solid waste regulations. Because transite is an asbestos-cement composite, it is classified as Category II non-friable material under federal rules. However, if shingles are dropped, broken, snapped, or crushed during removal, they are legally reclassified as Regulated Asbestos-Containing Material (RACM), triggering stringent containment and disposal mandates. Abatement teams must wet the shingles continuously, place them into lined containers or double-wrap them in six-mil polyethylene, and haul them using certified transporters.

Contractor vetting is critical when budgeting for siding remediation. Property owners should request detailed bids itemizing labor, containment, disposal manifests, and third-party air monitoring fees. The chosen contractor must carry dedicated pollution liability insurance with specific coverage for asbestos operations. Reputable firms will never propose using power washers or high-speed circular saws on asbestos shingles, as high-pressure water and power abrasives release immense clouds of toxic fibers into surrounding neighborhoods.

How to Plan and Budget for Asbestos Siding Removal

A step-by-step workflow for calculating project expenses, vetting contractors, and executing safe exterior asbestos siding removal.

  1. Calculate Exterior Wall Square Footage Accurately

    Measure the total exterior surface area of the building, deducting major window and door openings to establish exact net square footage.

  2. Commission Laboratory Confirmation Testing

    Have a licensed inspector collect a small, unweathered corner sample of siding to confirm asbestos percentage via Polarized Light Microscopy.

  3. Solicit Multiple Itemized Abatement Proposals

    Obtain at least three competitive bids from certified asbestos contractors that clearly itemize labor, site containment, disposal fees, and permitting costs.

  4. Compare Complete Removal with Encapsulation Options

    Evaluate whether your long-term property plans, budget, and local real estate dynamics favor full hazardous removal or installing over-siding.

  5. Verify Landfill Disposal Manifests and Final Clearances

    Ensure the contractor supplies a signed Waste Shipment Record from a certified landfill and an environmental air clearance report upon completion.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can homeowners legally remove asbestos siding themselves?

In many states, single-family homeowners can legally remove exterior siding, but environmental agencies strongly advise against it due to severe airborne fiber risks.

Q2: How much does it cost per square foot to remove asbestos siding?

Professional removal typically costs between seven and fifteen dollars per square foot, covering labor, ground containment, hauling, and certified landfill fees.

Q3: Is it cheaper to side over asbestos rather than remove it?

Yes, encapsulating asbestos siding with foam backer board and new vinyl siding typically costs forty to fifty percent less than complete removal.

Q4: Does homeowners insurance pay for asbestos siding removal?

Standard homeowners insurance policies exclude hazardous material abatement unless the siding was damaged by a covered peril, such as a major tree fall or storm.

Q5: How long does it take to remove asbestos siding from an average house?

A professional crew of four to six certified technicians can typically remove, package, and clean up exterior siding on an average home in three to five working days.

Q6: Can you power wash asbestos siding before painting?

No, power washing asbestos siding is illegal under federal and state environmental regulations because high-pressure water blasts dislodge dangerous fibers into the air.

Q7: What replacement siding options are best after asbestos removal?

Popular modern replacements include fiber-cement lap siding (such as James Hardie), insulated vinyl siding, engineered wood panels, and traditional cedar cladding.

Q8: Where is asbestos siding taken for final disposal?

Asbestos siding must be transported in lined containers to an EPA-permitted Subtitle D municipal solid waste landfill approved to operate dedicated asbestos cells.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

While the initial cost to remove asbestos siding represents a substantial financial investment, completely eradicating toxic exterior shingles permanently removes legal liabilities and protects household health. By weighing the immediate savings of encapsulation against the enduring market value of full removal, and by retaining licensed abatement professionals, property owners make sound, safe decisions for their properties.
