Can I Sell My Half of a Jointly Owned Property?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, you can legally sell your half of a jointly owned property without the consent of the other owner if you hold title as Tenants in Common or Joint Tenants. However, while you have the legal right to sell your fractional ownership interest, finding an outside buyer willing to purchase half a house is difficult, making co-owner buyouts or a court-ordered partition action the most practical solutions.

How Property Title Dictates Your Right to Sell

Co-owning real estate with a sibling, romantic partner, business associate, or family member often begins with shared enthusiasm. Over time, however, differing financial priorities, marital divorces, inheritance disputes, or lifestyle changes frequently cause one co-owner to desire immediate liquidity while the other refuses to sell. In such contentious situations, the central legal question arises: do you have the legal power to sell your fractional share of the property independently?

The answer is governed strictly by the legal form of concurrent ownership recorded on the official deed in the county land records office. In American real property jurisprudence, there are three primary forms of joint ownership: Tenancy in Common (TIC), Joint Tenancy with Right of Survivorship (JTWROS), and Tenancy by the Entirety (TBE). Each form carries drastically different legal rights regarding independent alienation and sale.

If you hold title as Tenants in Common, you own an undivided fractional percentage (such as 50 percent) that you have an absolute statutory right to sell, mortgage, gift, or bequeath to anyone you choose without the permission or knowledge of your co-owner. If you hold title as Joint Tenants, transferring your deed to an outside party automatically severs the joint tenancy, converting it into a tenancy in common. However, if you are married and own property as Tenants by the Entirety, neither spouse can sell their share independently without the other spouse's signature. The table below delineates the legal rules governing each property ownership classification.

Concurrent Ownership Form Can You Sell Your Half Independently? Consent of Co-Owner Required? Legal Effect of Selling Your Share
Tenancy in Common (TIC) Yes (Absolute statutory right to alienate) No consent required whatsoever Buyer becomes new Tenant in Common with remaining owner
Joint Tenancy with Survivorship (JTWROS) Yes (Right to convey fractional interest) No consent required whatsoever Destroys right of survivorship; converts title to TIC
Tenancy by the Entirety (TBE) No (Strictly barred by law) Yes (Both spouses must sign jointly) Attempted individual conveyance is legally null and void

The Practical Reality: Who Buys Half a House?

While real estate law grants you the theoretical right to sell your 50 percent ownership share to a third party, practical market dynamics introduce severe economic obstacles. Ordinary homebuyers shopping for a residential home will never purchase a 50 percent fractional share of a single-family house because doing so would require sharing the kitchen, bedrooms, and living spaces with a complete stranger who refuses to move out.

The only outside buyers who purchase fractional shares of residential real estate are specialized real estate investors, cash wholesalers, or private equity firms. These institutional buyers understand the legal system and exploit your co-owner's deadlock. When an investor buys a 50 percent share, they purchase it at an extreme discount—often paying only 25 to 40 cents on the dollar compared to full market value.

Once the investor acquires your fractional deed, they immediately file a coercive lawsuit against your former co-owner to force an eviction and sale, capturing huge profits. Consequently, selling your half directly to an outside investor should be viewed as a last-resort option. Far more profitable paths involve negotiating a private co-owner buyout or pursuing a structured judicial partition action. The Legal Nuclear Option: Filing a Partition Lawsuit

When your co-owner categorically refuses to sell the property, cannot qualify for a mortgage to buy you out, and rejects all reasonable compromise, you possess an absolute statutory remedy known as a Partition Action.

A partition action is a formal civil lawsuit filed in the county court where the real estate is located. Under state property codes across all fifty states, any tenant in common or joint tenant has an absolute legal right to demand the partition of real property. Because a residential single-family home cannot be physically divided down the middle with a chainsaw (partition in kind), the court will order a Partition by Sale.

In a partition by sale, the judge appoints a neutral court referee or orders the local sheriff to sell the entire property at a public auction or through a court-approved licensed real estate broker. The proceeds of the sale are collected by the court, outstanding mortgage balances and court costs are paid off, and the remaining net cash proceeds are divided equally between the co-owners according to their ownership percentages, adjusted for any unequal property tax payments or maintenance contributions.

How to Liquidate Your Share of Jointly Owned Real Estate

Step-by-step roadmap to successfully sell or liquidate a 50% interest in co-owned real estate.

  1. Examine County Deed and Ownership Title

    Obtain a certified copy of your property deed from the county recorder's office to confirm whether you hold title as Tenants in Common or Joint Tenants.

  2. Commission an Independent Certified Real Estate Appraisal

    Hire an independent licensed real estate appraiser to establish the objective fair market value and calculate exact net equity after mortgage balances.

  3. Propose a Formal Co-Owner Equity Buyout

    Send a written settlement offer giving your co-owner 30 to 60 days to refinance the existing mortgage and pay you 50% of the net equity in cash.

  4. File a Partition Lawsuit in County Court

    If the co-owner refuses to buy you out or sell mutually, retain a real estate litigator to file a partition by sale action to force a court-supervised sale.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sell a jointly owned house without the other owner's signature?

You cannot sell the entire house without their signature, but you can legally sell your undivided 50 percent ownership share.

Q2: Can my co-owner stop me from selling my half?

No. If you are Tenants in Common or Joint Tenants, your co-owner has no legal power to block you from conveying your fractional share.

Q3: What is a partition by sale?

A court-ordered process where a judge forces the sale of an entire co-owned property on the open market and distributes the cash proceeds among owners.

Q4: Who pays the mortgage if one co-owner leaves the house?

Both borrowers named on the promissory note remain 100 percent liable to the lender; unpaid payments damage the credit scores of both owners.

Q5: Can a married person sell their half of a home independently?

Generally no. Homes owned by spouses as Tenants by the Entirety or community property require both spouses to sign any conveyance deed.

Q6: What is an accounting in a partition lawsuit?

A judicial audit where the court credits a co-owner who paid extra for mortgage payments, property taxes, or necessary structural repairs from the final sale proceeds.

Q7: How much does a partition lawsuit cost?

Legal fees for a partition action typically range between $5,000 and $20,000, but these fees are commonly reimbursed directly out of the property's gross sale proceeds.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

You have the legal right to sell your half of a jointly owned property if title is held as Tenants in Common or Joint Tenants. However, because selling a fractional interest to outside investors results in massive financial discounts, the smartest strategy is negotiating an amicable buyout or mutual listing on the open market. If cooperation fails, exercising your statutory right to a court-ordered partition action ensures you successfully liquidate your equity at true fair market value.
