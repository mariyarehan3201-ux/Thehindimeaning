Remove Asbestos Siding

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Asbestos-cement siding shingles—frequently referred to by the historical trade name Transite—were among the most popular exterior claddings installed on North American residential homes and agricultural buildings from the 1930s through the 1970s. Valued by mid-century builders for their fire resistance, pest immunity, and structural rigidity, these shingles consist of a dense composite containing ten to thirty percent chrysotile asbestos embedded within Portland cement. While transite siding is classified as non-friable when intact, undertaking structural removal releases hazardous airborne fibers whenever shingles are cracked, drilled, sawed, or broken. Safely planning how to remove asbestos siding requires understanding regulatory mandates, proper non-destructive dismantling techniques, personal protective equipment, and environmentally responsible disposal protocols.

Understanding Transite Siding Properties and Exposure Hazards

Transite exterior siding shingles are readily identifiable by their dense, cement-like texture, pressed faux-wood grain or striated surface patterns, wavy or straight bottom edges, and visible securing nails along their bottom margins. Because the asbestos fibers are locked within a Portland cement matrix, intact siding affixed to an exterior wall poses virtually no airborne health hazard to building occupants. However, when homeowners or untrained contractors attempt removal using crowbars, hammer strikes, power saws, or pneumatic nail pullers, the brittle shingles shatter, releasing sharp mineral dust into the surrounding breathing zone.

Regulatory agencies such as OSHA and the EPA classify intact asbestos siding as Category II non-friable material. This classification changes instantly to friable regulated asbestos-containing material (RACM) if the siding is subjected to crushing, grinding, mechanical abrasion, or violent demolition. When fibers are pulverized into breathable dust, wind currents disperse them across residential yards, contaminating landscaping, open window frames, and neighboring properties. Safe removal hinges upon extracting each individual shingle whole and uncompromised.

Removal vs. Siding Over: Evaluating Encapsulation Alternatives

Before embarking on an arduous, costly exterior siding removal project, property owners should carefully evaluate whether complete removal is necessary or if siding over is a superior alternative. Siding over—often termed encapsulation—is an EPA-accepted and building-code-compliant strategy. In this process, contractors install rigid foam insulation board directly over the existing transite shingles and secure new vinyl, fiber-cement, or engineered wood cladding on top. This approach completely encapsulates the asbestos, adds superior thermal insulation (R-value) to the building envelope, avoids hazardous waste disposal fees, and eliminates labor-intensive abatement.

However, complete removal becomes unavoidable under specific architectural conditions. If extensive sections of transite shingles are already broken, missing, or compromised by rot in the underlying wood sheathing, adding new siding over an unstable base is structurally unfeasible. Furthermore, complete removal is required if the building is undergoing full structural demolition, exterior wall reconfiguration, or window and door rough opening resizing. When removal is chosen, precise dismantling methodology must be deployed.

Systematic Removal Methodology, PPE, and Waste Handling

Executing the physical removal of asbestos siding requires methodical patience and strict adherence to wet methods. The work area perimeter must be demarcated with caution tape, and wide six-mil polyethylene drop cloths must be laid on the ground extending at least ten feet out from the foundation to catch any incidental debris. Workers must equip themselves with NIOSH-certified half-face elastomeric respirators equipped with P100 HEPA filters, disposable Tyvek coveralls, safety glasses, and puncture-resistant work gloves. All power saws, grinders, pressure washers, and dry brushes are strictly prohibited on the job site.

To detach each shingle without cracking it, workers spray the shingle generously with water amended with liquid surfactant to suppress dust. Rather than prying against the brittle face of the shingle with a crowbar, technicians slide a specialty nail puller, shingle ripper, or end-cutting nippers beneath the overlapping shingle to cleanly snip or extract the nail shank. Each loosened shingle is lowered carefully to the ground—never thrown, dropped, or tossed into a dumpster. Shingles are stacked directly onto heavy-duty six-mil poly sheeting, wrapped airtight, sealed with duct tape, and labeled with OSHA hazardous material warning placards prior to transport.

How to Remove Asbestos Siding Safely and Compliantly

Follow these five operational steps to dismantle, package, and dispose of exterior transite siding without creating airborne hazards.

  1. Establish Perimeter Containment and PPE Protocols

    Seal all adjacent home windows, drape six-mil plastic drop cloths along the foundation, and don P100 respirators and Tyvek suits.

  2. Apply Continuous Wetting with Amended Water

    Thoroughly mist siding shingles with a garden sprayer filled with water and surfactant to prevent microscopic fiber aerosolization.

  3. Extract Fasteners Using Non-Destructive Techniques

    Slide a shingle ripper under overlapping courses to sever nail shanks, gently releasing each intact shingle without fracture.

  4. Package Shingles in Labeled Airtight Poly Bundles

    Stack shingles directly onto heavy-duty plastic sheets, double-wrap each bundle in six-mil poly, and seal with industrial duct tape.

  5. Transport Waste to a Certified Asbestos Landfill

    Deliver sealed bundles accompanied by formal waste shipment manifests to an EPA-approved solid waste disposal facility.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can a homeowner legally remove asbestos siding themselves?

In many states, single-family homeowners are legally permitted to remove asbestos siding from their primary residence, though municipal notifications, permit rules, and hazardous waste disposal laws still apply.

Q2: Is it better to remove asbestos siding or side over it?

Siding over (encapsulation) with foam backerboard and new vinyl or fiber-cement siding is generally safer, faster, and significantly more cost-effective than complete removal if the existing siding is intact.

Q3: How much does it cost to have asbestos siding professionally removed?

Professional siding abatement typically ranges from $4 to $9 per square foot of wall area, meaning an average 1,500-square-foot home costs between $5,000 and $12,000 for removal and disposal.

Q4: Can you pressure wash asbestos siding to prepare it for painting?

No, never pressure wash asbestos siding. High-pressure water streams pulverize the cement matrix, blasting millions of friable fibers into the surrounding air, soil, and neighboring properties.

Q5: What respirator is required when removing asbestos siding?

Workers must wear a NIOSH-approved respirator equipped with P100 particulate filters (such as a half-mask elastomeric or powered air-purifying respirator). Standard N95 dust masks are insufficient.

Q6: How can you tell if exterior siding shingles contain asbestos?

Siding shingles installed before 1978 measuring roughly 12 by 24 inches with a hard, brittle cement-like texture and embossed grain almost always contain asbestos, verifiable via PLM lab analysis.

Q7: Where do you dispose of removed asbestos siding?

Asbestos siding cannot be discarded in curbside trash or standard construction dumpsters; it must be delivered to a licensed landfill authorized to accept non-friable asbestos with formal manifests.

Q8: Will broken transite shingles contaminate my yard soil?

If shattered dry, fragments can contaminate surrounding soil. If breakage occurs, mist the debris immediately, hand-pick larger pieces, and HEPA vacuum surface soil while wearing protective gear.
