What to Expect When Repiping a House?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

When repiping a house, you can expect a comprehensive 2-to-5-day plumbing overhaul where technicians shut off main water during daytime work hours, cut precise drywall access incisions behind fixtures, replace corroded galvanized or polybutylene pipes with modern PEX or copper, pass city plumbing inspections, and patch walls back to pristine condition.

Why Homes Require a Whole-House Repipe

For most homeowners, residential plumbing pipes remain completely out of sight and out of mind behind painted drywall, ceiling joists, and concrete foundations. However, as residential properties pass 30, 40, or 50 years of age, original plumbing supply lines inevitably succumb to chemical corrosion, mineral scaling, and material degradation. Understanding what to expect when undertaking a complete whole-house repipe transforms an intimidating home improvement project into a structured, predictable process.

Older homes built before the late 1970s predominantly utilized galvanized steel supply lines. Over decades, water chemistry corrodes the interior zinc lining of galvanized steel, causing heavy rust tubercles that restrict water pressure down to a trickle, produce discolored brown or metallic-tasting tap water, and trigger dangerous pinhole leaks inside enclosed walls. Similarly, homes built between 1978 and 1995 often contain defective Polybutylene (Quest) plastic piping, which reacts catastrophically with municipal water disinfectants (chlorine), resulting in sudden structural pipe wall fractures and multi-room flooding.

A whole-house repiping project completely replaces your incoming cold and hot water delivery network from the municipal water meter or well pressure tank all the way to every sink faucet, showerhead, toilet supply valve, and outdoor hose bib. The table below outlines the primary pipe materials replaced during repiping and modern replacement options.

Pipe Material Type Historical Era Used Primary Failure Mechanism Modern Replacement Recommendation
Galvanized Steel 1900 to late 1970s Internal rust buildup, severe scale, pinhole leaks PEX-A (Expansion) or Type L Rigid Copper
Polybutylene (Quest) 1978 to mid-1990s Micro-fractures from municipal chlorine reaction PEX-A or PEX-B Flexible Tubing
Aging Rigid Copper 1960s to Present Acidic water pitting corrosion, slab leaks under concrete PEX-A homerun manifold system overhead
CPVC Plastic 1980s to Present Severe thermal embrittlement; cracks during repairs PEX-A flexible piping throughout

The Step-by-Step Repiping Process and Timeline

A professional whole-house repiping project is a highly choreographed engineering operation executed by specialized plumbing crews. For an average 1,800 to 2,500 square-foot residential home with two to three bathrooms, the active plumbing phase typically spans two to three business days, followed by another one to two days for drywall patching and texturing.

The process begins on Day One with comprehensive protective site preparation. Technicians lay heavy drop cloths and plastic sheeting over hardwood flooring, carpets, and furniture. Next, the master plumbing crew shuts off the main water valve at the street meter, drains all residual water from the system, and begins cutting small, surgical drywall access panels (typically 6-by-6 or 12-by-12 inches) behind bathroom vanities, shower enclosures, and kitchen cabinets.

Unlike invasive legacy methods, modern plumbers utilize flexible Cross-Linked Polyethylene (PEX) tubing. PEX can be snaked through existing wall studs, ceiling joists, and attic cavities with minimal drywall intrusion, completely eliminating the need to tear down entire walls. Water is restored at the end of each working day by connecting temporary bridge fittings, ensuring your family has running water every evening. Day 2: Fixture Branch Routing Connecting PEX lines to sinks, showers, toilets, and laundry Water off during work hours; temporary evening restoration All new shutoff quarter-turn stop valves installed

PEX vs. Copper: Selecting Your New Piping Material

When planning a residential repiping project, homeowners face an essential material decision: modern PEX tubing or traditional rigid copper pipes. Both materials meet building code standards, but their physical characteristics, installation costs, and long-term durability diverge significantly.

PEX (particularly PEX-A expansion tubing) has captured over 80 percent of the residential repiping market. Because PEX is completely flexible, it requires 70 percent fewer fitting connections than rigid pipes, drastically reducing potential leak points. Furthermore, PEX is 100 percent immune to acidic water pitting corrosion, resists mineral scale buildup, will not burst if subjected to moderate freezing temperatures due to its elastic expansion capability, and costs 30 to 45 percent less than copper. Rigid copper remains an elite, durable choice with natural antimicrobial properties, but its vulnerability to acidic water pinhole leaks and high material costs make PEX the superior modern investment.

How to Prepare Your Home for a Whole-House Repipe

Step-by-step homeowner preparation checklist to ensure a smooth, efficient residential repiping project.

  1. Clear Access to All Plumbing Fixtures and Cabinets

    Empty vanity cabinets beneath all bathroom and kitchen sinks, and clear a 3-foot perimeter around your water heater, washer, and attic access hatch.

  2. Store Clean Drinking and Sanitation Water

    Fill several 5-gallon jugs for drinking and fill a bathtub with water to manually flush toilets during daytime water shutoff hours.

  3. Verify Contractor Secures Municipal Building Permits

    Confirm your plumbing contractor pulls an official city plumbing permit and schedules a certified inspector to review pressure testing.

  4. Conduct Final Pressure and Fixture Walkthrough

    Inspect every faucet, showerhead, and outdoor hose bib with the project lead, ensuring quarter-turn ball valves function smoothly with zero leaks.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: How long does it take to repipe a house?

A standard whole-house repipe takes 2 to 3 days for active plumbing installation, followed by 1 to 2 days for drywall patching and texturing.

Q2: Can I stay in my house while it is being repiped?

Yes. Water is shut off only during daytime working hours (typically 8:30 AM to 4:30 PM) and restored every evening for normal nighttime use.

Q3: How much does it cost to repipe a house?

Repiping an average single-family home typically costs between $4,500 and $10,000 using PEX, or $9,000 to $18,000 using rigid copper.

Q4: Do repiping plumbers repair the holes in the drywall?

Most reputable turn-key repiping specialists include full drywall patching, taping, mudding, and texture matching in their written contract.

Q5: What is the difference between PEX-A and PEX-B?

PEX-A is manufactured via peroxide method offering maximum flexibility and memory expansion; PEX-B is stiffer and uses crimp ring fittings.

Q6: Will repiping increase my home's resale value?

Yes. Replacing old galvanized or defective polybutylene pipes removes a major buyer objection, passes home inspections, and lowers home insurance rates.

Q7: How long does new PEX piping last?

Modern PEX piping has an engineered design life of over 50 years and typically includes a 25-year manufacturer warranty.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Repiping a house is a substantial structural upgrade that restores pristine water pressure, eliminates toxic corrosion, and protects your home against catastrophic water damage. By understanding the daily project timeline, choosing high-performance PEX-A piping, and ensuring your contractor secures municipal permits and handles seamless drywall restoration, you can navigate a whole-house repipe smoothly and add significant lasting equity to your home.
