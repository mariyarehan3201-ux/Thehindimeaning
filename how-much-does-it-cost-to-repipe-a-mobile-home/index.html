How Much Does It Cost to Repipe a Mobile Home?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Repiping a mobile home or manufactured home typically costs between $1,800 and $5,000 for a standard single-wide or double-wide dwelling, with most homeowners spending an average of approximately $2,800 to $3,800 for a complete whole-house overhaul using modern PEX piping. Unlike site-built single-family residences where water supply lines are routed through drywall stud bays, overhead attics, or concrete foundation slabs, mobile home plumbing is uniquely configured within the narrow underbelly crawlspace beneath the subfloor deck. Because millions of manufactured homes constructed between the late 1970s and mid-1990s were fitted with notorious polybutylene piping that suffers from catastrophic micro-fractures, repiping has become an essential home maintenance project to prevent devastating floor rot, mold growth, and water damage.

Mobile Home Repiping Cost Breakdown: Single-Wide vs Double-Wide Homes

The total investment required to repipe a manufactured or mobile home is primarily dictated by the physical size of the structure, the total number of plumbing fixtures, and the configuration of the home. Manufactured homes are constructed in factory-controlled environments using standardized modular dimensions, categorized primarily as single-wide units (typically 600 to 1,200 square feet) or double-wide units (ranging from 1,200 to 2,400 square feet). In a single-wide home featuring one or two bathrooms, a kitchen sink, a water heater, and a washing machine connection (typically six to eight total plumbing fixtures), repiping costs generally range from $1,800 to $3,200. The plumbing runs in single-wides are relatively short and linear, often running in a straight line beneath the floor deck directly to the utility room and bathrooms.

Double-wide and triple-wide manufactured homes require a larger financial investment, with repiping expenses typically ranging from $2,800 to $5,200. Double-wide homes feature two distinct chassis joined together along a central marriage line. This layout requires routing cross-over plumbing lines beneath the subfloor to deliver hot and cold potable water from the central water heater and supply main across to the opposite side of the home. Furthermore, double-wide layouts often feature two to three full bathrooms, separate garden tubs, kitchen islands with secondary prep sinks, and outdoor hose bibbs, expanding the total fixture count to ten to fourteen fixtures. Homeowners comparing manufactured home plumbing costs against traditional stick-built houses often ask how much does it cost to repipe a house or evaluate project schedules like how long to repipe a house, discovering that mobile homes are generally faster and less expensive to repipe because plumbers do not have to cut open extensive drywall walls.

Additional cost variables include accessibility to the underbelly, the condition of the home's water heater, and the need for new shut-off valves. If the main water shut-off valve, pressure regulator, or individual fixture angle stops are corroded, plumbers will install new quarter-turn brass ball valves during the repiping process. Upgrading to a modern whole-house water filtration system or replacing an aged water heater while the water supply lines are disconnected adds $800 to $1,800 to the total invoice, but prevents future service calls.

The Polybutylene Crisis and Why Modern PEX Tubing Is the Premier Solution

The primary catalyst driving mobile home repiping projects across North America is the presence of obsolete polybutylene (PB) piping. Manufactured under trade names such as Quest and heavily utilized between 1978 and 1995, polybutylene is a flexible grey or blue plastic tubing that was installed in tens of millions of homes due to its low material cost and rapid installation speed. However, chemical oxidants present in municipal treated municipal drinking water—specifically chlorine and chloramines—react chemically with polybutylene polymers and acetal plastic insert fittings. Over five to twenty years of exposure, this chemical reaction causes microscopic scaling, micro-fracturing, and embrittlement throughout the inner pipe walls. Eventually, the weakened pipes burst without warning, causing catastrophic underbelly flooding, saturated fiberglass insulation, collapsed subflooring, and extensive mold growth.

Insurance underwriting policies have made polybutylene pipe replacement an urgent financial necessity. The vast majority of homeowners insurance companies now strictly refuse to write new property policies, or refuse to renew existing coverage, on manufactured homes fitted with polybutylene plumbing. Even if an insurer agrees to maintain a policy, they frequently insert strict water damage exclusion endorsements that deny all coverage for burst pipe losses. Replacing polybutylene with modern plumbing materials is often the only way mobile home owners can secure hazard insurance, obtain mortgage refinancing, or sell their property on the open real estate market.

When repiping a manufactured home, cross-linked polyethylene, universally known as PEX tubing, is the gold standard material utilized by licensed plumbing professionals. PEX offers unmatched flexibility, allowing technicians to snake seamless lines through tight crawlspaces, around chassis I-beams, and through subfloor penetrations with minimal mechanical joints. Furthermore, PEX is completely impervious to chlorine degradation, resists mineral scale buildup, and can expand slightly during mild freezing events without splitting. When evaluating material costs, reviewing benchmarks such as the average cost to repipe a house with PEX demonstrates why PEX delivers the optimal balance of material affordability, mechanical longevity, and labor efficiency.

Unique Underbelly Challenges: Insulation, Belly Wrap, and Labor Dynamics

What distinguishes a mobile home repipe from a traditional site-built house is the unique structural environment known as the manufactured home underbelly. Beneath the wooden floor decking lies a dense layer of loose-fill or batt fiberglass insulation, supported by a heavy-duty black polyethylene or woven asphaltic fabric sheeting commonly referred to as the belly wrap or bottom board. This underbelly envelope serves as a crucial thermal and vapor barrier, protecting the living area from cold ground drafts, moisture intrusion, and rodent infestation. When repiping a mobile home, plumbers must spend hours working in extremely cramped crawlspaces, lying on their backs beneath the chassis steel I-beams.

To access the water supply lines, technicians must carefully slit the bottom board along strategic seams to expose the water trunks. If previous leaks have occurred, the plumbers frequently discover saturated fiberglass insulation that has trapped gallons of standing water against the floor joists. In such instances, water-logged insulation must be removed, the undercarriage allowed to dry, and fresh insulation batts installed. Once the new PEX water lines are secured and pressure-tested, the plumber must meticulously seal every incision in the belly wrap using specialized high-adhesion bottom board flex tape or spray adhesive patches. Failing to patch the underbelly wrap properly leaves the home vulnerable to freezing pipes in the winter and animal intrusions.

Plumbers utilize two primary plumbing architectural strategies when repiping a manufactured home: trunk-and-branch systems or home-run manifold systems. In a traditional trunk-and-branch layout, a large three-quarter-inch main line runs down the center of the chassis, with half-inch branch tees feeding individual fixtures. In a home-run manifold configuration, a central brass or poly manifold is mounted near the water heater, and dedicated individual half-inch PEX lines run directly to each sink, shower, and toilet without underground fittings. Manifold systems eliminate subterranean connection joints entirely, minimizing leak risks and providing individual fixture shut-offs from a central utility panel.

How to Plan and Complete a Mobile Home Repiping Project

Follow this practical step-by-step homeowner protocol to evaluate existing plumbing lines, hire qualified manufactured home plumbers, and oversee a professional PEX repipe.

  1. Inspect Existing Pipes for Polybutylene and Active Leaks

    Check the plumbing connections beneath your kitchen sink, behind the water heater, and near the toilet shut-off valves. Look for dull grey flexible tubing stamped with 'PB2110' indicating failure-prone polybutylene piping.

  2. Solicit Detailed Estimates from Manufactured Home Specialists

    Contact three licensed plumbing contractors who specifically specialize in mobile and manufactured home plumbing. Ensure their written quotes include underbelly crawlspace access, insulation restoration, and bottom board resealing.

  3. Select Modern PEX Piping and Plan Manifold Layouts

    Review proposed piping materials with your contractor, ensuring they use commercial-grade PEX-A or PEX-B tubing. Decide between a central manifold home-run system or a traditional trunk-and-branch layout with brass shut-off valves.

  4. Perform Hydrostatic Pressure Testing Before Sealing Underbelly

    Once all new PEX water supply lines and fittings are connected, have the plumber pressurize the system with air or water to verify that zero pressure drops or fitting leaks exist throughout the system.

  5. Inspect Underbelly Insulation and Bottom Board Patching

    Verify that any damp or compressed insulation was replaced with dry fiberglass, and ensure that all cuts in the underbelly vapor barrier are permanently sealed with commercial bottom board seam tape.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How much does it cost to repipe a mobile home on average?

On average, repiping a mobile home costs between $2,000 and $4,500. A smaller single-wide mobile home typically costs $1,800 to $3,200, while a larger double-wide or triple-wide home ranges from $2,800 to $5,200 depending on fixture count and underbelly accessibility.

Q2: Why is PEX the best plumbing material for mobile homes?

PEX is flexible, durable, highly resistant to chlorine degradation, and expands slightly during freezing temperatures without splitting. Its flexibility allows plumbers to snake lines through tight crawlspaces with minimal joints, making it the premier choice for manufactured homes.

Q3: How can I tell if my mobile home has polybutylene pipes?

Polybutylene pipes are typically dull grey, blue, or black flexible plastic tubing, often stamped with the code 'PB2110'. They are connected with plastic or copper crimp rings and are commonly found in homes built between 1978 and 1995.

Q4: Will insurance companies cover a mobile home with polybutylene pipes?

Many major insurance carriers refuse to issue new policies or renew existing coverage on mobile homes with polybutylene plumbing due to extremely high leak risks. If coverage is offered, it often includes strict endorsements excluding all water damage claims.

Q5: How long does it take to repipe a mobile home?

A complete whole-house repipe on a mobile home typically takes 1 to 2 working days. Single-wide homes can often be completed in a single day, while larger double-wide homes with multiple bathrooms may require two days to complete and test.

Q6: Can I repipe a mobile home myself as a DIY project?

While DIY repiping is possible with push-fit or crimp PEX fittings, it is physically demanding and technically challenging. Working in tight crawlspaces, handling insulation, navigating chassis I-beams, and sealing vapor barriers properly makes professional plumbing installation strongly recommended.

Q7: Do plumbers have to tear open drywall to repipe a mobile home?

Rarely. In manufactured homes, almost all water supply lines run through the underbelly crawlspace beneath the floor. Plumbers route new PEX pipes up through small holes in the floor directly beneath cabinets and behind toilets, avoiding drywall demolition.

Q8: What is the underbelly bottom board and why is it important?

The bottom board (or belly wrap) is a heavy-duty woven polyethylene vapor barrier that encloses the insulation and plumbing beneath a mobile home. Properly taping and resealing the bottom board after repiping prevents drafts, moisture buildup, frozen pipes, and pest infestations.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Investing in a complete mobile home repipe is one of the most effective ways to preserve your property equity, prevent devastating water damage, and eliminate insurance cancellation headaches. With typical whole-home PEX replacement costs averaging between $2,000 and $4,500, repiping is substantially more affordable than repairing rotted subfloors and structural joists following a catastrophic polybutylene pipe failure. By selecting high-quality PEX-A or PEX-B piping, hiring licensed plumbing professionals experienced in manufactured home underbelly dynamics, and insisting on professional bottom board resealing, you can ensure dependable water pressure and worry-free plumbing performance for decades to come.
