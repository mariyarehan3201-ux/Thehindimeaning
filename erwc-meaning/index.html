Defining ERWC: The Expository Reading and Writing Curriculum

In secondary education, college readiness administration, and English Language Arts (ELA) curriculum design, ERWC is an acronym that stands for Expository Reading and Writing Curriculum (frequently referenced as the Expository Reading and Writing Course).

Developed in 2003 by a joint task force of California State University (CSU) English faculty, University of California (UC) educators, and high school teachers, ERWC was created to solve a persistent educational crisis: high numbers of incoming college freshmen requiring remedial, non-credit developmental English courses upon entering university. ERWC is a full-year, college-preparatory English course designed specifically for high school juniors and seniors to develop advanced academic literacy and analytical reading skills.

The A-G Requirement and College Remediation Exemption

A major milestone of ERWC is its official approval under the University of California and California State University A-G High School Articulation Requirements. ERWC satisfies the 'Area B' (English) college preparatory admissions requirement with honors distinction in many school districts.

Furthermore, ERWC is closely integrated with the California Early Assessment Program (EAP) administered via the Smarter Balanced (SBAC) 11th-grade assessments. High school seniors who score as 'Conditionally Ready' on their 11th-grade state tests and subsequently complete ERWC with a grade of 'C' or higher are automatically granted full college-ready English status at all 23 California State University campuses and participating California Community Colleges—completely exempting them from costly remedial placement tests or non-credit foundational classes.

By shifting the focus from interpreting fictional symbolism to dissecting complex informational non-fiction, ERWC equips students to thrive in university lecture halls and professional careers.

How High School Students Master the ERWC Rhetorical Assignment Template

Excel in an ERWC classroom using this standardized assignment sequence.

  1. Engage in Pre-Reading Topic Brainstorming

    Explore personal viewpoints on the module central ethical or social issue before reading the source texts.

  2. Annotate Author Claims and Rhetorical Devices

    Read the text actively with a pen; mark Ethos (credibility), Pathos (emotion), and Logos (logic) throughout the margins.

  3. Summarize and Chart Opposing Arguments

    Complete a rhetorical precis summarizing the author central thesis, intended audience, purpose, and rhetorical tone.

  4. Synthesize Multiple Source Perspectives

    Compare how two or three diverse authors address the identical social dilemma, noting conflicting evidence or unstated assumptions.

  5. Draft and Polish an Evidence-Based Argument

    Write a structured argumentative essay utilizing MLA or APA citations, clearly establishing your own reasoned stance while refuting counterclaims.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What does ERWC stand for?

ERWC stands for Expository Reading and Writing Curriculum (or Course), a college-preparatory English class for high school seniors.

Q2: Who created ERWC?

It was developed by the California State University (CSU) system in collaboration with UC educators and high school English teachers.

Q3: Does ERWC count as a college prep English class?

Yes. ERWC fulfills the University of California and California State University 'Area B' English admission requirement.

Q4: What is the difference between ERWC and AP English?

AP English focuses heavily on literary analysis or AP exam prep; ERWC focuses on practical non-fiction rhetoric, research, and college writing readiness.

Q5: Can ERWC exempt you from college placement tests?

Yes. In California, earning a C or better in ERWC after scoring 'Conditionally Ready' on 11th-grade tests exempts you from CSU remedial English courses.

Q6: What kind of texts do students read in ERWC?

Students read complex non-fiction texts: newspaper editorials, scholarly research essays, legal court opinions, speeches, and public policy articles.

Q7: What are ethos, pathos, and logos in ERWC?

They are the three classical Aristotelian rhetorical appeals: Ethos appeals to credibility, Pathos appeals to emotion, and Logos appeals to logic and evidence.

Q8: Is ERWC only taught in California?

While developed for California schools, the ERWC curriculum has been adopted by high school districts across several other US states.
