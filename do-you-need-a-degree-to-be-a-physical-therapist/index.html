Do You Need a Degree to Be a Physical Therapist?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 12, 2026

Aspiring healthcare professionals drawn to sports medicine, rehabilitation, and biomechanics frequently inquire about the educational credentials required to practice physical therapy. The answer is unequivocal: you cannot practice as a licensed physical therapist in the United States without earning an accredited clinical doctoral degree. Today, all 50 states require aspiring physical therapists to graduate with a Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT) degree and pass a rigorous national licensure examination.

The Evolution of Physical Therapy Education: The DPT Standard

Physical therapy education in the United States has undergone a dramatic academic transition over the past several decades. Historically, clinicians entered the profession by earning a bachelor's degree in physical therapy, which later evolved in the 1990s into a Master of Physical Therapy (MPT). However, under the visionary guidance of the American Physical Therapy Association (APTA), the entire profession shifted to an autonomous doctoral education model by 2015 to match the diagnostic complexity of modern patient care.

Today, the Commission on Accreditation in Physical Therapy Education (CAPTE) exclusively accredits programs that confer the Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT) degree. Bachelor's and master's degree programs in physical therapy no longer exist in the United States. While legacy therapists who earned bachelor's or master's degrees prior to this transition remain legally grandfathered into clinical practice, all new entrants must complete a rigorous post-baccalaureate doctoral curriculum.

The Educational Roadmap: From Bachelor's to Clinical Doctorate

Achieving licensure as a physical therapist requires approximately seven years of rigorous post-secondary education. The journey begins with completing a four-year undergraduate bachelor's degree. While DPT programs do not mandate a specific undergraduate major, most prospective students major in exercise science, kinesiology, biology, or psychology to fulfill intensive prerequisite courses in human anatomy, physiology, physics, chemistry, and statistics.

Following undergraduate graduation, students enroll in a CAPTE-accredited DPT program, which typically requires three intensive calendar years of year-round study. The DPT curriculum blends deep didactic coursework—including biomechanics, neuroscience, pharmacology, differential diagnosis, and musculoskeletal pathology—with at least 30 weeks of full-time clinical rotations across outpatient orthopedics, acute inpatient care, and neuro-rehabilitation facilities.

Licensure, Board Certification, and the PTA Alternative

Graduating with a DPT degree does not automatically grant the legal right to treat patients; candidates must obtain state licensure. Licensure requires passing the National Physical Therapy Examination (NPTE), administered by the Federation of State Boards of Physical Therapy (FSBPT). The NPTE is a comprehensive, five-hour examination evaluating clinical diagnostic reasoning, treatment safety, and therapeutic interventions. Licensed DPTs can subsequently pursue board certification in specialties such as orthopedics, pediatrics, or sports physical therapy.

For individuals passionate about physical rehabilitation who wish to enter the workforce faster without spending seven years in doctoral academia, the Physical Therapist Assistant (PTA) pathway offers a compelling alternative. PTAs complete a two-year Associate of Applied Science degree from a CAPTE-accredited program and pass the national PTA exam. PTAs deliver therapeutic exercises and modalities under the direct supervision of licensed DPTs, earning competitive salaries with minimal educational debt.

How to Become a Licensed Physical Therapist in 5 Steps

Follow these five clear sequential steps to navigate the educational and licensing path to becoming a licensed physical therapist.

  1. Earn a Relevant Bachelor's Degree

    Complete a four-year undergraduate degree in exercise science, kinesiology, or biology while maintaining high prerequisite GPAs in sciences.

  2. Accumulate Clinical Observation Hours

    Complete 100 or more volunteer or technician observation hours across diverse physical therapy settings under licensed DPT clinicians.

  3. Apply to CAPTE-Accredited DPT Programs

    Submit applications through the Physical Therapist Centralized Application Service (PTCAS), providing transcripts, GRE scores, and recommendations.

  4. Graduate from a Doctor of Physical Therapy Program

    Complete the rigorous three-year doctoral curriculum, successfully passing all academic coursework and 30+ weeks of clinical rotations.

  5. Pass the NPTE and Obtain State Licensure

    Register for and pass the National Physical Therapy Examination and complete your state board's jurisprudence exam to receive your clinical license.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I become a physical therapist with just a bachelor's degree?

No, new physical therapists must graduate with an accredited Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT) degree. Bachelor's programs in PT were phased out years ago.

Q2: How long does it take to become a physical therapist?

It typically takes seven years of total higher education: four years for an undergraduate bachelor's degree and three years in an accredited DPT program.

Q3: Is a master's degree in physical therapy still accepted?

New master's programs are no longer offered. However, clinicians who earned an MPT before the DPT mandate was enacted remain fully licensed to practice.

Q4: What is the difference between a PT and a PTA?

A physical therapist (PT) holds a clinical doctorate, evaluates patients, and designs care plans. A PTA holds a two-year associate degree and carries out treatments under PT supervision.

Q5: What undergraduate major is best for physical therapy?

Exercise science, kinesiology, biology, and physiology are the most popular undergraduate majors because their coursework fulfills standard DPT prerequisites.

Q6: Is the NPTE exam difficult to pass?

The National Physical Therapy Exam is rigorous and comprehensive, though first-time pass rates for accredited US DPT graduates typically average between 85% and 92%.

Q7: Can a physical therapist prescribe medication?

In the United States, physical therapists cannot prescribe oral systemic prescription medications, though they utilize prescription topical medications for modalities like iontophoresis.

Q8: Can I practice physical therapy with an online degree?

No 100% online DPT programs exist due to essential hands-on manual therapy requirements, though hybrid programs combining online lectures with in-person lab immersions are CAPTE-accredited.
