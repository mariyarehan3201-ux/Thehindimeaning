What Does Charge the Hydrant Mean?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

In structural firefighting and municipal emergency operations, "Charge the Hydrant" is a direct tactical command issued by a fire engine apparatus pump operator to a firefighter stationed at a municipal fire hydrant. It instructs the hydrant firefighter to fully open the hydrant's operating nut using a specialized pentagonal hydrant wrench, allowing pressurized municipal water to flood through the barrel, enter the large diameter supply hose (LDH), and charge the intake manifold of the fire engine battling a structural blaze.

The Tactical Architecture of Establishing a Continuous Water Supply

When a structural fire engine arrives first on the scene of a working structure fire, its onboard water tank typically holds between 500 and 750 gallons of water. While 500 gallons is sufficient to initiate an immediate initial attack through handlines, an aggressive interior hose line flowing 150 to 200 gallons per minute (GPM) will exhaust that internal tank in less than three minutes. To sustain an uninterrupted interior attack and protect trapped occupants, establishing a continuous municipal water supply is the engine company's highest strategic priority.

The standard operating procedure involves a "Forward Hose Lay." As the engine approaches the fire scene, the hydrant firefighter catches the hydrant, wraps a 4-inch or 5-inch Large Diameter Hose (LDH) around the barrel, and steps down into a secure stance while the engine drives toward the burning structure, unspooling hundreds of feet of heavy rubberized hose behind it. The hydrant firefighter attaches the supply line to the large 4.5-inch steamer port. However, water must never be sent through the line until the pump operator commands it.

Why Hydrants Must Be Opened Fully and Slowly

When the engine pump operator radioes or hand-signals "Charge the Hydrant!" the firefighter must execute the operation following strict mechanical principles. Municipal fire hydrants are typically "Dry-Barrel" models in freezing northern climates, where the main operating valve is buried deep beneath the winter frost line (often six to eight feet underground). Rotating the top operating nut turns a long central steel stem that lowers the main valve gate into the water main.

Firefighters must open the hydrant operating nut completely until it stops—typically requiring 15 to 20 complete revolutions. If a firefighter opens a dry-barrel hydrant only halfway, the subterranean drain holes along the lower barrel remain uncovered. High-pressure water will spray out through these drain openings directly into the surrounding soil bedding, rapidly undermining the earth, creating subterranean sinkholes, and potentially toppling the entire cast-iron hydrant structure.

The Danger of Water Hammer and Pump Transitions

Water is an incompressible fluid. If a firefighter opens or slams shut a high-volume fire hydrant rapidly, the kinetic energy of thousands of pounds of rushing water creates an acoustic pressure shockwave known as "Water Hammer." This hydraulic shockwave can spike pressures inside cast-iron city mains past 1,000 PSI, shattering underground municipal pipes, blowing off hydrants, and causing severe mechanical damage to the fire apparatus's centrifugal pump casing.

Once water reaches the engine, the pump operator bleeds trapped air through a bleeder valve before opening the master intake. Once pressurized municipal water floods the pump, the operator switches the internal tank-to-pump valve, establishing a seamless, continuous water supply capable of flowing 1,500 to 2,000 GPM for hours to suppress five-alarm commercial and residential fires.

How to Safely Dress and Charge a Fire Hydrant

Follow these standardized fire academy operational procedures to dress, flush, and charge a municipal fire hydrant safely.

  1. Place Your Hydrant Bag and Remove the Cap

    Step off the apparatus with your hydrant kit and use the pentagonal wrench to remove the large 4.5-inch steamer nozzle cap.

  2. Flush the Hydrant to Clear Rust and Stones

    Crack the operating nut counter-clockwise for several seconds to blow out accumulated street gravel, rust, and debris before hooking up hose.

  3. Couple the Large Diameter Supply Hose (LDH)

    Attach the 5-inch supply hose coupling or Storz quarter-turn fitting firmly to the steamer port, verifying gasket condition.

  4. Stand Behind the Barrel and Await the Radio Order

    Position yourself safely behind the hydrant barrel away from caps; hold position until the driver commands 'Charge the Hydrant.'

  5. Open the Operating Nut Fully and Smoothly

    Rotate the operating nut counter-clockwise smoothly until it stops completely, ensuring bottom drain ports are sealed to prevent erosion.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What does 'charge the hydrant' mean in firefighting?

It is the order from the engine driver to the hydrant firefighter to open the hydrant valve and send pressurized water through the supply hose.

Q2: What is a dry-barrel fire hydrant?

A dry-barrel hydrant has its operating valve located deep underground below the frost line, keeping water out of the upper barrel to prevent freezing.

Q3: Why must a fire hydrant be opened all the way?

Opening it partially leaves underground drain holes open, causing high-pressure water to wash away the soil foundation and topple the hydrant.

Q4: What is water hammer in firefighting?

Water hammer is a violent pressure surge caused by opening or closing valves too quickly, which can rupture water mains and destroy pumps.

Q5: How much water is inside a standard fire engine tank?

Most structural municipal fire engines carry between 500 and 750 gallons of water in their onboard booster tank, lasting about 3 to 4 minutes.

Q6: What wrench is used to open a fire hydrant?

Firefighters use a specialized five-sided pentagonal hydrant wrench, designed to prevent unauthorized operation with standard civilian tools.

Q7: What size hose connects to the fire hydrant?

Most fire departments use 4-inch or 5-inch Large Diameter Hose (LDH) to connect the hydrant steamer port to the fire engine intake.

Q8: Why do firefighters flush hydrants before connecting?

Flushing blows out rust, rocks, and mud that would otherwise enter the fire engine pump and damage the spinning bronze impellers.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

"Charge the Hydrant" is a foundational firefighting command that triggers the transition from a limited onboard engine tank to an infinite municipal water supply. By fully opening the subterranean valve slowly, flushing debris, and respecting the physics of water hammer, firefighters ensure that attack teams battling interior flames receive a steady, life-saving supply of high-pressure water.
