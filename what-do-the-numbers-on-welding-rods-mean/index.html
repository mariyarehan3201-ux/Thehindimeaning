What Do the Numbers on Welding Rods Mean?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

The alphanumeric codes stamped onto shielded metal arc welding (SMAW) electrodes, such as E6010, E7018, or E6013, follow a standardized classification system established by the American Welding Society (AWS). Far from random factory part numbers, each letter and digit provides critical metallurgical specifications regarding the rod's minimum tensile strength, certified welding positions, chemical flux coating composition, and compatible electrical current types. Deciphering these numbers is essential for structural fabricators, pipe welders, and maintenance technicians to select the proper electrode for sound, crack-resistant welds.

The American Welding Society (AWS) Electrode Identification System

Shielded metal arc welding, commonly known as stick welding, relies on consumable metal electrodes coated in a solid flux mantle. When an electric arc strikes between the electrode tip and the workpiece, the core wire melts into the weld puddle while the flux coating decomposes, generating a protective shielding gas envelope and liquid slag blanket that prevents atmospheric oxygen and nitrogen from embrittling the molten steel. The AWS standardized system ensures that an E7018 rod manufactured in North America performs with identical mechanical properties to one manufactured in Europe or Asia.

Every standard AWS stick electrode code begins with the prefix letter "E," which stands for "Electrode." Following this letter, a four-digit or five-digit sequence conveys three distinct engineering properties: tensile strength, welding position capability, and coating/current chemistry. Understanding this sequence enables welders to determine immediately whether an electrode is suited for heavy overhead structural steel framing or thin sheet metal automotive repairs.

The comparative table below deconstructs the four core elements of the AWS stick electrode numbering system using the industry-standard E7018 electrode as a model.

Code Segment Sample Value (E7018) Technical Specification Engineering Meaning
Prefix Letter E Electrode Classification Identifies consumable as an arc welding stick electrode
First 2 or 3 Digits 70 (or 60, 80, 110) Minimum Tensile Strength 70,000 pounds per square inch (PSI) minimum yield strength
Next-to-Last Digit 1 (or 2, 4) Approved Welding Positions 1 = All positions (Flat, Horizontal, Vertical, Overhead)
Last Digit 8 (combined with '1') Flux Coating & Electrical Current Low-hydrogen iron powder coating; DCEP or AC current
Suffix (Optional) -H4R (e.g., E7018-H4R) Specialty Mechanical Suffix Diffusible hydrogen limit (4mL/100g) and moisture-resistant (R)

Decoding Strength, Position, and Coating Digits

The first two digits (or three digits in high-strength rods) represent the minimum tensile strength of the deposited weld metal measured in thousands of pounds per square inch (PSI). For example, an E6010 rod produces a weld deposit with a minimum tensile strength of 60,000 PSI, whereas an E7018 rod yields 70,000 PSI, and an E11018 rod delivers 110,000 PSI for high-yield military armor plate and heavy construction equipment.

The second-to-last digit defines the spatial welding positions for which the rod is certified. A "1" indicates an all-position electrode capable of flat, horizontal, vertical-up, and overhead welding. A "2" signifies that the rod is restricted to flat groove welds and horizontal fillet welds, typically because it deposits a fluid, high-deposition puddle that would sag and spill out of a vertical joint. A "4" designates a specialty rod optimized for vertical-down welding, common in cross-country pipeline construction.

The final digit, evaluated in conjunction with the positional digit, defines the chemical formulation of the flux coating, the depth of arc penetration, and the compatible electrical currents (AC, DCEP reverse polarity, or DCEN straight polarity). For example, an ending digit of "0" in E6010 denotes a high-cellulose sodium coating providing aggressive, deep arc penetration on DCEP, making it ideal for cutting through rusted pipe and burning through mill scale.

The Critical Importance of Low-Hydrogen Storage

Electrodes ending in "5," "6," or "8"—most notably E7018—are classified as low-hydrogen electrodes. These rods are mandatory in commercial structural steel fabrication and nuclear construction because they prevent hydrogen-induced delayed cracking, a catastrophic failure mode where moisture trapped in the flux releases hydrogen atoms that migrate into the crystalline structure of hardened steel.

Unlike cellulose-coated E6010 rods that require a small percentage of moisture to operate correctly, E7018 electrodes must be kept hermetically sealed until use. Once an airtight factory tin is unsealed, low-hydrogen rods must be stored in an electric rod oven maintained at 250 to 300 degrees Fahrenheit (120 to 150 degrees Celsius) to prevent atmospheric humidity absorption. Rods exposed to ambient air beyond their certified exposure window (typically 4 hours) must be rebaked or discarded.

How to Select the Correct Welding Rod for a Project

Follow these step-by-step engineering guidelines to choose the perfect stick welding electrode for your metal fabrication task.

  1. Identify the Base Metal Type and Thickness

    Determine the composition of the metal (mild steel, low-alloy steel, or cast iron) and measure its thickness to guide rod diameter selection.

  2. Match or Exceed Base Metal Tensile Strength

    Select an electrode whose first two digits match the base metal strength (e.g., use an E70-series rod yielding 70,000 PSI for A36 structural steel).

  3. Evaluate the Required Welding Position

    Ensure the second-to-last digit is '1' if welding vertical or overhead joints, or choose '2' for high-speed flat fillet welds.

  4. Assess Available Welder Polarity (AC vs DC)

    Verify whether your welding machine outputs AC or DC current, matching the final digit (e.g., E6011 for AC machines, E6010 for DC only).

  5. Inspect Surface Cleanliness and Joint Fit-Up

    Use deep-penetrating cellulose rods (E6010/E6011) for rusted steel, or low-hydrogen rods (E7018) for pristine structural joints.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What does E7018 mean on a welding rod?

E stands for Electrode, 70 means 70,000 PSI minimum tensile strength, 1 means all welding positions, and 8 indicates a low-hydrogen iron powder flux coating.

Q2: What is the difference between E6010 and E6011?

E6010 operates only on DC current (DCEP), while E6011 has potassium in its coating allowing it to run smoothly on both AC and DC welders.

Q3: Why is E7018 called a low-hydrogen rod?

Its flux coating contains minimal hydrogen-forming compounds, preventing hydrogen embrittlement and catastrophic delayed cracking in structural steel.

Q4: Can I weld vertical-down with an E7018 rod?

No. E7018 is designed for vertical-up welding; welding vertical-down traps slag ahead of the weld puddle, causing severe slag inclusion defects.

Q5: What is the easiest welding rod for beginners?

E6013 is widely considered the easiest rod for beginners because it strikes and restrikes easily, produces low spatter, and creates a smooth bead.

Q6: Do E7018 welding rods need to be kept in an oven?

Yes. Once opened from their hermetic factory container, E7018 rods must be kept in a heated rod oven at 250°F to prevent absorbing atmospheric moisture.

Q7: What do the numbers 60 and 70 mean on a welding rod?

They indicate minimum tensile strength in thousands of PSI; 60 equals 60,000 PSI yield, while 70 equals 70,000 PSI.

Q8: What welding rod is best for rusty, dirty metal?

E6010 and E6011 have aggressive, digging cellulose arcs that cut through rust, oil, paint, and mill scale better than any other electrodes.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

The numbers stamped on welding rods provide a complete engineering blueprint of their mechanical capabilities. By reading the tensile strength digits, position indicator, and flux chemistry code, a welder can immediately determine whether an electrode is suited for cutting through contaminated pipe or fabricating high-strength, low-hydrogen structural joints. Mastering this classification system ensures every weld deposited meets the highest standards of safety and structural integrity.
