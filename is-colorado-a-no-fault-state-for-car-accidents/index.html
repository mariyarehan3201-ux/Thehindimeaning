Is Colorado a No Fault State for Car Accidents?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

No, Colorado is not a no-fault state for car accidents; it operates strictly under a traditional 'fault-based' or 'tort' insurance system. While Colorado historically operated under a no-fault system that required mandatory Personal Injury Protection (PIP) coverage, the state legislature repealed that law in July 2003 to lower runaway consumer insurance premiums. Under current Colorado law, the motorist who causes a motor vehicle collision is legally and financially responsible for all resulting property damage, medical treatment expenses, lost wages, and pain and suffering.

The History and Structure of Colorado's Fault-Based Auto Insurance Law

Between 1973 and July 2003, Colorado was one of a dozen states enforcing a mandatory no-fault auto insurance system. Under that historical statutory scheme, injured drivers turned directly to their own insurance carriers to recover medical bills and lost earnings up to statutory PIP limits, regardless of who caused the crash. Lawsuits against negligent drivers were restricted unless injuries exceeded severe statutory medical thresholds.

However, rampant medical billing inflation and high insurance fraud caused automobile insurance premiums in Colorado to skyrocket. On July 1, 2003, the Colorado General Assembly officially allowed the Auto Accident Reparations Act to sunset, completely transitioning the state to a fault-based tort liability system. Today, injured motorists have the immediate legal right to file a claim against the at-fault driver's liability insurance policy or pursue a civil personal injury lawsuit in Colorado District Court without passing arbitrary injury severity thresholds.

Colorado's Modified Comparative Negligence Standard (The 50% Rule)

Determining legal fault in a Colorado collision is governed by the state's Modified Comparative Negligence statute, codified under Colorado Revised Statutes (C.R.S.) Section 13-21-111. Under this legal doctrine, an injured party can recover financial compensation from another driver only if their own share of fault is less than 50%.

If an injured driver is found to be partially responsible for an accident, their gross financial compensation award is reduced in direct proportion to their percentage of negligence. For example, if a jury awards $100,000 in total damages but finds the plaintiff was 20% at fault for failing to brake promptly while the other driver ran a stop sign (80% at fault), the plaintiff receives $80,000. However, if the plaintiff is determined to be 50% or more at fault, Colorado law completely bars them from recovering any compensation whatsoever.

Mandatory Insurance Minimums and Medical Payments (MedPay) Coverage

Because Colorado enforces a fault-based system, state law mandates that every registered motor vehicle owner carry minimum liability insurance under C.R.S. § 10-4-620. The statutory minimums are 25/50/15: $25,000 for bodily injury per person, $50,000 for total bodily injury per accident, and $15,000 for property damage.

Additionally, Colorado law requires insurance carriers to automatically include $5,000 in Medical Payments ('MedPay') coverage on all newly issued auto policies. While policyholders have the legal right to opt out in writing, MedPay is highly advantageous: it pays up to $5,000 in immediate emergency medical, ambulance, or chiropractic bills regardless of fault, providing immediate cash flow while fault investigations are underway.

How to Protect Your Rights After a Colorado Car Accident

Step-by-step procedure to document fault and preserve your personal injury claim following a collision in Colorado.

  1. Call Law Enforcement and Request a Formal Police Accident Report

    Dial 911 immediately. In Colorado, any collision resulting in injury or property damage exceeding $1,000 requires an official police traffic accident report documenting road conditions and initial officer fault impressions.

  2. Gather Physical and Digital Evidence at the Crash Scene

    Take clear photographs of all involved vehicles, license plates, skid marks, traffic signals, and street signs. Collect contact details and insurance policy numbers from all drivers and independent witnesses.

  3. Seek Prompt Medical Evaluation Within 72 Hours

    Visit an emergency department or urgent care facility immediately. Document all physical symptoms, as insurance adjusters routinely challenge injuries that lack immediate clinical documentation.

  4. Notify Your Auto Insurer and Utilize Colorado MedPay

    Report the incident to your insurance carrier to open a claim. Utilize your policy's $5,000 MedPay coverage to pay immediate emergency room and ambulance bills without waiting for the fault investigation to conclude.

  5. File Your Legal Claim Within the Colorado 3-Year Deadline

    Under C.R.S. § 13-80-101, the statute of limitations for automobile accident tort claims in Colorado is three years from the date of the collision. Retain experienced counsel to negotiate settlement before the statutory deadline.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is Colorado a no-fault or at-fault state for car crashes?

Colorado is an at-fault (tort) state. The driver responsible for causing the accident is liable for paying the medical and property damages of the victims.

Q2: When did Colorado stop being a no-fault state?

Colorado transitioned from no-fault to an at-fault tort system on July 1, 2003, when the state legislature repealed the Auto Accident Reparations Act.

Q3: What is Colorado's 50 percent comparative negligence rule?

Under Colorado law, you can recover damages only if your share of fault is 49% or less. If you are found 50% or more responsible, you receive zero compensation.

Q4: What are the minimum auto insurance requirements in Colorado?

Colorado requires $25,000 bodily injury per person, $50,000 bodily injury per accident, and $15,000 property damage liability coverage.

Q5: What is MedPay in Colorado auto insurance?

MedPay is optional first-party coverage ($5,000 default) that pays your immediate medical bills after a crash regardless of who was at fault, without requiring health insurance deductibles.

Q6: How long do I have to file a car accident lawsuit in Colorado?

Under C.R.S. § 13-80-101, the statute of limitations for car accident personal injury claims in Colorado is three years from the date of the accident.

Q7: Can I still recover damages if I was partially at fault in Colorado?

Yes, as long as you were less than 50% at fault. Your total financial payout will be reduced by your percentage of assigned fault.

Q8: Should I speak to the other driver's insurance adjuster?

No. You are not legally required to provide a recorded statement to the at-fault party's insurer. Statements can be used to shift blame to exceed the 50% fault threshold.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

In summary, Colorado is unequivocally a fault-based tort state for car accidents. The negligent driver who initiates a crash bears full financial responsibility for all medical treatment, property damage, and non-economic losses. Injured parties must establish the opposing party's fault while keeping their own negligence below 50% to secure compensation under Colorado's modified comparative negligence framework. Carrying robust uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage and MedPay ensures vital personal financial protection on Colorado highways.
