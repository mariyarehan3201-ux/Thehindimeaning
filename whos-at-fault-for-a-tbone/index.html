Who's at Fault for a T-Bone?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Fault in a T-bone (broadside) car accident lies with the driver who violated the statutory right-of-way, failed to yield, ignored a stop sign, or ran a red traffic light. Unlike rear-end accidents where the rear driver is presumed negligent, liability in a T-bone collision is never determined merely by who struck whom; it is determined by who had the legal right-of-way.

The Legal Doctrine of Right-of-Way in Intersection Collisions

Side-impact broadside collisions—universally referred to in automotive litigation as T-bone crashes—occur when the front bumper of one vehicle collides perpendicularly into the side door panels of another. Because vehicle occupants on the struck side are protected by barely eight to twelve inches of door sheet-metal, T-bone crashes frequently inflict catastrophic, life-altering bodily trauma.

When insurance adjusters and accident reconstructionists evaluate a T-bone collision, an immediate legal question arises: Who is at fault? A pervasive, highly dangerous misconception among motorists is assuming that whichever vehicle physically struck the other with its front bumper is automatically at fault. In American traffic jurisprudence, this assumption is completely false.

Fault in a T-bone accident is governed strictly by the statutory Legal Right-of-Way under state vehicle codes. The vehicle that did the physical striking may be 100% completely innocent—such as a driver traveling lawfully through a green light at 45 miles per hour who broadsides a driver who ran a red light directly into their path. Determining liability requires reconstructing intersection mechanics, traffic signal cycles, and vehicle movements. The table below delineates how fault is legally assigned across common T-bone intersection scenarios.

Intersection T-Bone Scenario Primary Right-of-Way Violation Driver Legally Assigned Fault Key Reconstruction Evidence Used
Red Light Runner vs Lawful Cross Traffic Disobeying steady red traffic control signal Red-light runner is 100% legally liable Municipal signal phasing logs, dashcam video
Unprotected Left Turn across Oncoming Traffic Failure to yield to oncoming vehicles Left-turning driver is primarily at fault (80-100%) Point of impact, vehicle rest positions
Stop Sign Violation at Two-Way Stop Failing to stop or pulling out into through-traffic Stop-sign driver is 100% legally liable Police skid measurements, physical road signs
Four-Way Stop Sign Failure to Yield Failing to yield to vehicle arriving first / to the right Driver who failed to yield right-of-way Eyewitness testimony, simultaneous arrival rules
Pulling Out from Commercial Parking Plaza Entering public highway without yielding Driver exiting private driveway / parking lot Traffic code rules giving highway traffic priority

The Left-Turn Trap: The Presumption of Fault

By far the most common real-world scenario producing catastrophic T-bone accidents is the Unprotected Left Turn at controlled signalized intersections.

Under vehicle codes across all fifty states (such as California Vehicle Code § 21801 or Texas Transportation Code § 545.152), the legal standard is crystal clear: A driver intending to turn left within an intersection or into an alley or driveway must yield the right-of-way to all vehicles approaching from the opposite direction which are within the intersection or so close as to constitute an immediate hazard.

Because statutory law places the affirmative duty on the left-turning driver, insurance companies and civil juries enforce a powerful Rebuttable Presumption of Fault against the turning driver. If you turn left across lanes and a broadside collision occurs, you are presumed negligent. To rebut this presumption, the left-turning driver must prove that the oncoming driver was engaged in extreme negligence—such as traveling 30 miles per hour over the speed limit, driving with headlights turned off at night, or running a stale red light. The table below outlines how comparative fault is split in left-turn T-bone crashes.

Left-Turn Crash Circumstance Left-Turning Driver Fault Share Oncoming Straight Driver Fault Share
Standard turn; oncoming car traveling normal speed 100% at fault 0% fault (Lawful right of way)
Oncoming car traveling 25 mph over speed limit 60% fault (Failed to yield) 40% comparative fault (Excessive speed)
Oncoming car ran clear red light 0% fault 100% at fault (Illegal intersection entry)
Oncoming car driving at night without headlights 20% fault 80% comparative fault (Invisible hazard)

Forensic Evidence: EDR Black Boxes and Signal Phasing Logs

In contested T-bone accidents where both drivers insist: 'I had the green light!' (colloquially called a 'he-said, she-said' dispute), insurance adjusters frequently deny claims due to lack of independent proof. Breaking this deadlock requires forensic engineering evidence.

Your personal injury trial attorney will immediately subpoena two critical electronic data sources. First is the Event Data Recorder (EDR or 'black box') located inside both vehicles' airbag control modules. The EDR records pre-crash telemetry five seconds before impact: exact vehicle speed in miles per hour, engine RPM, percentage throttle application, brake pedal engagement, and lateral G-forces. If the EDR proves that the striking driver never touched their brakes and accelerated into the intersection, their claims of caution are dismantled.

Second is the municipal Department of Transportation (DOT) Traffic Signal Timing and Phasing Logs. Modern computer-controlled intersections record the exact millisecond sequencing of green arrows, yellow clearance intervals, and all-red clearance phases. By cross-referencing the collision timestamp with the signal phase logs and surveillance video from adjacent gas stations or commercial banks, accident reconstruction experts can mathematically prove which driver faced a red light at the exact second of impact.

How to Prove Fault in a Contested T-Bone Accident

Step-by-step investigative roadmap to establish liability after an intersection side-impact crash.

  1. Identify and Secure Civilian Eyewitness Statements

    Obtain contact details from independent drivers or pedestrians who observed which vehicle entered the intersection under a green or red light.

  2. Photograph Skid Marks, Yaw Marks, and Resting Positions

    Capture photos of roadway tire scrub marks, gouge marks in asphalt, and the final resting orientation of both vehicles before they are towed.

  3. Subpoena City Traffic Signal Phasing and Timing Records

    Have your attorney issue a formal records request to the municipal DOT for electronic traffic controller logs for the intersection on the date of crash.

  4. Download Bosch Crash Data Retrieval (CDR) Black Box Data

    Hire an accredited accident reconstruction engineer to download the EDR modules from both vehicles to establish pre-crash speed and braking.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is the driver who hits the side of the car always at fault in a T-bone?

No! The striking driver is innocent if they had a green light or right-of-way and the other driver pulled out illegally in front of them.

Q2: Who is at fault if both drivers claim they had a green light?

Liability is resolved using independent evidence: traffic signal timing logs, intersection surveillance video, dashcams, and eyewitness testimony.

Q3: Are left-turning drivers always at fault in T-bone crashes?

Left-turning drivers are presumed at fault under failure-to-yield laws, but can share or shift fault if the oncoming driver was speeding or ran a red light.

Q4: What is the average settlement for a T-bone car accident?

Settlements frequently range from $50,000 for moderate soft-tissue injuries to well over $1,000,000 for pelvic fractures, spinal surgeries, or brain trauma.

Q5: What is an EDR black box in a T-bone crash?

The Event Data Recorder logs vehicle speed, throttle position, brake application, and lateral crash force (Delta-V) seconds before impact.

Q6: Can both drivers share fault in a T-bone accident?

Yes. Under comparative negligence laws, fault can be split (e.g., 70% to a driver turning left and 30% to an oncoming driver who was speeding).

Q7: Why do T-bone collisions cause such severe injuries?

Vehicle sides have minimal crumple zones (only 8 to 12 inches of door space), transferring kinetic crash force directly into occupants' bodies.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Fault in a T-bone collision is never determined by which car struck the other; it is determined by which driver violated the statutory right-of-way. While left-turning drivers and red-light runners face strong presumptions of negligence, comparative fault principles apply when excessive speeding or inattention contributed to the crash. Obtain immediate trauma medical care, secure the official police report, preserve vehicle black box data, and retain an experienced car accident attorney to uncover traffic signal logs and establish undeniable liability.
