How Long Does a Radon Mitigation System Last?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how long does a radon mitigation system last is a critical health, safety, and property maintenance question for homeowners living in high-radon regions. Radon is a naturally occurring, odorless, radioactive noble gas produced by the radioactive decay of uranium in soil and rock, recognized by the EPA as the leading cause of lung cancer among non-smokers. An active Sub-Slab Depressurization (SSD) radon mitigation system utilizes continuous suction to vent radon gas safely above your roofline. Knowing the lifespan of its fans, PVC piping, and monitoring gauges ensures your home remains permanently protected.

System Anatomy: Mechanical Fans vs. Structural PVC Piping

To evaluate how long a radon mitigation system lasts, one must separate the moving mechanical components from the static structural piping. The structural conduit—consisting of Schedule 40 PVC pipe, polyurethane sub-slab joint sealants, and exterior weather caps—is essentially permanent, easily lasting 30 to 50+ years, lasting as long as the foundation of the house.

The primary mechanical workhorse is the inline Radon Exhaust Fan (manufactured by premier brands like RadonAway, Fantech, or Festa Radon). Because radon fans are engineered with permanently sealed, water-hardened motorized impellers designed to run continuously 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, a quality radon fan typically lasts between 8 and 12 years, with many units running uninterrupted for 15+ years before bearing failure.

Diagnosing Fan Failure: Manometers and Warning Alarms

Because radon fans operate quietly outside in attics or along exterior walls, homeowners often do not realize when a fan has died. A radon fan failure will cause indoor radon concentrations to rebound to dangerous pre-mitigation levels within 24 to 48 hours. To provide continuous visual verification, building codes mandate that every system must feature a visual system monitor, most commonly an oil-filled U-Tube Manometer.

A U-tube manometer does not measure radon gas; it measures physical air pressure vacuum suction inside the pipe. When the fan is operating properly, the red or blue fluid levels in the U-tube are uneven, showing an offset (typically reading between 0.5 and 2.0 inches of water column). If the fluid levels in both columns are completely even at zero (0.0), the fan is completely dead, has lost electrical power, or the vent pipe has suffered a massive air obstruction, requiring immediate fan replacement.

The 2-Year Retesting Mandate and Fan Replacement Costs

Even if your manometer displays healthy suction and your fan is spinning smoothly, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) strongly recommends re-testing your home's radon levels at least once every two years. Over decades, geological tectonic shifts, blasting in nearby quarries, foundation settling, or newly installed bathroom exhaust fans can alter sub-slab vacuum dynamics, causing radon levels to rise despite a running fan.

When a radon fan eventually reaches the end of its 10-year lifespan, replacing it is fast, simple, and inexpensive. Professional radon mitigation contractors standardly replace a dead fan in under an hour. Because the existing PVC pipes and electrical wiring remain intact, replacing the fan with a new OEM model costs between $300 and $600 total (including parts, labor, and a fresh 5-year warranty).

How to Inspect and Maintain Your Radon Mitigation System

Follow these five routine inspection steps to ensure your radon system remains fully operational.

  1. Check the U-Tube Manometer Liquid Levels Monthly

    Look at the clear plastic gauge on the pipe in your basement; verify the liquid levels are uneven (non-zero).

  2. Listen for Unnatural Humming or Rattling Sounds

    Check the fan location periodically; metallic grinding or loud vibrations indicate impending motor bearing failure.

  3. Inspect Flexible Rubber Couplings for Dry Rot

    Ensure the black neoprene rubber couplings connecting the fan to the PVC pipe are tight and crack-free.

  4. Verify Exterior Discharge Exhaust Clearance

    Ensure the roof exhaust pipe remains unblocked by bird nests, leaves, wasps, or winter ice buildup.

  5. Conduct an EPA-Approved Radon Test Every Two Years

    Deploy a charcoal test canister or digital radon monitor every 24 months to certify levels remain below 4.0 pCi/L.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How do I know if my radon fan is working?

Look at the U-tube manometer gauge on the pipe; if the fluid levels are uneven, the fan is creating proper suction; if level at zero, the fan is dead.

Q2: How long does a radon fan run continuously?

Radon fans are engineered to run 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, 365 days a year without ever being shut off.

Q3: How much does it cost to replace a radon fan?

Replacing a dead radon fan professionally typically costs between $300 and $600, including the new fan, labor, and warranty.

Q4: Can a radon fan catch on fire?

Radon fans feature internal thermal overload protection that automatically shuts off the motor if it overheats, preventing fires.

Q5: How often should you test your home for radon if you have a system?

The EPA recommends conducting an independent radon test at least once every 2 years to confirm the system maintains safe levels.

Q6: Why does my radon fan make a loud humming noise?

Loud humming or screeching indicates the internal motor bearings are worn out, signaling the fan must be replaced soon.

Q7: Can I turn off my radon fan to save electricity?

Never turn off your radon fan; indoor radon levels will surge back to dangerous cancer-causing levels within 24 to 48 hours.

Q8: How much electricity does a radon mitigation fan use?

A typical radon fan uses only 50 to 90 watts of electricity, costing approximately $50 to $100 per year in electricity.
