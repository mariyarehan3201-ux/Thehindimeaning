Can You Sue After a Car Accident?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, you can sue after a car accident if another driver's negligence caused your physical injuries or property damage. While traditional 'tort' states allow immediate lawsuits for all damages, 'no-fault' states require your physical injuries to meet a statutory 'verbal threshold'—such as permanent impairment, significant scarring, or bone fractures—before you can sue for pain and suffering.

The Foundational Jurisprudence: Tort States vs No-Fault States

Following a motor vehicle collision, surviving victims are confronted with escalating medical bills, vehicle repair expenses, lost paychecks, and intense physical pain. When insurance claims stall or adjusters offer insultingly low settlement checks, injured drivers naturally ask: 'Can I sue the person who hit me?'

In American personal injury law, your legal right to file a lawsuit against an at-fault driver is determined primarily by your state's automotive insurance system. The United States is fundamentally divided into two distinct legal frameworks: Traditional At-Fault (Tort) States and No-Fault Insurance States.

In Traditional Tort States (representing approximately 38 out of 50 states, including California, Texas, Georgia, Illinois, and Ohio), the legal system operates under pure fault principles. If another driver caused the crash through carelessness, speeding, texting, or failing to yield, you possess the immediate constitutional right to file a civil lawsuit against them. You can sue for all economic damages (gross medical bills, lost wages) and non-economic damages (physical pain, mental anguish, loss of life enjoyment) from day one. The table below delineates the procedural differences between tort and no-fault jurisdictions.

The 'Verbal Threshold' Barrier in No-Fault States

If your car accident occurred in a No-Fault state (such as Florida, New York, New Jersey, or Michigan), the legal rules governing your right to sue are substantially more complex.

In no-fault states, state law mandates that your own auto insurance policy's Personal Injury Protection (PIP) coverage pays your initial medical bills and lost earnings (typically up to $10,000 to $50,000) regardless of who caused the accident. In exchange for this guaranteed no-fault medical coverage, state legislatures stripped motorists of the right to sue for non-economic pain and suffering for minor, temporary injuries.

However, no-fault laws contain a vital statutory escape hatch known as the Serious Injury Threshold or Verbal Threshold. Under state insurance statutes (such as New York Insurance Law § 5102(d) or Florida Statutes § 627.737), you regain the full legal right to sue the at-fault driver for unlimited pain and suffering if your medical trauma satisfies statutory criteria. Qualifying injuries include: significant and permanent loss of an important bodily function, permanent injury within a reasonable degree of medical probability, significant disfigurement or scarring, bone fractures, or death. The table below outlines what clinical injuries pierce the no-fault threshold.

Bone Fractures (Any Location) Fractured collarbone, broken wrist, rib fracture Yes: Statutorily defined as serious injury in NY/FL Full right to sue for substantial damages
Severe Facial Lacerations / Scars Shattered windshield glass cuts requiring plastic surgery Yes: Qualifies as significant permanent disfigurement Full right to sue for substantial damages
Minor Cervical Sprain / Strain Soft-tissue whiplash resolving completely in 4 weeks No: Barred under no-fault verbal threshold Limited strictly to unpaid medical/PIP benefits

What Damages Can You Sue for in Civil Court?

When you file a personal injury lawsuit against a negligent driver, civil tort law entitles you to pursue full financial recovery across three comprehensive categories of damages.

First are Economic Damages. These are quantifiable, out-of-pocket financial losses resulting directly from the collision. Economic damages include past emergency room resuscitation fees, surgeries, hospital stays, physical therapy sessions, prescription medications, future projected medical surgeries, past lost wages, and diminished future earning capacity if your injuries force you into lower-paying employment.

Second are Non-Economic Damages. Often representing the largest portion of a personal injury recovery, non-economic damages compensate for the profound human toll of physical trauma. This includes physical pain and suffering, emotional anguish, sleep disturbances, Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), physical disfigurement from surgical scars, and loss of consortium (damage to marital intimacy). Third are Punitive Damages, awarded in cases where the at-fault driver engaged in gross negligence, such as driving while heavily intoxicated or fleeing the scene of a crash.

How to File a Lawsuit After a Car Accident

Step-by-step litigation roadmap to initiate a personal injury lawsuit after a crash.

  1. Complete Thorough Diagnostic Medical Evaluations

    Obtain high-resolution MRI imaging and specialist evaluations to document permanent spinal disc tears, fractures, or neurological trauma.

  2. Establish Negligence and Pierce Statutory Thresholds

    Have your attorney audit medical records to verify injuries satisfy state 'serious injury' thresholds in no-fault jurisdictions.

  3. Send a Formal Pre-Suit Policy Limits Demand Letter

    Submit a comprehensive demand package detailing liability and medical damages, giving the insurer 30 days to tender policy limits.

  4. File a Formal Civil Summons and Complaint in Court

    If the insurance company refuses fair settlement, file a lawsuit in county civil court and initiate formal discovery and depositions.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sue if I was partially at fault for the accident?

Yes. Under comparative negligence laws, you can recover damages as long as you were not 50% or 51% or more at fault, with your payout reduced by your fault share.

Q2: How long do I have to sue after a car accident?

The statute of limitations typically ranges from two to three years from the date of the crash, governed by your state's civil practice codes.

Q3: Can I sue the at-fault driver if I don't have insurance?

In most states yes, but 'No Pay, No Play' states (like CA, LA, MI) restrict uninsured drivers from recovering non-economic pain and suffering damages.

Q4: Do most car accident lawsuits go to a jury trial?

No. More than 95% of personal injury car accident lawsuits settle out of court during pre-trial mediation or depositions.

Q5: What is a verbal threshold in a car accident lawsuit?

A statutory rule in no-fault states requiring physical injuries to meet specific legal definitions of seriousness before suing for pain and suffering.

Q6: How much does a lawyer charge to sue for a car accident?

Personal injury lawyers work on a contingency fee basis (typically 33.3% to 40%), charging zero upfront fees and getting paid only if you win.

Q7: Can I sue for a car accident if I feel fine initially?

Yes, provided you seek medical care once latent symptoms emerge; many severe spinal disc and soft-tissue injuries take days to manifest.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

You can sue after a car accident to recover complete financial justice from a negligent driver. While traditional tort states permit immediate lawsuits, no-fault states require clinical proof that your physical injuries cross statutory serious injury thresholds. Seek emergency room medical treatment within 24 hours to create an undeniable clinical record, preserve physical scene evidence, decline recorded statements from insurance adjusters, and retain a personal injury trial attorney to navigate complex insurance thresholds and maximize your settlement.
