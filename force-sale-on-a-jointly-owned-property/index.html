Force Sale on a Jointly Owned Property

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 12, 2026

When co-owners of real estate reach an irreconcilable impasse regarding the use, management, or liquidation of real estate, understanding how to force sale on a jointly owned property is a fundamental legal right. Co-ownership disputes frequently arise among siblings who inherit family homes through probate, unmarried couples who purchase residential real estate and subsequently separate, or real estate business partners whose investment strategies diverge. Under the longstanding common-law doctrine of partition, no individual is legally forced to remain in co-ownership against their will. If voluntary buyout negotiations fail, a co-owner can file a judicial partition action in court to compel the liquidation of the property and equitable distribution of net sales proceeds.

Voluntary Buyouts and Pre-Litigation Dispute Resolution

Before initiating formal legal proceedings in court to force a sale, co-owners should exhaust voluntary negotiation and structured buyout mechanisms. Judicial lawsuits are costly, emotionally exhausting, and public proceedings that consume substantial portions of real estate equity through court fees and attorney billing. The most straightforward resolution is a voluntary buyout, where one co-owner purchases the equity interest of the departing owner. To execute an equitable buyout, both parties should hire a licensed, independent real estate appraiser to establish an objective fair market valuation, deducting standard mortgage payoff balances to determine net equity.

If neither co-owner can independently qualify for refinancing or possess sufficient capital to buy out the other, retaining a professional real estate mediator or collaborative attorney provides a structured negotiation forum. Mediation allows parties to negotiate practical compromises—such as listing the property voluntarily with a neutral real estate broker on the open multiple listing service (MLS), agreeing on a minimum reserve price, and scheduling move-out timelines. Selling cooperatively on the open market yields maximum retail dollar value, whereas forced court auctions frequently liquidate real estate at substantial wholesale discounts.

The Legal Mechanism: Filing a Partition Action in Court

When one co-owner flatly refuses to sell, communicates unreasonable buyout terms, or remains completely non-responsive, the petitioning co-owner must file a formal lawsuit known as a Partition Action in the state superior or district court where the property is located. Under property jurisprudence, whether holding title as Tenants in Common or Joint Tenants with Right of Survivorship, an owner statutory right to partition is virtually absolute. Unless a co-owner previously signed an enforceable written waiver of partition rights in a partnership agreement, the defending co-owner cannot block the lawsuit simply because they wish to remain living in the house.

The court partition process begins with filing a formal Complaint for Partition and immediately recording a Notice of Pendency of Action (commonly known as a Lis Pendens) with the county recorder office. The Lis Pendens acts as a formal cloud on the property title, putting title insurers, lenders, and prospective buyers on public notice of ongoing litigation. Once filed, the defending co-owner cannot secretly encumber the real estate with new loans or attempt an unauthorized transfer of title while the court litigation is pending.

Judicial Accounting: Offsets, Mortgage Payments, and Improvements

A forced sale is rarely as simple as dividing gross proceeds fifty-fifty. The presiding judge or court-appointed referee must conduct an equitable judicial accounting before a single dollar of net sales proceeds is disbursed to the parties. Judicial accounting adjusts distributions based on each co-owner financial contributions during the period of co-ownership. If one owner paid the mortgage, property taxes, structural roof repairs, or mandatory homeowner association dues out of pocket while the other contributed nothing, the paying owner is legally entitled to reimbursement offsets (known in equity as contribution credits) taken directly from the delinquent owner share.

Furthermore, the court considers claims regarding occupancy and property improvements. If one co-owner enjoyed exclusive physical possession of the residence while barring the other owner access (an event termed an ouster), the excluded co-owner can demand an offset credit for the fair rental value of the property for the duration of their exclusion. Regarding home remodeling, the law does not reimburse the full retail dollar spent on luxury upgrades; rather, the court credits only the proven increase in fair market value that the improvement contributed to the final sales price.

How to Force Sale on a Jointly Owned Property in 5 Steps

Follow this step-by-step legal and strategic roadmap to compel the sale of co-owned real estate.

  1. Attempt a Formal Buyout or Mediation First

    Hire an independent appraiser to establish fair market value and make a written buyout offer or propose a consensual MLS broker listing.

  2. Consult an Experienced Real Estate Litigation Attorney

    Retain a local real estate attorney who specializes in partition actions to draft a formal partition complaint under state statutes.

  3. File the Partition Complaint and Record Lis Pendens

    File the lawsuit in the county superior court and record a Notice of Pendency of Action with the county recorder to cloud the title.

  4. Obtain Interlocutory Judgment and Referee Appointment

    Petition the court to decree the forced sale and appoint an independent referee to manage the real estate listing and broker selection.

  5. Conduct Judicial Accounting and Disburse Equity

    Submit proof of mortgage, tax, and maintenance payments to claim reimbursement offsets, then receive your equitable share of net proceeds.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can one co-owner force the sale of a jointly owned home?

Yes, under property law, any co-owner has a statutory right to file a partition lawsuit to compel the sale of the property.

Q2: Can a co-owner stop a forced partition sale?

Generally no, unless they previously signed a valid written waiver of partition rights or exercise a buyout right under heirs property acts.

Q3: How are proceeds divided in a court-ordered forced sale?

Net proceeds are divided according to ownership percentages, adjusted for judicial accounting credits like mortgage and tax payments.

Q4: What is an interlocutory judgment in a partition lawsuit?

It is an interim court decree finding that the property cannot be physically divided and ordering that it be sold at market.

Q5: Can I get reimbursed for mortgage payments I made alone?

Yes, judges grant contribution offsets during the judicial accounting phase, reimbursing owners who paid mortgage principal and taxes.

Q6: What is an ouster in joint property disputes?

An ouster occurs when one co-owner locks out or excludes another, entitling the excluded co-owner to credits for fair rental value.

Q7: How long does a forced partition lawsuit take from start to finish?

A typical partition lawsuit takes between nine and eighteen months, though voluntary settlements during litigation can shorten it.

Q8: Does a forced property sale always happen at a public auction?

No, courts and modern partition statutes overwhelmingly prefer appointing a real estate referee to list the home on the open MLS.
