Can a Pedestrian Sue If Hit by a Car?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, a pedestrian can sue if hit by a car under civil personal injury and negligence laws. Because motor vehicle drivers owe an elevated legal duty of care to watch for vulnerable pedestrians, an injured pedestrian can recover compensation for medical bills, trauma surgeries, lost wages, and pain and suffering from the at-fault driver's auto liability insurance, even if the pedestrian was partially at fault.

The Physics and Kinetic Disparity of Pedestrian Collisions

When an automobile collides with an unprotected human body, the laws of physics dictate catastrophic bodily consequences. Motor vehicles weigh between 3,500 pounds for a compact sedan to over 6,000 pounds for an electric SUV or pickup truck. In contrast, an adult human pedestrian possesses zero armor, crumple zones, or restraint mechanisms.

In vehicular accident reconstruction and forensic biomechanics, a pedestrian impact involves a three-phase trauma trajectory. In the Primary Impact, the vehicle's front bumper strikes the pedestrian's lower extremities, fracturing the tibia, fibula, and knee joints. In the Secondary Impact, the pedestrian's torso and head whip onto the vehicle's hood and shatter the rigid windshield. In the Tertiary Impact, the pedestrian is thrown violently onto the asphalt road surface.

Even low-speed collisions occurring at 15 to 25 miles per hour frequently inflict catastrophic orthopedic trauma, complex pelvic ring fractures, spinal cord severance, and traumatic brain injury (TBI). Because pedestrian medical bills routinely exceed hundreds of thousands of dollars, civil litigation against the negligent driver is an absolute necessity to prevent personal bankruptcy. Establishing Driver Negligence: Duty of Care and Crosswalks

Under state vehicle traffic codes across the United States, motor vehicle drivers owe an affirmative Duty of Care to operate with reasonable caution, maintain a proper lookout, and exercise vigilance to avoid striking pedestrians.

When an accident occurs in a designated crosswalk—whether marked with bright white zebra stripes or classified as an Unmarked Crosswalk at a standard four-way intersection—state traffic statutes establish that pedestrians have the absolute Right-of-Way. A driver who strikes a pedestrian in a crosswalk has committed an explicit statutory violation. In civil court, this constitutes Negligence Per Se, establishing legal liability automatically and stripping the driver of ordinary defenses.

Furthermore, drivers are held liable when collisions result from distracted driving (texting behind the wheel), speeding in school or residential zones, failing to yield while executing left turns across intersections, or driving while impaired. Even outside crosswalks, drivers are legally obligated to exercise 'due care' and scan roadways for pedestrians. Can You Sue If You Were Jaywalking? Comparative Fault Rules

The single most common defense asserted by insurance adjusters representing drivers who hit pedestrians is: 'The pedestrian was jaywalking outside a crosswalk, so we are denying the claim.'

This insurance defense argument is fundamentally flawed under modern civil tort jurisprudence. Crossing mid-block outside a crosswalk (colloquially called jaywalking) does not give motorists legal permission to run you over. Under Comparative Negligence laws (enforced in 46 out of 50 states), an accident's financial responsibility is divided proportionally according to each party's percentage of fault.

In Modified Comparative Negligence states, an injured pedestrian can still recover substantial financial compensation as long as they were not 50% or 51% or more at fault. For example, if a jury determines that a jaywalking pedestrian was 25% responsible for crossing mid-block, but the speeding driver who was looking down at a smartphone was 75% responsible, the pedestrian recovers 75% of their total financial damages. In catastrophic injury cases where damages equal $1,000,000, recovering 75% equals $750,000 in vital financial relief.

How to Build a Personal Injury Claim as a Struck Pedestrian

Step-by-step litigation roadmap for pedestrians injured by negligent motor vehicles.

  1. Ensure Responding Police Complete an Official Crash Report

    Confirm investigating police officers document the driver's exact point of impact, skid marks, speed limits, and traffic citations issued.

  2. Photograph the Crosswalk, Traffic Signals, and Vehicle Damage

    Capture photos of crosswalk striping, pedestrian walk signal phasing, vehicle windshield spidering, and damaged street lighting.

  3. Identify Nearby Surveillance Cameras and Civilian Witnesses

    Locate municipal red-light cameras, city bus dashcams, and storefront security cameras that recorded the vehicle hitting you.

  4. Audit All Available Auto, PIP, and UIM Insurance Policies

    Have your attorney stack the driver's liability policy, your own household auto Personal Injury Protection (PIP), and Underinsured Motorist (UIM) riders.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sue if I was hit by a car while jaywalking?

Yes. Under comparative negligence laws, you can still recover compensation even if you were partially at fault for crossing outside a crosswalk.

Q2: Who pays my medical bills if I don't own a car?

Depending on your state, the at-fault driver's bodily injury liability, no-fault PIP coverage, or your household family auto policy covers your bills.

Q3: What is an unmarked crosswalk in pedestrian law?

An unmarked crosswalk is the invisible legal continuation of a sidewalk across an intersection; pedestrians retain full right-of-way.

Q4: What happens if a driver hits a pedestrian and flees (hit and run)?

You can file a claim through your own auto insurance policy's Uninsured Motorist (UM) bodily injury coverage to receive full settlement benefits.

Q5: How much is an average pedestrian accident settlement?

Settlements frequently range from $100,000 for fractures to over $1,500,000 for traumatic brain injuries, spinal cord damage, or wrongful death.

Q6: Can a driver sue a pedestrian for damaging their car?

While theoretically possible if a pedestrian acted recklessly, drivers almost never succeed due to their elevated duty of care to watch for people.

Q7: What is the statute of limitations for a pedestrian accident?

Statutes of limitations typically allow two to three years from the date of the crash, governed by your state's personal injury civil practice codes.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

A pedestrian hit by a car possesses powerful legal rights to sue the negligent driver and recover comprehensive compensation for medical bills, surgical trauma, and life-altering suffering. Crosswalk laws strictly protect pedestrians, while comparative fault principles ensure recovery even in mid-block crossing disputes. Ensure responding police document the crash, seek emergency trauma medical care immediately, preserve scene photographs, and retain a specialized pedestrian injury lawyer to hold the driver fully accountable.
