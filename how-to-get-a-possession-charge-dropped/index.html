How to Get a Possession Charge Dropped?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

While many people use the legal terms 'dismissed' and 'dropped' interchangeably, they represent two distinctly different procedural outcomes within the American criminal justice system. A charge dismissal is an order issued by a presiding judge, whereas having a charge dropped is an executive decision made exclusively by the prosecuting attorney (such as a District Attorney, State Attorney, or City Prosecutor). Getting a possession charge dropped occurs when defense counsel convinces prosecutors to decline filing formal charges or enter a formal notice of nolle prosequi, permanently ending prosecution before trial.

The Difference Between Charges Dropped and Judicial Dismissal

Navigating criminal proceedings requires an accurate understanding of who holds the authority to terminate a criminal case. When an individual is arrested for possession of a controlled substance or illegal contraband, law enforcement officers only make an initial arrest and recommend charges. The formal charging authority belongs entirely to the prosecutor's office. If defense counsel intervenes during the early pre-filing window, prosecutors can review the police report and exercise their absolute prosecutorial discretion to 'drop' or reject the charges, electing not to file an official criminal Information or Indictment.

Even after formal court charges have been docketed, a prosecutor can drop the prosecution at any stage prior to a jury verdict by filing an official notice known as a 'nolle prosequi' (formal abandonment of the charge). In contrast, a judicial dismissal occurs when a judge issues an order throwing out the charges, typically following a defense motion demonstrating an unconstitutional search, lack of probable cause, or a speedy trial violation. Securing dropped charges from a prosecutor avoids adversarial courtroom litigation, saves substantial legal fees, and concludes the ordeal swiftly.

Persuading the Prosecutor: Evidentiary Gaps and Pre-Filing Advocacy

The most effective strategy to get a possession charge dropped is aggressive, early intervention by a criminal defense lawyer before the state attorney makes a formal filing decision. Once an arrest occurs, there is typically a two to four-week window while the case sits on the intake prosecutor's desk awaiting review. During this critical window, proactive defense counsel contacts the reviewing prosecutor, presents exculpatory evidence, highlights fatal evidentiary weaknesses, and provides mitigating personal background documentation.

Evidentiary infirmities that frequently persuade prosecutors to abandon possession cases include constructive possession issues—such as drugs located in rental cars or shared living quarters where multiple parties had access. If defense counsel can present witness affidavits or vehicle lease agreements proving the defendant had no knowledge or control over the contraband, prosecutors will routinely drop the charge rather than risk an embarrassing courtroom loss. Similarly, pointing out obvious Fourth Amendment search violations, such as an unjustified warrantless stop or defective canine sniff, signals to prosecutors that the physical evidence will inevitably be suppressed in court.

Prosecutorial Discretion, Deferred Prosecution, and First-Offender Deals

Modern prosecuting agencies increasingly prioritize substance abuse treatment over punitive incarceration for non-violent, simple possession offenses. When prosecutors are reluctant to drop a charge outright due to departmental policies, defense attorneys negotiate 'Deferred Prosecution Agreements' or informal conditional drop agreements. Under these agreements, the prosecutor agrees to place the charges on hold for a designated period (typically three to six months) while the defendant completes specified benchmarks.

These benchmarks routinely include completing an accredited outpatient substance awareness seminar, completing twenty-four to forty hours of community service, and maintaining clean random urine screenings. Once the defendant successfully fulfills all agreed conditions, the prosecutor honors the commitment by filing a formal dismissal or nolle prosequi notice. This completely wipes out the prosecution without leaving a criminal conviction on the defendant's background record.

How to Convince a Prosecutor to Drop a Possession Charge

A practical guide to early defense intervention, gathering exculpatory evidence, and negotiating dropped charges with prosecutors.

  1. Engage a Defense Lawyer During the Pre-Filing Window

    Retain an attorney immediately after release from jail so counsel can communicate with intake prosecutors before formal charges are officially filed.

  2. Compile Exculpatory and Mitigating Evidence

    Gather witness statements, vehicle rental agreements, proof of clean drug screens, and character reference letters demonstrating your lack of involvement.

  3. Submit a Formal Pre-Filing Defense Package

    Have your attorney submit a written presentation highlighting police search flaws and constructive possession weaknesses directly to the assigned prosecutor.

  4. Enroll Proactively in Substance Counseling

    Voluntarily enroll in a recognized substance education or counseling program to demonstrate personal accountability and rehabilitation.

  5. Negotiate a Deferred Prosecution Agreement

    If the prosecutor will not drop charges immediately, negotiate an agreement where charges are dropped upon completing community service or treatment.

  6. Secure Written Confirmation of Nolle Prosequi

    Ensure your attorney obtains official written documentation confirming that the prosecutor has formally declined to prosecute or entered a nolle prosequi.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What does it mean when a prosecutor drops a charge?

When a prosecutor drops a charge, they officially choose not to pursue criminal prosecution, either by declining to file formal court papers or by filing a nolle prosequi dismissing existing charges.

Q2: Can police drop charges after making an arrest?

No, police officers lack legal authority to drop charges once an arrest is completed. Only prosecuting attorneys possess the constitutional discretion to file, reduce, or drop charges.

Q3: How long does a prosecutor have to drop or file charges?

Prosecutors typically decide within 30 to 90 days after arrest, though the statutory deadline is dictated by state statutes of limitations, which range from one year for misdemeanors to three or more years for felonies.

Q4: Can a dropped possession charge be brought back later?

If charges are dropped without prejudice prior to trial, the prosecutor can technically refile charges within the statute of limitations, though refiling rarely occurs unless new incriminating evidence emerges.

Q5: What is a nolle prosequi in a drug case?

Nolle prosequi is a formal legal notice filed by the prosecutor declaring that the state will no longer prosecute the pending criminal charges against the defendant.

Q6: Does going to rehab help get a drug charge dropped?

Yes, proactively entering licensed drug rehabilitation shows prosecutors that you are seeking treatment, often motivating them to drop simple possession charges in favor of public health recovery.

Q7: Can an illegal vehicle stop cause a prosecutor to drop charges?

Yes, if defense counsel proves the police officer pulled the vehicle over without reasonable suspicion, prosecutors will drop the case because the evidence would be suppressed by the judge anyway.

Q8: Can I expunge a possession charge that was dropped?

Yes, in most states, once a criminal charge has been officially dropped or dismissed without conviction, you are immediately eligible to petition the court for a full record expungement.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Getting a criminal possession charge dropped by a prosecutor requires swift, proactive legal advocacy before formal charging documents are filed in court. By demonstrating constructive possession vulnerabilities, highlighting unconstitutional police searches, presenting proof of third-party ownership, and leveraging proactive rehabilitation programs, defendants can persuade prosecutors to drop charges, preserving their clean criminal records and long-term futures.
