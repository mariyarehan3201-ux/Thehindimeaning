Paying a Canadian Employee from a US Company

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Paying a Canadian employee from a US company requires strict compliance with cross-border tax laws, statutory provincial employment standards, and mandatory Canadian payroll deductions. US businesses cannot simply issue a standard US W-2 or direct deposit in US dollars without triggering severe penalties from the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA).

Cross-Border Employment Structures: Legal Frameworks and Options

With the widespread adoption of remote work, US businesses frequently recruit top-tier technical and managerial talent located in Canada. However, hiring an employee residing in Canada is fundamentally different from hiring a worker across US state lines. Under international labor and taxation treaties, an individual performing work while physically located inside Canada is subject to Canadian federal and provincial labor laws, regardless of where the employing corporate entity is headquartered.

A US company seeking to engage Canadian talent generally has three primary legal pathways. The first and most compliant model for growing companies is partnering with an Employer of Record (EOR). An EOR (such as Remote, Deel, or Oyster) acts as the legal employer of the worker in Canada through an established local entity, managing local payroll taxes, statutory benefits, and provincial labor standards while the US company manages daily work directives.

The second option is incorporating a Canadian subsidiary or branch office and registering for a business number and payroll program account (RP account) directly with the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA). This approach provides complete direct control but requires substantial legal, accounting, and corporate maintenance expenses. The third option is engaging the Canadian worker as an independent contractor; however, this carries severe misclassification risks under Canadian common law if the individual works full-time hours, uses company equipment, and does not operate an independent commercial business. Mandatory Canadian Statutory Payroll Deductions (CPP, EI, Taxes)

Employers paying Canadian workers must administer statutory payroll withholdings and employer contributions in accordance with CRA regulations. Canadian payroll cannot be processed through standard US payroll software (like ADP US or Gusto US) because US systems are not programmed to calculate Canadian federal income tax, provincial income taxes, Canada Pension Plan (CPP), or Employment Insurance (EI).

Both the employer and the employee must contribute to the Canada Pension Plan (or the Quebec Pension Plan for workers in Quebec) up to an annual maximum statutory earnings ceiling. Additionally, Employment Insurance (EI) premiums must be withheld from the employee pay, and the employer must contribute 1.4 times the employee premium amount. Employers must also remit provincial health levies, workers compensation premiums (such as WSIB in Ontario or WorkSafeBC), and account for statutory paid vacation (typically a minimum of two to three weeks per year, or 4% to 6% vacation pay). Permanent Establishment Risks and Currency Considerations

A critical corporate tax risk that US companies overlook when hiring Canadian workers is creating an unintended Permanent Establishment (PE) in Canada. Under the Canada-US Tax Treaty, if a Canadian-based employee possesses and habitually exercises the legal authority to negotiate or conclude binding corporate contracts on behalf of the US parent company, the CRA may determine that the US company has established a taxable physical presence in Canada. This triggers corporate income tax liabilities and complex cross-border financial audits.

Currency management is another practical hurdle. Canadian employees expect to be paid in Canadian Dollars (CAD) to pay local mortgages, utility bills, and grocery expenses without incurring foreign exchange conversion fees. If a US company quotes salary in US Dollars (USD), fluctuating exchange rates can create unpredictability for both employee and employer budgets. Utilizing an EOR or specialized international cross-border payroll processor ensures funds are converted and deposited seamlessly into the worker local Canadian bank account in CAD.

How to Legally Hire and Pay a Canadian Employee from the US

A step-by-step roadmap for US businesses to onboard, set up payroll, and compliantly pay a remote Canadian worker.

  1. Choose Your Cross-Border Employment Structure

    Decide whether to partner with an Employer of Record (EOR), incorporate a Canadian subsidiary with a CRA payroll account, or engage a true independent contractor based on workforce size and job role.

  2. Draft a Canadian-Compliant Employment Agreement

    Create an employment contract that adheres to the employment standards legislation of the specific Canadian province where the worker resides, addressing statutory notice periods, severance, and vacation pay.

  3. Collect Required Canadian Tax and Banking Forms

    Collect the employee Canadian Social Insurance Number (SIN), completed federal TD1 personal tax credits return form, provincial TD1 form, and direct deposit void cheque for a Canadian bank account.

  4. Calculate and Remit Statutory Canadian Deductions

    Utilize your EOR or Canadian payroll software to calculate and deduct Canada Pension Plan (CPP), Employment Insurance (EI), and provincial income taxes, remitting matching employer portions to the CRA.

  5. Issue Year-End Canadian T4 Tax Slips

    Ensure annual Canadian T4 Statements of Remuneration Paid are generated and filed with the Canada Revenue Agency and provided to the employee by the statutory deadline of late February.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can a US company pay a Canadian employee in US dollars?

Yes, but it is not recommended. Canadian employees must pay local taxes and living expenses in Canadian dollars (CAD). Paying in USD forces the worker to incur exchange fees and creates fluctuating income due to currency swings.

Q2: Can a US company issue a W-2 to a Canadian remote worker?

No. US W-2 forms are strictly for workers subject to US payroll taxes. Canadian residents working in Canada must receive a Canadian T4 slip, with statutory taxes remitted directly to the Canada Revenue Agency (CRA).

Q3: What is an Employer of Record (EOR) in Canada?

An EOR is a third-party company that legally employs Canadian workers on behalf of a foreign company, handling local payroll, CPP/EI remittances, benefits, and provincial labor law compliance.

Q4: What is the penalty for misclassifying a Canadian worker as a contractor?

The CRA can retroactively reclassify the worker as an employee, holding the US company liable for all unpaid employer CPP and EI contributions, unremitted income taxes, plus substantial interest and penalty fines.

Q5: Do Canadian employees get healthcare benefits from a US employer?

While basic medical and hospital care is provided through provincial public healthcare (like OHIP in Ontario), Canadian employers typically provide supplemental private health insurance covering dental, vision, and prescription drugs.

Q6: What is Permanent Establishment risk in Canada?

Permanent Establishment (PE) occurs when a Canadian employee has the authority to sign contracts or make binding commercial deals for a US company, potentially subjecting the US company's global profits to Canadian corporate taxes.

Q7: How much vacation time are Canadian employees entitled to?

Under Canadian provincial employment standards, full-time employees are legally entitled to a minimum of two weeks paid vacation annually (accrued at 4% of gross wages), increasing to three weeks (6%) after several years of service.

Q8: What forms must a Canadian employee fill out for payroll?

Canadian employees must provide their 9-digit Social Insurance Number (SIN) and complete both the Federal TD1 Personal Tax Credits Return and the Provincial TD1 form corresponding to their province of residence.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Hiring and paying a Canadian employee allows US companies to access world-class talent, but circumventing local employment laws invites severe CRA audit penalties, back taxes, and misclassification lawsuits. For most US businesses, utilizing a certified Employer of Record provides an immediate, turn-key, and fully compliant solution. By respecting Canadian statutory deductions, adhering to provincial employment standards, and structuring employment agreements carefully, US enterprises can scale their cross-border workforce successfully.
