How Do I Get Into Canada with a DUI?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how do i get into canada with a dui is essential for American citizens, international business travelers, and tourists planning to cross the Canadian border. Under the Canadian Immigration and Refugee Protection Act (IRPA) and Canadian Bill C-46, driving under the influence (DUI)—along with driving while intoxicated (DWI), wet reckless, and similar offenses—is classified as serious criminality. A single misdemeanor DUI conviction can render you strictly criminally inadmissible at Canadian border ports of entry unless you secure formal legal relief.

Bill C-46 and the Canadian Definition of Serious Criminality

Prior to December 2018, an American misdemeanor DUI was generally classified under Canadian immigration law as ordinary criminality. However, with the enactment of Canadian federal legislation Bill C-46 in December 2018, Canada dramatically increased the maximum penalty for impaired driving under the Canadian Criminal Code from five years to ten years of imprisonment. This statutory shift automatically elevated DUI to 'Serious Criminality' under IRPA Section 36(1).

Because of this reclassification, travelers with a single DUI conviction can no longer benefit from automatic 'Deemed Rehabilitation' simply because ten years have elapsed since completing their criminal sentence. Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) officers have full, real-time access to the FBI's National Crime Information Center (NCIC) database at border booths and airport terminals. When your passport is scanned, any past arrest or conviction instantly alerts border officers.

Temporary Resident Permits (TRP) for Urgent Travel Needs

If you must enter Canada for compelling reasons before five years have elapsed since completing your entire DUI sentence, your primary legal option is applying for a Temporary Resident Permit (TRP). A TRP is a discretionary waiver granted under IRPA Section 24. To qualify, you must demonstrate to Canadian immigration officials that your reason for entering Canada substantially outweighs any perceived risk to Canadian health, safety, or society.

Compelling reasons typically include urgent business commitments, international corporate meetings, attending close family weddings or funerals, or performing essential professional work. Pure leisure tourism is rarely considered compelling for a TRP. Applicants can submit a TRP dossier in advance to a Canadian consulate (taking 3 to 8 months for processing) or, in urgent circumstances, submit an application directly at a land border port of entry to a senior CBSA officer.

Permanent Solution: Individual Criminal Rehabilitation

The most permanent and robust solution for travelers with a DUI is applying for Individual Criminal Rehabilitation through Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC). Once five full calendar years have elapsed since the completion of all sentencing terms—including payment of all court fines, termination of supervised probation, completion of alcohol education classes, and reinstatement of driver privileges—you become eligible to petition for formal rehabilitation.

An approved Criminal Rehabilitation application permanently erases your criminal inadmissibility regarding the specific disclosed offenses. Once granted, you can enter Canada freely for business or tourism for the rest of your life without needing permits or waivers, provided you commit no further offenses. Processing times typically range from 6 to 18 months, making advance filing vital before future travel.

How to Legally Enter Canada with a DUI Conviction

Follow these five strategic steps to prepare documentation and resolve Canadian criminal inadmissibility.

  1. Calculate Your Exact Sentence Completion Date

    Confirm the calendar date you finished all probation, paid every court fine, and completed all required courses.

  2. Obtain FBI Fingerprint Background Check

    Request an official FBI Identity History Summary via digital fingerprinting through an approved channeler.

  3. Gather Certified Court Records and Police Certificates

    Acquire certified copies of your court docket, conviction record, and proof that probation was terminated.

  4. Select TRP or Criminal Rehabilitation Track

    Apply for a TRP if your travel is urgent or under 5 years; apply for Criminal Rehabilitation if over 5 years.

  5. Submit Application to IRCC Consulate or Port of Entry

    File your complete dossier with Canadian immigration processing centers or present at the border with fees.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I enter Canada with a misdemeanor DUI on my record?

No, under Canadian law, a US misdemeanor DUI is classified as serious criminality, rendering you inadmissible without a TRP or Criminal Rehabilitation.

Q2: Does Canada know about my DUI if it happened years ago?

Yes, the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) has direct real-time access to the FBI NCIC database when your passport is scanned.

Q3: Does an expunged DUI count in Canada?

Yes, standard US state expungements often do not erase the conviction for Canadian immigration purposes unless equivalent to an official pardon.

Q4: How long after a DUI can I apply for Canadian Criminal Rehabilitation?

You must wait exactly 5 years from the date your entire sentence was completed (including probation and fine payments).

Q5: What is the fee for a Canadian Criminal Rehabilitation application?

Because DUI is serious criminality under Bill C-46, the IRCC government processing fee is $1,000 CAD (approximately $750 USD).

Q6: Can I apply for a TRP directly at the border?

Yes, US citizens can apply for a TRP in person at a land port of entry, though approval is at the sole discretion of the CBSA officer.

Q7: Does a wet reckless charge make me inadmissible to Canada?

Yes, reckless driving involving alcohol equivalency is treated similarly to impaired driving under the Canadian Criminal Code.

Q8: Can I transit through a Canadian airport with a DUI?

Generally no; even transit flights touching Canadian soil require passing through Canadian customs where inadmissibility rules apply.
