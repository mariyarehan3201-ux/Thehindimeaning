Can You Sue a Driver in Michigan for an Accident?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, you can sue a driver in Michigan for a car accident, but Michigan's unique no-fault insurance system restricts lawsuits to specific legal categories. You can sue the at-fault driver for vehicle damage up to $3,000 under the Michigan Mini-Tort law, and you can sue for pain and suffering only if your injuries cross the statutory threshold of Serious Impairment of Body Function.

The Strict Architecture of Michigan No-Fault Insurance

Michigan operates the most distinctive and legally protective automobile insurance system in the United States. Governed by the Michigan No-Fault Automobile Insurance Act (MCL § 500.3101 et seq.), the system was fundamentally engineered to eliminate protracted, contentious courtroom litigation over routine fender benders. When a collision occurs on Michigan roadways, the default legal rule is that each driver's own insurance company covers their own medical bills, wage loss, and replacement services, completely regardless of who caused the crash.

Historically, Michigan provided mandatory lifetime uncapped medical Personal Injury Protection (PIP) benefits. Following landmark legislative reforms enacted in 2019, Michigan drivers can now select from tiered PIP medical coverage limits (ranging from $50,000 for Medicaid recipients up to unlimited lifetime medical care). Because your own PIP policy pays 100 percent of your reasonable and necessary medical treatment and three years of lost wages, state law severely limits your ability to file a traditional third-party personal injury lawsuit against the at-fault driver.

However, Michigan law does not provide complete immunity to reckless drivers. The statute carves out two primary legal pathways where an injured victim can step outside the no-fault system and sue the negligent driver: third-party tort claims for non-economic damages (pain and suffering) and property damage claims under the Michigan Mini-Tort statute. The table below outlines how Michigan insurance law divides claims between first-party no-fault and third-party tort lawsuits.

Crossing the Threshold: Serious Impairment of Body Function

To successfully sue an at-fault driver for non-economic damages—such as physical pain, mental anguish, physical disability, and loss of life enjoyment—Michigan law requires an injured plaintiff to satisfy a stringent statutory threshold established under MCL § 500.3135(1).

Under the statute, an injured person can recover non-economic damages only if their injuries resulted in: Death, Permanent Serious Disfigurement, or a Serious Impairment of Body Function. Following the landmark Michigan Supreme Court ruling in McCormick v. Carrier, the definition of a Serious Impairment of Body Function requires meeting a three-prong objective legal test.

First, the impairment must be objectively manifested—meaning it is medically demonstrable through clinical imaging (such as an MRI showing herniated discs or X-rays showing broken bones) rather than merely subjective patient complaints of pain. Second, it must involve an important body function (such as walking, lifting, using hands, or cognitive thinking). Third, the impairment must affect the plaintiff's general ability to lead their normal life, meaning it influences the trajectory of their daily living, employment, or recreation. Suing for Vehicle Damage: The Michigan Mini-Tort Law

Under Michigan no-fault rules, standard vehicle collision damage is not paid by the at-fault driver's liability insurance. Instead, vehicle owners must purchase collision coverage on their own policy to fix their car.

However, the Michigan Mini-Tort law (MCL § 500.3135(3)(e)) provides a vital exception. Under this provision, an innocent driver whose vehicle was damaged by a negligent motorist can sue the at-fault driver for up to $3,000 to cover out-of-pocket vehicle damage expenses, such as reimbursing your collision deductible or paying for cosmetic repairs if you carried only broad or basic collision coverage. Mini-tort claims are typically settled directly through the at-fault driver's insurer or filed in local Small Claims District Court.

How to File a Lawsuit Against a Driver in Michigan

Step-by-step procedure to file a third-party bodily injury lawsuit and mini-tort claim under Michigan no-fault law.

  1. File First-Party PIP Claim with Your Own Insurer

    Open an immediate no-fault PIP claim with your auto insurance company to ensure medical bills and 85% of lost wages are paid promptly.

  2. Undergo High-Field MRI Imaging for Objective Proof

    Obtain objective diagnostic imaging (MRI, CT) to establish that your spinal or joint injuries are objectively manifested under Michigan law.

  3. Document Changes to Your Normal Pre-Accident Life

    Maintain detailed documentation from physicians and employers showing how your injuries altered your ability to work and enjoy life.

  4. Submit a Mini-Tort Demand and File Third-Party Lawsuit

    Send a $3,000 mini-tort demand for your collision deductible and have an attorney file a third-party tort complaint against the at-fault driver.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sue for pain and suffering in Michigan after a car accident?

Yes, but only if your injuries satisfy the statutory threshold: death, permanent serious disfigurement, or serious impairment of body function.

Q2: What is the Michigan Mini-Tort limit?

The Michigan Mini-Tort statute allows innocent drivers to recover up to $3,000 from the at-fault driver for vehicle collision damages and deductibles.

Q3: What is the McCormick v. Carrier standard in Michigan?

A Michigan Supreme Court ruling establishing that an injury qualifies as a serious impairment if it is objectively manifested and affects your normal life.

Q4: Does Michigan no-fault insurance pay for car repairs?

No. PIP covers bodily injury medical bills and lost wages; vehicle damage is covered by your own collision policy or the $3,000 mini-tort law.

Q5: Can I sue an out-of-state driver in Michigan?

Yes. An out-of-state driver operating a non-registered vehicle in Michigan can be sued under broader tort rules without threshold restrictions.

Q6: How long do I have to file a car accident lawsuit in Michigan?

Under MCL § 600.5805, the statute of limitations for third-party personal injury lawsuits in Michigan is three years from the date of the collision.

Q7: What happens if I was partially at fault in Michigan?

Michigan follows modified comparative negligence (51% bar). You can recover non-economic damages as long as you were not more than 50% at fault.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

You can sue a driver in Michigan for an accident, but doing so requires navigating the strict boundaries of Michigan's no-fault legal system. While routine medical bills and lost wages are paid automatically by your own PIP insurer, you have the full legal right to sue the at-fault driver for up to $3,000 under the Mini-Tort statute, and recover substantial pain and suffering damages if your medical imaging proves a Serious Impairment of Body Function under the McCormick test.
