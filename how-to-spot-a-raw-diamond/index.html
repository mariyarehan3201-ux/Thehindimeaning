How to Spot a Raw Diamond?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Searching for raw, uncut diamonds in nature—whether prospecting along alluvial gravel riverbeds, sifting through kimberlite pipes at Arkansas's Crater of Diamonds State Park, or evaluating rough mineral specimens—is an exhilarating pursuit. However, contrary to the dazzling, fire-filled brilliance of a polished brilliant-cut engagement ring, a raw diamond in its natural state often resembles an unassuming, greasy piece of rounded sea glass or common river quartz. Successfully identifying an authentic rough diamond requires understanding crystal geometry, adamantine luster, specific gravity, and mineralogical hardness.

The Mineralogy of Raw Diamonds: Crystal Habit and Adamantine Luster

Diamonds are composed of pure crystallized carbon formed under astronomical pressures exceeding seventy thousand atmospheres at temperatures surpassing two thousand degrees Fahrenheit deep within Earth's mantle. When brought to the surface through ancient volcanic kimberlite eruptions, raw diamonds crystallize into distinct geometric structures governed by the isometric (cubic) crystal system. Over seventy percent of natural raw diamonds form as clean, eight-sided Octahedrons (resembling two four-sided pyramids joined at their bases) or twelve-sided Dodecahedrons.

One of the most diagnostic visual features of a raw diamond is its distinctive Adamantine Luster. In mineralogy, adamantine describes a brilliant, oily, or greasy sheen that reflects light with extraordinary intensity even before polishing. When holding an uncut diamond in your hand, it feels strangely slippery or soapy to the touch, as if coated in a thin film of petroleum jelly. Furthermore, raw diamond crystal faces frequently display microscopic triangular growth markings known as 'trigons'—tiny, equilateral triangular indentations etched into the octahedral faces that are chemically unique to natural diamonds.

Field Tests: Mohs Hardness, Streak, and Water Drop Surface Tension

In the field, prospectors use simple non-destructive tests to separate genuine rough diamonds from common quartz crystals. The most famous property of diamond is its rating of 10.0 on the Mohs Hardness Scale. A diamond will easily scratch corundum (sapphire/ruby, hardness 9), topaz (hardness 8), and quartz (hardness 7). However, never use a hammer or attempt to crush a stone to test hardness; while diamond is the hardest substance against scratching, its four planes of perfect octahedral cleavage make it brittle; a sharp impact from a steel hammer will shatter a priceless raw diamond into dust.

A safe, definitive field check is the Streak Test. Rub the unknown mineral firmly across an unglazed white porcelain streak plate. True diamond is so hard that it will leave zero colored powder streak on the porcelain; instead, it will gouge a deep physical scratch into the ceramic tile. Another fascinating diagnostic property is Hydrophobic Surface Tension. Diamonds are naturally lipophilic (grease-attracting) and hydrophobic (water-repelling). If you place a tiny droplet of water on a clean raw diamond face with a toothpick, the water forms a high, tight, rounded bead that does not spread out, whereas on glass or quartz, the droplet immediately flattens and sheets.

Electronic Thermal Testers and Color Variations

The ultimate modern tool for mineral prospectors is an electronic handheld diamond selector pen. Diamonds possess the highest thermal conductivity of any known solid on Earth—conducting heat over four times faster than copper. Handheld thermal testers feature a copper probe tip that delivers a micro-pulse of heat; when placed against an authentic raw diamond, the stone rapidly draws heat away from the sensor, triggering an instant audible beep and lighting up the LED indicator.

Do not expect raw diamonds to always appear crystal clear. While colorless (white) diamonds are prized, rough diamonds frequently display natural body colors: warm honey yellows, chocolate browns, champagnes, faint greens, and frosty milky whites. Some raw stones are encased in a dark, opaque skin known as 'bort' or 'boart' that conceals a pristine gem-quality diamond within. If you suspect you have found a genuine raw diamond, store it safely in a padded gem jar and take it to a credentialed graduate gemologist (GIA GG) for formal microscopic verification.

How to Identify a Raw Diamond in Nature

A step-by-step gemological procedure to examine crystal shape, luster, hardness, and thermal properties to verify rough diamonds.

  1. Inspect Crystal Symmetry with a 10x Loupe

    Examine the specimen under a 10x jeweler's loupe, looking for 8-sided octahedral or 12-sided dodecahedral crystal habits with rounded edges.

  2. Search for Inverted Triangular Trigons

    Look closely at the crystal faces for microscopic, equilateral triangular growth marks (trigons), which are unique physical hallmarks of natural diamond.

  3. Assess Adamantine Greasy Luster

    Rub the stone between your fingers; authentic raw diamonds exhibit an intense adamantine brilliance paired with an unmistakable slippery, soapy texture.

  4. Perform the Hydrophobic Water Drop Test

    Place a tiny drop of water onto a clean, dry crystal face; true diamond repels water, forcing the droplet into a high, tight, rounded sphere.

  5. Verify Thermal Conductivity with a Diamond Tester

    Touch an electronic diamond tester probe to the rough surface; diamonds instantly draw heat away, triggering a positive audible detection alert.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What does a raw diamond look like when found in the ground?

A raw diamond typically looks like an eight-sided rounded pebble with a greasy, oily sheen, resembling smooth sea glass or unpolished yellowish quartz.

Q2: Can you scratch glass with a raw diamond?

Yes, diamond easily scratches glass (hardness 5.5), but so does common quartz (hardness 7.0), so the glass scratch test does not prove a stone is diamond.

Q3: Will a raw diamond break if you hit it with a hammer?

Yes! While diamond is the hardest mineral against scratching, it has perfect cleavage planes and is brittle; a hammer blow will shatter it completely.

Q4: What are trigons on a raw diamond?

Trigons are microscopic triangular indentation growth markings found on the crystal faces of natural rough diamonds formed deep in the earth's mantle.

Q5: What is the difference between raw quartz and a raw diamond?

Quartz forms 6-sided pointed prisms with glassy luster, while diamond forms 8-sided octahedrons with a distinct greasy, slippery adamantine luster.

Q6: Can electronic diamond testers test rough uncut stones?

Yes. Electronic thermal conductivity testers work on rough, unpolished diamond faces just as effectively as on polished faceted gemstones.

Q7: Where can the public hunt for diamonds in the United States?

Crater of Diamonds State Park in Murfreesboro, Arkansas, is an eroded volcanic kimberlite pipe where visitors can search for and keep raw diamonds.

Q8: What colors do raw diamonds come in?

Raw diamonds occur in transparent colorless, yellow, brown, champagne, grey, green, black, and rare pink hues depending on trace chemical nitrogen or boron.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Hunting for raw diamonds is a thrilling blend of geological science, outdoor exploration, and keen observation. By mastering the geometric symmetry of octahedral crystals, looking for that unmistakable greasy adamantine luster, verifying hydrophobic water beading, and testing thermal conductivity with electronic pens, you will confidently separate ordinary river gravel from nature's most extraordinary, billion-year-old crystalline treasures.
