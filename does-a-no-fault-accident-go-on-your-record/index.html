Does a No Fault Accident Go on Your Record?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 12, 2026

Being involved in an automobile collision through no fault of your own—such as being rear-ended while completely stopped at a red light—is an infuriating experience. After trading insurance information and filing an insurance claim, innocent drivers naturally assume that because they were blameless, the collision will not follow them. However, drivers frequently ask: does a no-fault accident go on your record? The answer is yes: a not-at-fault accident will appear on your driving record and insurance claims history, though its impact on your insurance premiums depends on state law.

MVR Records Versus Insurance CLUE Reports

To understand how a not-at-fault accident is tracked, one must distinguish between your state Motor Vehicle Record (MVR) and insurance claims databases. Your state Department of Motor Vehicles (DMV) maintains your public driving record (MVR). If police responded to the scene and filed an official collision report, the state DMV logs the crash incident on your MVR. However, because you were not at fault, zero moving violation points are assessed against your driver's license.

Concurrently, insurance companies report all claim activities to the Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (CLUE) database, maintained by LexisNexis. Whenever a claim is opened—even if your insurer subrogates against the other driver's policy and recovers 100 percent of repair costs—the claim is logged in CLUE for seven years with the designation 'Not-At-Fault Accident'. When shopping for auto insurance, prospective insurers review both your state MVR and CLUE report.

Can Your Insurance Rates Increase After a No-Fault Accident?

Whether your insurance carrier can legally raise your rates following a not-at-fault collision depends entirely on state insurance statutes. Several progressive states—including California, Oklahoma, and New York—have enacted explicit consumer protection statutes prohibiting auto insurance companies from raising premiums or applying surcharges for accidents where the policyholder was not principally at fault.

However, in states without protective statutes, insurance companies utilize sophisticated actuarial risk algorithms. Actuaries observe that drivers with multiple not-at-fault collisions within a three-year period (e.g., two rear-endings and a parking lot scrape) have a statistically higher likelihood of filing future comprehensive or collision claims. Insurers in these states may eliminate a 'loss-free discount', reclassify the driver into a higher tier, or increase baseline base rates upon policy renewal.

Navigating True No-Fault States: The PIP Factor

A frequent point of confusion is the legal concept of 'No-Fault Insurance' states (such as Michigan, New Jersey, Florida, and New York). In these jurisdictions, 'no-fault' refers specifically to medical injury coverage under Personal Injury Protection (PIP). Under PIP statutes, regardless of who caused the collision, each driver's medical bills and lost wages are paid directly by their own insurance carrier up to policy limits.

Even in a PIP no-fault state, fault is still legally determined for vehicle physical damage. The at-fault driver's property damage liability policy pays for your damaged car. When filing a PIP claim with your own carrier, the claim will show on your CLUE report as a 'No-Fault Medical PIP Claim', which underwriters evaluate when calculating your overall risk profile.

How to Protect Your Record After a No-Fault Accident in 5 Steps

Follow these five disciplined steps to ensure a not-at-fault collision is properly categorized on your driving and insurance records.

  1. Obtain a Clear Police Accident Report

    Ensure the investigating police officer clearly notes that the other driver committed the moving violation and was the sole proximate cause.

  2. File a Third-Party Claim with the At-Fault Insurer

    File your vehicle property damage claim directly through the at-fault party's insurer to avoid opening a first-party claim on your policy.

  3. Demand a Subrogation Status Letter If Using Collision

    If your own insurer pays repairs upfront, request a formal letter confirming they recovered 100% of funds from the at-fault carrier.

  4. Order Your Free Annual CLUE Report

    Request a copy of your personal auto loss history from LexisNexis to verify that the collision is explicitly coded as 'Not-At-Fault'.

  5. Dispute Erroneous Fault Coding Promptly

    If an insurer mistakenly coded the crash as at-fault on your CLUE report, submit a formal dispute with police reports to correct the data.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Will a no-fault accident give me points on my driver's license?

No, state DMVs only assess penalty points for moving traffic violations (like speeding or reckless driving), not for not-at-fault collisions.

Q2: How long does a no-fault accident stay on your record?

Not-at-fault accidents remain on state DMV records for 3 to 5 years and on insurance CLUE reports for 7 years.

Q3: Can I be dropped by insurance for too many not-at-fault accidents?

Yes, in many states, having three or more claims within three years—even if not at fault—can cause an insurer to decline policy renewal.

Q4: Does a parking lot hit-and-run count as a no-fault accident?

Yes, if your parked car is struck by an unknown driver, it is processed under collision or uninsured motorist coverage as a not-at-fault accident.

Q5: Why did my insurance go up after an accident that wasn't my fault?

In states without surcharge bans, insurers may remove your 'disappearing deductible' or 'claim-free discount', raising your net premium.

Q6: What is a subrogation letter?

A subrogation letter proves that your insurance company successfully recovered all repair costs and your deductible from the at-fault driver's insurer.

Q7: Can I see my LexisNexis CLUE report for free?

Yes, under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, you are legally entitled to one free disclosure copy of your CLUE auto report every 12 months.

Q8: Does California allow rate hikes for not-at-fault accidents?

No, California Proposition 103 strictly prohibits auto insurance carriers from increasing rates or surcharging for accidents where you were not principally at fault.
