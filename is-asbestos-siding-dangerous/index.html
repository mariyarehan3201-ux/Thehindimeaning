Is Asbestos Siding Dangerous?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Is asbestos siding dangerous is a common concern for owners of residential and commercial properties constructed between 1920 and the late 1970s. Asbestos siding shingles—commonly marketed under brand names such as Transite—are rigid, cement-bonded building products that pose virtually zero health hazard when intact, but become dangerous if broken, sawed, drilled, or pressure-washed.

Material Composition and the Non-Friable Matrix

To understand the risk profile of asbestos exterior siding, one must examine its material composition. Asbestos siding consists of Portland cement reinforced with twelve to twenty percent chrysotile or amosite asbestos fibers. In this manufacturing process, the asbestos fibers are tightly locked within a dense, hardened stone-like matrix.

Under environmental regulations established by the EPA and OSHA, intact asbestos cement siding is classified as a 'Category II non-friable' material. Non-friable materials cannot be crumbled, pulverized, or reduced to powder by hand pressure. Consequently, as long as siding shingles remain securely fastened to exterior walls in good structural condition, they do not release airborne fibers into ambient air.

When Asbestos Siding Becomes Hazardous

The primary danger of asbestos siding emerges when homeowners or contractors attempt uncontained remodeling, repair, or surface preparation. A widespread mistake is using a high-pressure power washer to clean siding before painting. High-pressure water jets scour the weathered cement surface, pulverizing the protective matrix and spraying a hazardous slurry of water and loose asbestos fibers across yards and driveways.

Similarly, using circular saws, reciprocating saws, or high-speed drills to cut holes for new windows, electrical conduits, or siding vents mechanically shatters the cement matrix. This power tool friction generates millions of microscopic, respirable asbestos fibers that float in the air for hours, presenting serious inhalation risks for workers and nearby residents.

Encapsulation vs Removal: Industry Best Practices

Major environmental agencies, including the EPA and state departments of environmental protection, strongly recommend managing intact asbestos siding in place rather than removing it. Complete removal is labor-intensive, expensive, and creates the very exposure hazards that proper stewardship avoids.

The most effective and widely adopted remediation strategy is siding encapsulation. Homeowners can safely install modern vinyl or fiber-cement siding directly over existing asbestos shingles by first applying a layer of rigid foam insulation board. This wraps the older siding in a permanent barrier, improves home energy efficiency, and eliminates the substantial expense and hazard of disposal.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is asbestos siding dangerous to live in a house with?

No. Intact asbestos siding in good condition is non-friable and does not emit airborne fibers. It poses virtually zero health danger to residents as long as it is left undisturbed.

Q2: Can you pressure wash asbestos siding?

Never pressure wash asbestos siding. The high-pressure stream erodes the cement matrix, dislodging millions of microscopic asbestos fibers that contaminate surrounding soil.

Q3: Can you put new vinyl siding over asbestos siding?

Yes. Encapsulation is an EPA-approved, cost-effective method. Installing rigid foam board directly over asbestos siding before applying vinyl siding seals fibers permanently.

Q4: How do you know if exterior siding contains asbestos?

Siding installed before 1978 that features rigid, brittle cement shingles (often measuring 12x24 inches with scalloped or wavy edges) likely contains 12% to 20% asbestos; lab testing confirms it.

Q5: Can you paint asbestos siding?

Yes. Painting asbestos siding with high-quality exterior acrylic or elastomeric paint encapsulates the cement surface, extends its lifespan, and prevents weathering.

Q6: What is Transite siding?

Transite was a prominent commercial brand name for asbestos-cement building materials manufactured by Johns-Manville, widely used for exterior siding and roofing.

Q7: How should broken asbestos shingles be repaired?

Broken shingles can be sealed with exterior caulking mastic, or gently replaced by carefully removing individual nails without shattering adjoining tiles.

Q8: How much does it cost to remove asbestos siding?

Professional asbestos siding removal typically costs between $7 and $15 per square foot, or $8,000 to $20,000 for an entire house, making encapsulation much more economical.
