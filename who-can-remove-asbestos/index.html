Who Can Remove Asbestos?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 24, 2026

Who can remove asbestos? In the majority of jurisdictions and commercial settings, asbestos removal must be performed exclusively by state-licensed asbestos abatement contractors employing certified technicians trained under EPA and OSHA guidelines. While some state regulations permit single-family homeowners to remove limited non-friable materials in their own primary residence, health and environmental agencies strongly recommend professional abatement for all hazardous remediation.

Professional Licensing and Certification Credentials

Removing asbestos legally and safely requires rigorous technical credentials that distinguish certified environmental contractors from general demolition trades. Under the EPA Model Accreditation Plan (MAP) established by the Asbestos Hazard Emergency Response Act (AHERA) and OSHA 29 CFR 1926.1101, individuals who physically handle asbestos materials must complete state-approved certification programs. Technicians complete a minimum thirty-two-hour hands-on training course, while site supervisors must complete a comprehensive forty-hour curriculum covering legal liabilities, air engineering, and emergency procedures.

In addition to individual worker licensing, remediation businesses must hold a dedicated Asbestos Abatement Contractor License issued by state departments of environmental protection or labor standards. Licensed contractors must demonstrate active comprehensive pollution liability insurance, maintain medical surveillance registries for all crew members, prove mandatory respiratory protection fit-testing, and adhere to strict hazardous waste manifesting procedures.

Homeowner DIY Exemptions vs. Regulatory Prohibitions

A frequent question among property owners is whether they can legally remove asbestos from their own home without hiring a contractor. Under federal EPA NESHAP rules, private single-family residential owner-occupied dwellings are generally exempt from federal commercial contractor mandates. However, state environmental statutes and local municipal bylaws frequently supersede federal exemptions. States such as Massachusetts, New Jersey, and California strictly prohibit homeowners from removing friable asbestos materials due to public health risks.

Even in states where DIY removal of non-friable materials (like exterior transite shingles or vinyl floor tiles) is technically permissible, homeowners face significant legal and practical roadblocks. Curbside sanitation services refuse hazardous mineral debris, and municipal landfills will not accept asbestos waste without an official hazardous waste shipment manifest. Furthermore, attempting DIY removal without negative air machines and decontamination airlocks often contaminates carpets, air ducts, and soft furnishings.

When selecting a qualified contractor, property owners should request copies of the firm’s state license and proof of asbestos-specific pollution liability insurance. Standard commercial general liability policies contain explicit asbestos exclusion clauses.

Finally, always hire an independent third-party industrial hygiene firm to conduct clearance testing. Allowing the removal contractor to inspect and clear their own work creates an inherent conflict of interest.

How to Vet and Hire a Qualified Asbestos Contractor

Step-by-step verification process to ensure your abatement contractor is legitimate.

  1. Verify State Contractor Licensing

    Check your state labor or environmental protection online registry to confirm the contractor holds an active, unblemished license.

  2. Confirm Asbestos Pollution Liability Insurance

    Request a Certificate of Insurance verifying active occurrence-based asbestos pollution liability coverage, not standard general liability.

  3. Review Itemized Containment Protocols

    Ensure the contractor's written proposal includes negative pressure HEPA filtration, decontamination chambers, and wet methods.

  4. Arrange Independent Third-Party Clearance

    Contract an independent industrial hygienist to conduct visual inspection and aggressive air testing before poly containment is dismantled.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I remove asbestos from my own home myself?

Federal law exempts single-family homes, but many state and city laws strictly ban DIY removal of friable asbestos due to contamination hazards.

Q2: What qualifications must an asbestos contractor have?

Contractors must hold a state abatement contractor license, employ EPA/AHERA-certified workers, and carry specialized pollution liability insurance.

Q3: Can a general handyman or plumber remove asbestos pipes?

No. General contractors, handymen, and plumbers cannot legally disturb or remove friable asbestos without specialized abatement licensing.

Q4: Why is DIY asbestos removal discouraged?

Without commercial HEPA air scrubbers and containment airlocks, DIY removal releases microscopic fibers that contaminate air ducts and living areas.

Q5: How do I check if an asbestos company is certified?

Search your state environmental protection agency or department of labor website for licensed asbestos abatement contractors.

Q6: Can landlords remove asbestos in rental properties?

No. Federal and state laws strictly classify rental properties as commercial facilities, requiring certified licensed contractors for all remediation.

Q7: What happens if someone illegally removes asbestos?

Illegal removal carries severe environmental penalties, civil fines exceeding $10,000 per day, stop-work orders, and substantial liability lawsuits.

Q8: Who performs the final air clearance test?

An independent third-party industrial hygienist or licensed project monitor must conduct the clearance air testing, not the removal contractor.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Who can remove asbestos? Only certified, licensed abatement professionals possess the engineering equipment, medical training, and legal waste disposal channels to abate hazardous fibers safely. Relying on professional contractors protects family health, prevents legal penalties, and preserves property value.
