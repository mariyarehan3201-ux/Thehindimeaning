Can a CPA Set Up an LLC?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Entrepreneurs launching a new venture frequently consult their Certified Public Accountant (CPA) for guidance on entity structure, tax classifications, and operational planning. While CPAs possess deep expertise in business taxation and accounting principles, their legal authority to form a limited liability company (LLC) is subject to strict statutory boundaries. Understanding what entity formation tasks a CPA can legally perform versus actions reserved exclusively for licensed corporate attorneys protects small business owners from procedural errors, governance voids, and the unauthorized practice of law.

When starting a new commercial enterprise or converting a sole proprietorship into an LLC, business owners naturally seek guidance on how to organize their entity efficiently. Because the limited liability company is the most versatile hybrid business structure in the United States, founders often assume their tax accountant can handle the entire formation process from inception to execution. After all, tax accountants regularly manage corporate ledgers, file annual returns, and prepare federal financial declarations.

However, forming an LLC is fundamentally a legal transaction governed by state corporate statutes rather than merely a financial filing. While Certified Public Accountants routinely assist with administrative filings and tax registrations, there is a distinct legal boundary known as the Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL). Crossing this threshold can expose accounting professionals to regulatory penalties and leave business founders with legally deficient organizational documents that fail to shield personal assets during commercial litigation.

The Scope of CPA Authority vs. Corporate Attorneys

In almost every state jurisdiction, an individual who is not a licensed attorney cannot provide bespoke legal advice, interpret contractual liabilities, or draft customized governance instruments on behalf of third parties. CPAs are licensed and regulated by state boards of accountancy to practice public accounting, perform audits, provide tax preparation, and offer general financial counseling. When requested to form an entity, an accountant's permissible scope depends heavily on whether the task is classified as ministerial administrative filing or substantive legal counsel.

Administrative tasks such as filing standard Articles of Organization with a state secretary of state, obtaining an Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the Internal Revenue Service via Form SS-4, and issuing a formal certified CPA letter confirming business operations fall well within standard professional accounting practice. Conversely, drafting tailored provisions for an operating agreement, defining fiduciary duties among managing members, or advising on piercing the corporate veil requires licensed legal representation. The Unauthorized Practice of Law (UPL) Doctrine in Entity Formation

The Unauthorized Practice of Law doctrine exists to protect the public from receiving deficient, unqualified legal representation that could jeopardize their financial well-being or legal rights. State bar associations actively enforce UPL statutes against non-lawyers who provide substantive legal interpretations. If a CPA attempts to draft customized indemnification clauses, non-compete covenants, or member expulsion mechanisms, they violate state legal ethics rules and their professional liability errors and omissions insurance may refuse to defend resulting malpractice claims.

For single-member LLCs with straightforward business operations, the legal risks are comparatively modest because there are no co-owners to engage in internal governance battles. In such cases, many founders utilize their CPA to file the initial articles and adopt a standard statutory operating agreement. However, as soon as multiple owners enter the enterprise, the complexity escalates exponentially. When disputes arise regarding capital contributions, distribution waterfalls, or removing a member from an LLC, generic or poorly drafted agreements can lead to costly court battles and personal liability exposure.

Tax Classifications, Entity Elections, and Financial Architecture

Where a CPA provides extraordinary value during LLC formation is in the strategic selection of federal and state tax classifications. By default, the IRS classifies a single-member LLC as a disregarded entity taxed as a sole proprietorship, while a multi-member LLC is taxed as a general partnership. Neither of these default classifications requires a separate entity tax return for corporate profits, as income passes directly to owner tax schedules.

However, high-earning small business owners frequently benefit from electing alternative corporate tax treatment. A CPA can run dynamic financial projections to determine whether filing IRS Form 2553 to elect S-Corporation status will yield meaningful self-employment tax savings through the prudent division of reasonable owner compensation and shareholder distributions. Alternatively, if the enterprise plans to retain significant earnings for research and equipment capital expenditures, filing IRS Form 8832 to elect C-Corporation taxation under Subchapter C might optimize overall tax liability. A proficient accountant also guides the new business owner through mandatory state tax registrations. This includes obtaining local sales and use tax permits, establishing state employer withholding accounts for payroll, registering for state unemployment tax systems, and complying with annual franchise tax reporting schedules. Neglecting these state-level compliance obligations can result in immediate administrative dissolution of the LLC by the secretary of state, stripping the founders of their liability protection.

Ultimately, small business owners should not view the choice between a CPA and a corporate attorney as an either-or proposition. The most resilient and legally sound business entities are created when founders adopt a collaborative model: engaging a qualified business attorney to draft airtight organizational and governance documents, while partnering with a CPA to design the tax architecture, configure general ledger accounting, and execute timely federal entity classification elections.

How to Coordinate Between a CPA and Attorney to Form an LLC

Follow these strategic legal and financial steps to properly divide responsibilities between your accountant and legal counsel when establishing a new limited liability company.

  1. Conduct Initial Tax and Financial Feasibility Modeling with Your CPA

    Schedule an exploratory consultation with your Certified Public Accountant to review your projected revenue, operational costs, anticipated profit margins, and owner compensation requirements. Your CPA will calculate the tax implications of default partnership or sole proprietorship taxation versus electing S-Corporation status to minimize self-employment tax burdens.

  2. File Articles of Organization with the Secretary of State

    Determine whether your CPA or legal counsel will submit the formal Articles of Organization or Certificate of Formation to your state corporate registry. Ensure the legal entity name complies with state naming conventions, designate a registered agent with a physical street address, and pay the mandatory state incorporation fee.

  3. Engage an Attorney to Draft a Custom LLC Operating Agreement

    Retain a licensed business attorney to draft a comprehensive, state-specific LLC Operating Agreement tailored to your business structure. Your legal counsel will formulate provisions governing voting rights, capital contribution requirements, profit and loss allocations, dispute resolution mechanisms, and member transfer restrictions.

  4. Obtain Your Federal Employer Identification Number (EIN)

    Authorize your CPA to act as your third-party designee to submit IRS Form SS-4 online to secure your business Employer Identification Number. This unique federal tax identifier is mandatory for opening commercial bank accounts, hiring employees, establishing merchant processing, and filing federal tax returns.

  5. Execute Tax Elections and Establish Formal Accounting Controls

    Have your CPA prepare and submit IRS Form 8832 or Form 2553 if an alternative corporate tax classification is advantageous for your business. Simultaneously, set up separate commercial bank accounts, implement dedicated bookkeeping software, and establish strict corporate record-keeping practices to prevent commingling of personal and business funds.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can a CPA draft an LLC operating agreement?

In general, a CPA cannot draft a customized LLC operating agreement from scratch for a client because drafting binding legal contracts that define member rights, fiduciary duties, and liability indemnification constitutes the practice of law. A CPA can provide general financial input or standard templates, but substantive legal drafting should always be performed by a licensed attorney.

Q2: Is it cheaper to have a CPA or an attorney form an LLC?

Having a CPA form an LLC is often more affordable because accountants generally charge standard administrative preparation fees ranging from $200 to $600 plus state filing fees. In contrast, corporate attorneys typically charge between $800 and $2,500 for entity formation packages that include comprehensive legal consultations and customized operating agreements.

Q3: What is the unauthorized practice of law regarding business formation?

The unauthorized practice of law occurs when a non-attorney, including an accountant or business consultant, gives specific legal advice regarding corporate rights, interprets statutory legal liabilities, or crafts bespoke legal instruments. While filing standard state forms is widely permitted as a ministerial service, providing legal counsel crosses into statutory UPL violations.

Q4: Can a CPA act as my LLC registered agent?

Yes, many accounting firms offer registered agent services if they maintain a continuous physical office address within the state where the LLC is formed and are available during regular business hours to accept formal service of process, tax notices, and official government correspondence on behalf of the company.

Q5: Do I need both a CPA and an attorney to start an LLC?

While not legally mandatory to hire both, utilizing both professionals provides the most robust protection for multi-member businesses. An attorney ensures your governance contracts prevent internal deadlocks and preserve your liability shield, while a CPA ensures your entity classification, accounting setup, and tax election filings minimize overall tax liability.

Q6: Can a CPA file IRS Form 2553 for S-Corp election on an LLC?

Yes, a CPA is fully authorized under Treasury Circular 230 to advise on, prepare, and file IRS Form 2553 to elect S-Corporation tax treatment for an eligible LLC. This is considered an official federal tax procedure that falls squarely within a certified accountant core professional competencies.

Q7: What happens if an LLC does not have an operating agreement?

If an LLC fails to adopt an operating agreement, the company is governed entirely by the default statutory rules of the state in which it was formed. These default statutes may dictate equal profit sharing regardless of capital contributed, require unanimous consent for routine decisions, or permit sudden judicial dissolution that could destabilize the business.

Q8: Can a CPA help me open a business bank account for my LLC?

A CPA cannot physically open the commercial bank account on your behalf, but they can assemble the mandatory financial documentation package, including your certified Articles of Organization, filed EIN confirmation letter, and an official CPA letter verifying business legitimacy, enabling you to open the account smoothly.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

While a Certified Public Accountant can legally execute the ministerial filing of Articles of Organization and file essential tax elections on your behalf, they cannot provide customized legal counsel or draft bespoke multi-member governance agreements without violating unauthorized practice of law regulations. For single-member enterprises seeking simple tax efficiency, working with a CPA offers a streamlined, cost-effective route to commercial launch. However, whenever multiple partners, complex equity divisions, or substantial liability exposures are involved, retaining a licensed corporate attorney alongside your CPA ensures your corporate veil remains impenetrable and your business interests remain fully protected.
