Defining an Open Title: The Mechanism of Title Jumping

In automotive sales, motor vehicle department (DMV) administration, and contract law, an open title refers to an official vehicle Certificate of Title that has been signed by the legal registered seller, but where the buyer information (name, address, purchase date, and sale price) has deliberately been left completely blank.

When a title is left open, the transaction remains legally invisible to the government. The individual selling you the vehicle is not the registered owner listed on the title; they bought the car from the original owner, had them sign the seller line, and skipped registering the car or paying sales tax. Selling a car with an open title is commonly known as title jumping, title skipping, or floating a title—and it is a misdemeanor or felony crime in all fifty United States.

Review how legitimate vehicle sales compare against illegal open title sales:

Why Unlicensed Curbsiders Jump Titles and The Risks to Buyers

The primary perpetrators of open title sales are unlicensed, black-market car flippers known in the automotive trade as curbsiders. By floating titles, curbsiders avoid registering the car in their name, avoid paying hundreds of dollars in state vehicle sales tax, and bypass state limits that require commercial dealer licenses for anyone selling more than four or five cars per year.

For the innocent buyer, purchasing an open title is a legal nightmare. When you take an open title to the DMV to register the vehicle, DMV examiners inspect ink signatures, handwriting styles, and dates. If the DMV flags the title as jumped, they will refuse to issue license plates or transfer ownership. If the original registered owner reported the car stolen, or if an unresolved bank loan lien exists against the vehicle, law enforcement can impound the car on the spot—leaving you with zero car and zero cash refund.

Always match the seller driver license directly against the name printed on the front of the title before handing over cash.

How to Protect Yourself and Verify a Clean Title Transfer

Follow this defensive buying checklist whenever purchasing a used vehicle privately.

  1. Demand Government Photo ID Matching the Title

    Ask to see the seller driver license; verify that the full legal name and street address match the front face of the Certificate of Title exactly.

  2. Beware the 'Selling for a Friend / Relative' Excuse

    If the seller claims they are selling the car for their uncle, brother, or cousin who is unavailable, walk away immediately; this is the hallmark of title jumping.

  3. Inspect the Back of the Title for Existing Signatures

    Ensure the seller signs the title in front of you; verify that no names are crossed out, whited out, or written in different color inks.

  4. Complete the Buyer Name and Purchase Date Immediately

    Fill in your full legal name, current address, odometer reading, and agreed purchase price in ink before leaving the meeting.

  5. Complete the Transaction at a Local DMV or Notary

    Meet the seller directly at a local DMV branch or licensed title agency to complete the legal title transfer and registration on the spot.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What is an open title?

An open title is a vehicle title signed by the seller where the buyer name and information are deliberately left blank, allowing illegal title jumping.

Q2: Is buying a car with an open title illegal?

While the buyer is often a victim, selling an open title (title jumping) is illegal in all 50 states, classified as misdemeanor or felony fraud.

Q3: Why do people jump titles?

Unlicensed car flippers jump titles to evade paying state sales taxes and registration fees, and to bypass laws requiring auto dealer licenses.

Q4: What happens if I try to register an open title at the DMV?

If the DMV detects the seller on the title is not the person who sold you the car, they will reject the title transfer and refuse to issue plates.

Q5: Can an open title car be reported stolen?

Yes. If the original owner disputes the sale or claims they never received payment, police can seize the vehicle from you as stolen property.

Q6: What should I do if a seller offers me an open title?

Refuse to buy the car unless the seller first titles the vehicle legally in their own name and pays their back taxes before selling it to you.

Q7: Can you fix a jumped title?

You must either track down the original registered owner to issue a fresh bill of sale, or apply for an expensive Surety Bonded Title through the state.

Q8: What is a bonded title?

A bonded title is a title backed by a surety bond purchased by the buyer, establishing ownership when the original title chain was broken or lost.
