Violate a Protective Order in Texas

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

If you violate a protective order in Texas, you face immediate mandatory arrest without a warrant and criminal prosecution under Texas Penal Code § 25.07. A first-time violation is a Class A misdemeanor punishable by up to one year in county jail and fines up to $4,000. It is upgraded to a third-degree felony carrying up to 10 years in state prison for repeat violations or continuous contact.

Texas Statutory Framework: Protective Orders vs Restraining Orders

In the State of Texas, legal orders restricting contact between individuals fall into distinct categories that carry profoundly different criminal consequences. A civil Temporary Restraining Order (TRO) issued during a contested divorce or property dispute is primarily enforced through civil contempt hearings before a family court judge.

In contrast, a formal Protective Order issued under Title 4 of the Texas Family Code or Article 17.292 of the Texas Code of Criminal Procedure is a criminal-enforcement court order. Whether issued as a Magistrate's Order for Emergency Protection (MOEP) following a domestic violence arrest, a Sexual Assault Protective Order, or a permanent Family Violence Protective Order, violating any term is an immediate state crime.

Under Texas law, the protected person cannot 'waive' or 'consent' to contact. Even if the protected individual invites the restrained party over for dinner, sends affectionate text messages, or requests reconciliation, the restrained person commits a separate criminal offense the instant they arrive or respond. Only a formal written modification order signed by a presiding Texas judge can legally alter protective order terms. The table below delineates the statutory classifications and penalties under Texas Penal Code § 25.07.

Mandatory Warrantless Arrest Under Texas Criminal Procedure

One of the most consequential legal mechanisms surrounding Texas protective orders is the statutory mandate placed upon Texas peace officers. Under Article 14.03(b) of the Texas Code of Criminal Procedure, law enforcement officers do not possess discretion when responding to alleged protective order violations.

The statute explicitly dictates: 'A peace officer shall arrest, without a warrant, a person the peace officer has probable cause to believe has committed an offense under Section 25.07, Penal Code.' If a protected person calls 911, shows responding police officers a valid certified copy of a protective order, and demonstrates that the restrained person is present in the residence, sitting in a parked car across the street, or sending documented text messages, the officers must immediately handcuff and arrest the suspect on the spot.

Following arrest, the defendant is transported directly to the county jail for magistration. In Texas county courts, magistrates routinely impose strict no-bond holds or set exceptionally high cash bonds with mandatory electronic GPS ankle monitor conditions, house arrest restrictions, and mandatory weapon surrender orders under federal 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8). Collateral Consequences: Gun Rights, Immigration, and Record Clearing

Beyond the immediate prospect of county jail or state prison sentences, a conviction for violating a protective order in Texas unleashes devastating long-term collateral consequences across professional, personal, and constitutional domains.

Under Federal Gun Control legislation (18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(8)), any person subject to a qualifying domestic violence protective order is stripped of their Second Amendment rights; possessing a single firearm or round of ammunition constitutes a separate federal felony punishable by up to 15 years in federal prison. For non-U.S. citizens—including lawful permanent residents (green card holders) and visa holders—violating a protective order is classified under federal immigration law (8 U.S.C. § 1227(a)(2)(E)(ii)) as a mandatory deportable offense with zero eligibility for waivers.

Furthermore, under the Texas Code of Criminal Procedure, convictions or deferred adjudications for offenses involving family violence or protective order breaches are strictly ineligible for nondisclosure or criminal record expunction. The conviction remains permanently on your public criminal history, barring you from state professional licensing, law enforcement careers, healthcare employment, and rental housing.

How to Defend Against Protective Order Violation Allegations

Critical legal defense protocol if accused of violating a Texas protective order.

  1. Cease All Direct and Third-Party Contact Instantly

    Never call, text, or use social media to reach the protected individual; do not ask mutual acquaintances or family members to deliver messages.

  2. Exercise Your Constitutional Right to Remain Silent

    Do not explain your side to investigating police or detectives; statements made will be recorded and used as confessions in court.

  3. Preserve Digital Alibis and Location Telematics

    Save smartphone Google Maps timeline data, toll road transponder records, dashcam footage, and work timecards to prove location.

  4. Retain a Dedicated Texas Criminal Defense Attorney

    Hire an attorney experienced in Texas family violence litigation to challenge lack of proper legal service and negotiate bond terms.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I be arrested if the protected person invited me over?

Yes. The protected party cannot legally grant permission or modify a court order. You will still be arrested and charged with a crime.

Q2: Is violating a protective order a felony in Texas?

A first violation is typically a Class A misdemeanor, but it is upgraded to a third-degree felony if you have prior convictions or commit an assault.

Q3: Can Texas police arrest you without a warrant for this?

Yes. Under Texas Code of Criminal Procedure Art. 14.03(b), peace officers are legally mandated to make a warrantless arrest upon probable cause.

Q4: Can I own a gun while under a Texas protective order?

No. Possessing firearms while subject to a protective order violates both Texas state law and federal law (18 U.S.C. 922(g)(8)).

Q5: What is continuous violation of a protective order in Texas?

Under Texas Penal Code 25.072, violating an order two or more times within 12 months is a continuous violation and a third-degree felony.

Q6: Can a protective order violation be expunged in Texas?

No. Convictions for domestic violence and protective order violations are permanently barred from expunction or non-disclosure under Texas law.

Q7: How long does a Texas protective order last?

Orders typically last up to two years, but judges can grant protective orders lasting up to a lifetime in cases involving severe felony violence.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Violating a protective order in Texas is a severe criminal offense that triggers mandatory warrantless arrest, bond revocation, and harsh county jail or state prison sentences. Never assume informal texts or invitations from the protected person make contact legal; only a judge can modify court orders. If you are accused of violating an order, exercise your constitutional right to remain silent, avoid all direct and indirect contact, and retain an experienced Texas criminal defense trial lawyer immediately.
