How Do I File a Claim Against an Estate?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how do i file a claim against an estate is critical for creditors, medical providers, business partners, or family lenders who are owed money by an individual who has passed away. When a debtor dies, their financial obligations do not simply disappear; instead, unpaid liabilities transfer to their legal probate estate. To recover valid debts, claimants must navigate strict statutory deadlines, formal probate court filings, and documentation standards, or face statutory forfeiture of their lawful claims.

Probate Jurisdiction, Creditor Classification, and Statutory Deadlines

When an individual passes away, their assets and debts enter the probate process administered by the probate or surrogates court in the county where the decedent legally resided. Once the court appoints a personal representative, executor, or administrator, that fiduciary is legally required to publish a Notice to Creditors in a local newspaper and send direct written notices to all known or reasonably ascertainable creditors.

Filing deadlines—known as non-claim statutes—are unforgiving. Depending on state law, creditors generally have between three and six months from the date of the first published notice (or thirty to sixty days from the date of direct mail receipt) to file a verified claim. If a creditor fails to formally present their claim within this strict statutory window, the debt is permanently barred by law, regardless of whether the contract was legally ironclad.

Review the statutory creditor priority ranking during probate estate settlement:

Class 6: General Unsecured Debt Credit cards, personal loans, vendor invoices Paid last among creditors Frequently discharged with zero or partial payout

Drafting and Filing the Formal Statement of Claim

To submit a legally enforceable claim, creditors cannot simply mail a standard monthly invoice or send an email to the grieving family. Most probate courts require a specific judicial form, often titled Statement of Claim or Creditors Claim Against Estate. This document must be signed under penalty of perjury and clearly state the exact monetary principal owed, applicable interest rates, the legal origin of the debt, and any existing payment offsets.

Crucially, the Statement of Claim must be accompanied by comprehensive corroborating evidence. This includes signed promissory notes, formal contracts, itemized medical billing statements, delivery receipts, or court judgments. The completed package must be officially filed with the clerk of the probate court where the estate is open, and a formal copy must be served upon the personal representative or their attorney of record via certified mail with return receipt requested.

Executor Review, Claim Allowance, Rejection, and Litigation

Once the personal representative receives your claim, they conduct an audit to verify its legitimacy. The executor has three legal choices: allow the claim in full, allow it in part, or formally reject it. If the claim is allowed and the estate holds sufficient liquid assets, payment is issued during the final distribution phase of probate, following satisfaction of higher-priority tax and administrative obligations.

If the executor rejects your claim—whether due to lack of documentation, suspicion of fraud, or dispute over charges—they will file a formal Notice of Rejection of Claim. Receiving a rejection notice triggers a very narrow window (frequently only 30 to 90 days depending on the state) for the creditor to file an independent civil lawsuit against the estate in court. If you fail to file a lawsuit within that post-rejection timeframe, the claim is permanently extinguished.

How to File a Formal Claim Against a Probate Estate

Follow these five legal steps to document, file, and recover unpaid debts from a decedents estate.

  1. Locate Open Probate Court Case

    Contact the probate court clerk in the county where the decedent lived to find the open estate docket and executor details.

  2. Verify Non-Claim Statutory Deadlines

    Determine the exact filing cutoff date based on published notice dates or state absolute bar dates.

  3. Prepare the Formal Statement of Claim Form

    Complete the court-approved creditor claim form detailing the debt amount, origin, and itemized calculations.

  4. Attach Corroborating Legal Evidence

    Include signed contracts, invoices, proof of partial payments, loan promissory notes, and ledger statements.

  5. File with the Court and Serve the Executor

    File the original documents with the probate clerk and send certified copies to the estates personal representative.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What happens if I miss the creditor claim deadline in probate?

If you fail to file within the statutory window, your claim is permanently barred, and you forfeit all rights to collect the debt.

Q2: Are children or relatives responsible for paying a deceased parent's debt?

No, family members are generally not personally liable for a decedent's debts unless they co-signed the loan or shared joint account liability.

Q3: What if the estate does not have enough money to pay all creditors?

The estate is declared insolvent; claims are paid strictly according to statutory priority rankings, and lower-tier unsecured debts are discharged unpaid.

Q4: Can I file a claim if probate has not been opened yet?

Yes, in many jurisdictions, an interested creditor can petition the probate court to open administration and appoint an administrator.

Q5: How long does it take to get paid after filing an estate claim?

Payments are typically distributed at the conclusion of probate, which often takes 6 to 18 months depending on estate complexity.

Q6: Can a creditor place a lien on probate property?

Unsecured creditors cannot directly place liens during probate; they must file a probate claim and receive court-ordered distribution.

Q7: Does life insurance money pay for estate creditor claims?

Generally no; life insurance policies with designated living beneficiaries bypass probate and are protected from estate creditors.

Q8: What fee is required to file a creditor claim in probate court?

Court filing fees vary by county, typically ranging between $20 and $100 depending on local probate fee schedules.
