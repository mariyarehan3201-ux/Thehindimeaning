How Long Does a Malpractice Lawsuit Take?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how long does a malpractice lawsuit take is an essential expectation-setting inquiry for injured patients and grieving families pursuing civil justice for catastrophic medical errors. Unlike simple automobile collision claims that settle in a few months, medical malpractice litigation is among the most contentious, procedurally complex, and protracted areas of civil law. On average, a medical malpractice lawsuit takes between 2 and 5 years from initial consultation to final settlement or jury verdict.

The Pre-Suit Investigation and Certificate of Merit Phase (6 to 12 Months)

The timeline begins long before a formal summons and complaint are filed in civil court. Reputable medical malpractice law firms spend 6 to 12 months conducting rigorous pre-suit investigations. This phase involves requesting, collecting, and indexing thousands of pages of hospital charts, nursing notes, surgical logs, anesthesia records, and digital imaging archives (CT, MRI, X-rays).

Furthermore, in almost every US state, medical malpractice plaintiffs are statutorily required to obtain an 'Affidavit of Merit' or 'Certificate of Good Faith' prior to filing suit. The law firm must locate and retain independent, board-certified medical expert witnesses practicing in the exact same medical specialty as the defendant doctor (e.g., a board-certified neurosurgeon to review a spine surgery error). Retaining medical experts, having them analyze charts, and drafting formal expert opinions requires months of preparation.

The Discovery Phase: Depositions and Battle of the Experts (12 to 24 Months)

Once the formal lawsuit is filed, the case enters the Discovery Phase, which represents the longest leg of the litigation journey, typically consuming 12 to 24 months. During discovery, both sides exchange exhaustive written interrogatories, hospital incident reports, and internal peer-review documents.

The critical core of discovery is oral depositions taken under oath. Defense counsel will depose the injured plaintiff and family members for full days, scrutinizing their physical limitations, pre-existing medical conditions, and daily pain levels. In turn, plaintiff attorneys depose every doctor, resident, nurse, and hospital administrator involved in the procedure, followed by exhaustive depositions of retained medical expert witnesses on both sides. Coordinating schedules between busy practicing surgeons and court reporters adds substantial delays.

The 'Consent to Settle' Clause and Trial Realities

A primary reason medical malpractice cases take so long to resolve compared to standard personal injury claims is the 'Consent to Settle' clause found in physician professional liability insurance policies. Unlike auto insurance companies that can settle claims over the driver's objection, medical malpractice policies frequently require the physician's explicit written consent to settle.

Because a malpractice settlement or verdict must be reported to the National Practitioner Data Bank (NPDB) and state medical licensing boards—damaging the doctor's reputation and hospital privileges—physicians aggressively demand to fight cases in court. Over 80% of malpractice cases that proceed all the way to a jury verdict result in defense verdicts for the doctor. Consequently, plaintiff attorneys must prepare every case with extreme thoroughness, ensuring liability is undeniable before insurers will consider multi-million-dollar settlements.

How to Navigate a Medical Malpractice Lawsuit Timeline

Follow these five strategic steps to manage expectations and assist your legal team.

  1. Act Promptly to Protect the Statute of Limitations

    Contact a specialized malpractice lawyer within months of injury to allow time for expert reviews.

  2. Obtain Complete Medical Records Immediately

    Request electronic health records (EHR) and audit trail metadata directly from all treating facilities.

  3. Reach Maximum Medical Improvement (MMI)

    Complete surgeries and rehabilitation so economists can project accurate lifetime medical costs.

  4. Participate Fully in Deposition Preparation

    Spend days preparing with your legal team to ensure your sworn deposition testimony is precise and calm.

  5. Weigh Settlement Offers Against Trial Risks

    Evaluate mediated structured settlement offers against the risk of an unpredictable jury trial verdict.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Why do medical malpractice lawsuits take so long?

They involve thousands of medical records, multiple physician depositions, mandatory expert affidavits, and physician consent-to-settle clauses.

Q2: What is the average settlement payout time for malpractice?

Most successful cases settle during mediation after discovery is completed, typically between 18 and 36 months from filing.

Q3: Do most medical malpractice cases go to trial?

No, roughly 90% of filed malpractice cases settle out of court or are dismissed; only about 5% to 10% proceed to a full jury trial.

Q4: What is an Affidavit of Merit?

A sworn legal certificate signed by an independent doctor confirming that the defendant breached medical standards of care, required to file suit.

Q5: Can a doctor refuse to settle a malpractice case?

Yes, many physician insurance policies contain 'Consent to Settle' clauses allowing doctors to refuse settlements and force a jury trial.

Q6: What percentage of malpractice trials do doctors win?

Nationally, juries rule in favor of doctors and hospitals in approximately 80% to 85% of medical malpractice trials.

Q7: How much does it cost to litigate a medical malpractice case?

Law firms standardly front $50,000 to $200,000+ in medical expert witness fees, depositions, and exhibit costs on a contingency basis.

Q8: What happens if the defense appeals a verdict?

If the defense appeals a plaintiff jury verdict, the case enters state appellate court, adding another 12 to 24 months before funds are paid.
