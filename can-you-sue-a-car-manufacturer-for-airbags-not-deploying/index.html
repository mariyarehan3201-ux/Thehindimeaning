Can You Sue a Car Manufacturer for Airbags Not Deploying

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Yes, an injured driver or vehicle passenger can sue an automotive manufacturer if an airbag fails to deploy during a serious collision, provided the crash met the vehicle calibrated deployment thresholds and the non-deployment caused or worsened physical injuries. These complex legal claims are litigated under automotive products liability law and the crashworthiness doctrine, alleging design defects, manufacturing errors, defective crash sensors, faulty wiring harnesses, or failures to issue timely recall warnings regarding known safety restraint system defects.

Products Liability and the Automotive Crashworthiness Doctrine

When an individual suffers devastating bodily injury in a motor vehicle collision, immediate legal scrutiny typically focuses on the negligent driver who caused the impact. However, when passive vehicle safety restraint systems fail to function as engineered, an injured claimant may possess a formidable cause of action directly against the automotive manufacturer under products liability law. Central to these civil claims is the automotive crashworthiness doctrine, also recognized in civil jurisprudence as the secondary impact or enhanced injury doctrine.

Under the crashworthiness doctrine, automakers possess a strict legal duty to design, engineer, and manufacture motor vehicles that provide reasonable occupant protection against foreseeable collision forces. In any crash, two distinct impacts occur: the primary collision involving the vehicle striking another automobile or stationary barrier, and the secondary collision involving vehicle occupants striking interior structural surfaces, including the steering column, dashboard, windshield, or side door pillars. While an automaker is not responsible for initiating the primary collision, the manufacturer is held strictly liable if a defect in the supplemental restraint system fails to mitigate the secondary collision, causing catastrophic injuries that would have been prevented had the safety systems deployed properly.

Litigating an airbag failure lawsuit requires establishing that the vehicle suffered from an actionable defect that was the proximate cause of enhanced physical trauma. Products liability law recognizes three primary defect categories: design defects (flaws in the system engineering, algorithms, or sensor layout that make the entire vehicle model line unreasonably dangerous), manufacturing defects (departures from intended specifications during assembly, such as defective electrical soldering or contaminated gas propellant canisters), and failure to warn (automaker knowledge of component failure rates without providing adequate notices or executing timely recalls). When Airbags Should and Should Not Deploy During Collisions

A frequent defense raised by corporate automotive legal teams is that the airbag system functioned exactly as engineered by withholding deployment. Airbags are not designed to deploy in every vehicular accident. Supplemental restraint systems are sophisticated pyrotechnic devices engineered to deploy exclusively in moderate-to-severe crashes where seatbelts alone cannot prevent life-threatening blunt force trauma. Because an inflating airbag strikes an occupant at speeds approaching one hundred to two hundred miles per hour, deploying in minor low-speed bumps could inflict severe facial fractures, eye injuries, or concussions without providing protective utility, a mechanical distinction explored when analyzing sensor mechanics during rear-end collisions.

The decision to deploy an airbag is governed by an onboard microcomputer known as the Airbag Electronic Control Module (ACM), or Sensing and Diagnostic Module (SDM). The ACM continuously processes electrical telemetry transmitted by multi-axis accelerometer sensors located throughout the vehicle front bumper, fenders, passenger compartment floorboards, and door pillars. When decelerative G-forces match pre-programmed threshold curves equivalent to striking a solid concrete wall at twelve to sixteen miles per hour, the ACM sends a high-voltage electrical pulse through a squib wire, igniting chemical propellant pellets that generate nitrogen gas to inflate the cushion within twenty to thirty milliseconds.

However, engineering failures frequently compromise this precision sequence. Automotive investigations by the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) have documented cases where frontal sensors were improperly positioned behind crush zones that absorbed energy without triggering sensor thresholds, where defective clock-spring wiring inside the steering wheel column severed electrical continuity, or where moisture intrusion corroded sensor microchips. In such scenarios, catastrophic non-deployment leaves vehicle occupants unprotected against high-speed secondary impacts. Electronic Data Recorders, Evidence Preservation, and NHTSA Recalls

Winning an airbag failure lawsuit against a multibillion-dollar multinational automaker requires objective digital and physical proof. The single most pivotal piece of evidence in modern crashworthiness litigation is the vehicle Event Data Recorder (EDR), commonly known as the automotive black box. Built into the ACM, the EDR captures and freezes critical operational metrics recorded in the five to ten seconds immediately preceding and during the impact: vehicle travel velocity, engine throttle percentage, brake application status, seatbelt buckle latch engagement, steering input angles, and longitudinal and lateral delta-V deceleration curves.

Downloading this encrypted telemetry requires specialized commercial imaging hardware, such as the Bosch Crash Data Retrieval (CDR) tool. If the EDR readout confirms that the collision delta-V exceeded the manufacturer published deployment thresholds while the vehicle was traveling at speed with seatbelts fastened, yet the ACM issued zero deployment firing commands, the plaintiff legal team secures compelling objective proof of safety system failure. Conversely, plaintiffs must also understand the legal stakes and potential exposure associated with complex tort actions, as discussed in what happens if you lose a car accident lawsuit.

Immediate evidence preservation is essential. In the chaotic aftermath of a severe crash, insurance adjusters routinely declare damaged vehicles a total loss, hauling them to salvage auction yards where they are crushed, parted out, or sold for scrap. If the automobile is destroyed before an independent forensic inspection can take place, the legal claim is irreparably compromised under the legal doctrine of spoliation of evidence. An injured victim or their attorney must immediately dispatch a formal legal spoliation demand letter to insurance carriers and towing yards, securing private indoor storage to preserve the vehicle in its pristine post-crash condition.

How to Pursue a Defective Airbag Claim Against an Automobile Manufacturer

A comprehensive step-by-step litigation roadmap covering vehicle impoundment preservation, black box data imaging, engineering inspections, and strict liability complaint filing.

  1. Secure and Preserve the Crash Vehicle Immediately

    Prevent insurance carriers or salvage auctions from moving, parting out, or destroying the vehicle by issuing formal legal spoliation letters and storing the car in secure indoor storage.

  2. Download the Event Data Recorder (EDR) Black Box Telemetry

    Retain an accredited accident reconstructionist to connect certified Bosch CDR diagnostic hardware to the vehicle ACM to extract pre-crash velocity and deceleration delta-V telemetry.

  3. Conduct a Forensic Vehicle Teardown and Sensor Inspection

    Coordinate a joint formal physical inspection with automotive component engineers to inspect wiring harnesses, clock-springs, diagnostic trouble codes, and crush sensor integrity.

  4. Commission a Biomechanical Enhanced Injury Analysis

    Hire a biomechanical engineering expert to review medical diagnostic scans and compare sustained trauma against injuries that would have occurred had the airbag deployed properly.

  5. Search Federal Safety Databases for NHTSA Recalls and Complaints

    Investigate federal safety records, Technical Service Bulletins (TSBs), and NHTSA defect investigations for similar airbag non-deployment reports on the same vehicle model year.

  6. File a Strict Products Liability and Crashworthiness Lawsuit

    Have experienced automotive defect trial attorneys draft and file a formal civil complaint asserting design defect, manufacturing failure, and failure to warn causes of action.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sue if the airbag did not deploy in a low-speed accident?

Generally, no. Airbags are engineered with specific deceleration thresholds and are intentionally programmed not to deploy in minor low-speed impacts, typically below eight to twelve miles per hour, because deploying could cause unnecessary facial burns and concussions.

Q2: What is the crashworthiness doctrine in an airbag failure lawsuit?

The crashworthiness doctrine holds motor vehicle manufacturers strictly liable if a design or manufacturing defect in the vehicle fails to protect occupants from secondary impacts, causing enhanced injuries beyond what the primary collision would have inflicted.

Q3: Does my insurance company declaring the car a total loss hurt my airbag lawsuit?

It will completely destroy your lawsuit if the vehicle is dismantled or crushed before expert inspection. You or your lawyer must purchase the salvage vehicle back or place a formal legal hold to prevent spoliation of physical evidence.

Q4: Can I sue the automaker if I was not wearing my seatbelt during the crash?

Yes, but it is legally more challenging. While airbags are supplemental restraints designed to work in tandem with seatbelts, many modern systems have specific unbelted deployment thresholds, though comparative negligence may reduce monetary damages.

Q5: Who pays for the expensive engineering experts needed for an airbag lawsuit?

Reputable personal injury and products liability law firms represent clients on a contingency fee basis, advancing all upfront costs for accident reconstructionists, biomechanical experts, and laboratory teardowns, only recovering fees if they win the case.

Q6: How long do I have to file a lawsuit against a car manufacturer for an airbag defect?

The deadline is governed by state personal injury and products liability statutes of limitations, which typically range between two and four years from the date of the crash. Additionally, some states enforce statutes of repose that bar claims on older vehicles.

Q7: Can a used car buyer sue an automaker for an airbag failure?

Yes. Strict products liability protects subsequent purchasers and passengers. However, the manufacturer defense team will rigorously examine whether previous collision repairs, aftermarket modifications, or salvaged titles contributed to the failure.

Q8: What compensation is available in an airbag non-deployment lawsuit?

Claimants can recover compensatory economic damages (past and future medical bills, lost wages, diminished earning capacity) and non-economic damages (pain, emotional trauma, permanent disfigurement), as well as punitive damages if gross negligence is proven.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Suing a major automotive manufacturer for an airbag failure is an intricate, resource-intensive legal battle, but for victims who have sustained severe, life-altering injuries due to a defective restraint system, it offers a vital path to justice and full financial recovery. Automakers cannot escape responsibility when design shortcuts, defective electronic crash sensors, or delayed recall notifications cause devastating occupant harm. Preserving the physical vehicle immediately, securing expert Event Data Recorder downloads, and retaining seasoned products liability trial attorneys provides injured victims with the formidable legal foundation needed to hold powerful automobile manufacturers accountable in court.
