How Much Is It to Get a Truck Lifted?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Transforming your pickup truck with an aggressive lifted stance, massive off-road tires, and increased ground clearance is the ultimate customization for many truck enthusiasts. On average, getting a truck lifted costs between $1,000 and $5,000 for standard 2 to 4-inch suspension or leveling setups, and between $5,000 and $15,000 or more for premium 6-inch long-travel suspension lifts combined with oversize wheels and 35 to 37-inch mud-terrain tires. Understanding the profound engineering differences between leveling kits, body lifts, and true suspension lifts ensures you choose the right setup for your driving needs.

Lift Categories: Leveling Kits, Body Lifts, and Suspension Packages

The term lifting a truck encompasses three radically distinct mechanical methodologies, each commanding vastly different parts and installation price points. A leveling kit is the most economical gateway, designed to raise the front nose of the truck 1.5 to 2.5 inches to eliminate factory forward rake (where the front sits lower than the rear for aerodynamics and towing). Leveling kits utilize strut spacers or torsion keys, costing $150 to $400 for parts and $250 to $500 for professional installation and front-end alignment.

A body lift elevates the truck cab and bed 2 to 3 inches off the factory steel frame using high-strength polyurethane or aluminum spacers, leaving the suspension geometry and ride dynamics completely stock. Body lift kits cost $300 to $700, but labor-intensive bumper relocation and steering shaft extensions bring total costs to $900 to $1,800. Finally, a true suspension lift kit replaces factory control arms, steering knuckles, rear leaf blocks, and shock absorbers with heavy-duty components, elevating both frame and body 3 to 8 inches for $1,200 to $4,500 in parts alone.

Hidden Associated Costs: Tires, Wheels, Gearing, and Speedo Recalibration

The most common oversight among truck owners budgeting for a lift kit is ignoring the extensive secondary modifications required once the chassis is elevated. A lifted truck on small factory 31-inch tires looks disproportionate, necessitating larger 33, 35, or 37-inch all-terrain or mud-terrain tires ($1,200 to $2,400 per set of four) paired with custom aftermarket 17 to 20-inch alloy wheels ($800 to $2,000).

Mounting massive 35-inch rubber drastically increases rotating mass and alters final drive ratios, making the truck feel sluggish off the line and hunting for gears on highway inclines. To restore factory acceleration and prevent transmission overheating, you often need to re-gear front and rear differentials with 4.56 or 4.88 ring-and-pinion gearsets ($1,800 to $2,800 installed). Additionally, a digital speedometer calibrator ($150 to $250) is required to correct dash speed readings and transmission shift schedules.

Fuel Economy, Ride Quality, and Warranty Implications

Before pulling the trigger on a substantial suspension lift, you must anticipate changes in daily driving characteristics. Elevating a truck increases aerodynamic drag significantly and adds hundreds of pounds of unsprung rotating weight, causing fuel efficiency to drop by 2 to 5 miles per gallon. Furthermore, entry and exit become more challenging, often prompting the installation of electric drop-down running boards like AMP Research PowerSteps ($1,400 to $2,000 installed).

Regarding manufacturer warranties, the federal Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act protects consumers by requiring automakers to prove that an aftermarket lift kit directly caused a specific mechanical failure before denying warranty claims. While a lift kit will not void your entire bumper-to-bumper vehicle warranty, dealerships will routinely deny claims on related components such as ball joints, tie rods, wheel bearings, CV axles, or drive shafts if premature failure is traced to altered suspension geometry.

How to Plan and Get Your Truck Lifted in 5 Steps

Follow these five strategic steps to choose the right lift height, coordinate installation, and ensure highway safety.

  1. Define Vehicle Purpose and Target Tire Size

    Decide whether the truck is for daily street cruising, towing, or rock crawling to choose appropriate tire size and lift height.

  2. Select a Reputable Suspension Brand

    Choose established manufacturers like Rough Country, BDS Suspension, ReadyLIFT, or Icon Vehicle Dynamics based on your budget.

  3. Source an Experienced 4x4 Off-Road Shop

    Hire an ASE-certified off-road customization facility rather than a general auto shop to ensure proper torque specs and bracket welding.

  4. Perform Speedometer and ADAS Camera Calibration

    Recalibrate the transmission shift computer and front windshield radar/camera safety sensors to account for the raised bumper height.

  5. Retorque All Suspension Hardware at 500 Miles

    Return to the installer after driving 500 miles to have U-bolts, control arm bolts, and lug nuts checked and retorqued to specification.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How much does it cost to get a truck lifted on average?

A complete professional lift kit installation ranges from $1,500 for a basic 3-inch setup to $6,000 to $12,000+ for a 6-inch lift with new wheels and 35-inch tires.

Q2: Does lifting a truck ruin the ride quality?

Inexpensive lift kits with stiff coil spacers degrade ride comfort, but high-end suspension systems featuring Fox or King coilovers often ride better than stock.

Q3: How much does a leveling kit cost compared to a lift kit?

A leveling kit typically costs $500 to $1,000 fully installed, whereas a full suspension lift kit ranges from $2,500 to $7,000 or more.

Q4: Does a lift kit void your truck factory warranty?

It does not void the entire warranty, but dealerships will deny claims on suspension parts, ball joints, and CV axles damaged by altered angles.

Q5: How much does gas mileage drop after lifting a truck?

Expect fuel economy to drop by 2 to 4 miles per gallon due to increased wind resistance, heavy oversize tires, and elevated chassis height.

Q6: Can I install a truck lift kit myself at home?

While a simple front leveling kit can be installed in a home garage with jack stands, multi-link 6-inch suspension lifts require professional lifts and spring compressors.

Q7: Do you need an alignment after lifting a truck?

Yes, an immediate computerized front-end alignment is mandatory to correct toe and camber angles, preventing severe tire wear within hundreds of miles.

Q8: How much is labor to install a lift kit?

Labor costs typically range from $600 to $1,500, reflecting 4 to 10 hours of shop time at standard hourly rates of $120 to $175.
