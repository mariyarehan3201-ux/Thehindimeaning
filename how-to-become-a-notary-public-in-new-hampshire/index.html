How to Become a Notary Public in New Hampshire?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Serving as a commissioned Notary Public in New Hampshire is an esteemed civic duty that empowers individuals to act as impartial, official witnesses to major legal, commercial, and personal transactions. Governed by the New Hampshire Secretary of State and confirmed by the Governor and Executive Council, obtaining a notary commission in the Granite State follows a distinct, traditional statutory procedure. Whether you want to provide notarial services for a law firm, banking institution, real estate office, or mobile signing practice, this comprehensive guide explains every step of the process.

New Hampshire Statutory Qualifications and Unique Endorsement Rules

The appointment of notaries public in New Hampshire is governed under Chapter 455 of the New Hampshire Revised Statutes Annotated (RSA 455). To qualify for appointment, an applicant must be at least 18 years of age, be a resident of New Hampshire, or be a resident of an adjoining state (such as Maine, Massachusetts, or Vermont) who is regularly employed or carries on a business in New Hampshire. Candidates must demonstrate sound moral character, have no disqualifying criminal felony convictions, and be registered voters in their municipality of residence.

One of the most distinctive requirements in New Hampshire notary law is the endorsement mandate. Unlike states that only require an online form and filing fee, New Hampshire requires every applicant's petition to be formally signed and recommended by two individuals: either two registered New Hampshire voters or a member of the New Hampshire Bar Association, alongside an endorsement from a local public official such as a police chief, town clerk, or judge. This tradition ensures that only trusted community members receive official state seals.

Application Procedure, Background Verification, and Council Approval

The application procedure requires coordinating with municipal authorities and state agencies. Applicants download the official Notary Public Application from the New Hampshire Secretary of State website. After completing personal identifying information, the applicant takes the form to their local town or city clerk, who verifies their registered voter status and completes the municipal certification section. The applicant then collects the required signatures from their two endorsers and local sponsoring official.

Next, the candidate must request a formal New Hampshire Criminal History Record Information (CHRI) report from the New Hampshire Division of State Police. Once the background clearance is completed, the entire package—including the application, voter certification, criminal record report, and the non-refundable $75 state statutory fee—is submitted to the Secretary of State's office in Concord. Applications undergo review by the Secretary of State before being presented to the Governor and Executive Council for official confirmation.

Oaths, Official Seal Specifications, and Professional Best Practices

Upon confirmation by the Governor and Executive Council, the Secretary of State mails the applicant their official Commission Certificate alongside an Oath of Office form. The applicant must take their constitutional oath before a commissioned New Hampshire Notary Public or Justice of the Peace within thirty days of issuance. The executed oath document is mailed back to the Secretary of State's office to formally activate the commission, which remains valid for a five-year term.

Commissioned notaries must procure an official New Hampshire notary seal, which can be an inked rubber stamp or an embossing seal. State law mandates that the seal include the notary's name, the words "Notary Public - New Hampshire," and the commission expiration date. While maintaining a notarial journal is not explicitly required by statutory law for traditional paper notarizations, the Secretary of State strongly recommends keeping a detailed sequential record of all notarial acts. Logging the date, time, document type, signer identity method, and signature safeguards the notary against legal challenges.

How to Apply for a New Hampshire Notary Public Commission

Follow these clear steps to prepare your paperwork, obtain local endorsements, and receive your New Hampshire notary commission.

  1. Verify Residency and Registered Voter Standing

    Confirm you are at least 18 years old, a New Hampshire resident (or bordering state employee), and registered to vote in your town.

  2. Obtain Required Signatures and Endorsements

    Secure endorsement signatures from two registered New Hampshire voters or an attorney, plus your municipal clerk's certification.

  3. Complete the State Police Criminal Record Check

    Submit a formal Criminal History Record Information request form to the New Hampshire Division of State Police.

  4. Submit Application and Fee to the Secretary of State

    Mail your completed package, criminal background report, and the seventy-five dollar application fee to the Secretary of State in Concord.

  5. Take the Official Oath of Office

    Upon confirmation by the Governor and Executive Council, take your oath before a commissioned official and return the signed form.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What is the fee to become a notary public in New Hampshire?

The statutory application fee is seventy-five dollars payable to the New Hampshire Secretary of State, along with state police background check fees.

Q2: How long is a New Hampshire notary public commission valid?

A New Hampshire notary public commission is issued for a term of five years from the date of executive council confirmation.

Q3: Can residents of Massachusetts or Maine become NH notaries?

Yes, residents of bordering states can qualify if they are regularly employed or carry on a trade, business, or practice within New Hampshire.

Q4: Does New Hampshire require a notary exam or mandatory class?

No, New Hampshire does not currently mandate a written examination or formal educational course for traditional notary public applicants.

Q5: Is a surety bond required for New Hampshire notaries?

No, New Hampshire state law does not require notaries public to purchase or file a commercial surety bond.

Q6: What information must appear on a New Hampshire notary stamp?

The seal must display the notary's name, the title 'Notary Public - New Hampshire', and the exact commission expiration date.

Q7: Can a New Hampshire notary perform marriages?

A New Hampshire Notary Public cannot solemnize marriages unless they also hold a commission as a Justice of the Peace or obtain a special license.

Q8: Is keeping a notary journal required in New Hampshire?

A journal is mandatory for remote online notarizations and strongly recommended by the Secretary of State for all standard paper notarizations.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Becoming a notary public in New Hampshire is a distinguished civic responsibility that combines historic executive council tradition with essential modern legal verification. By securing your local endorsements, fulfilling state background requirements, taking your oath of office, and upholding ethical impartiality, you provide an indispensable legal service that protects community commerce and public trust across the Granite State.
