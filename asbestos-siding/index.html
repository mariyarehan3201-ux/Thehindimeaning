Asbestos Siding

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Asbestos siding, commonly known by the historical trade name Transite, is an exterior wall cladding material composed of a compressed matrix of Portland cement reinforced with chrysotile asbestos fibers. Widely installed on residential homes from the 1920s through the late 1970s, this cementitious shingle siding was praised for its rot resistance, termite immunity, and complete fireproof durability. While intact siding poses minimal health hazards, understanding its condition, maintenance options, and safe removal guidelines is critical for homeowners.

Composition, Characteristics, and Historical Brands

Transite and asbestos-cement siding shingles were manufactured by blending roughly fifteen to thirty percent refined chrysotile asbestos fibers with seventy to eighty-five percent hydraulic Portland cement. This slurry was pressed under immense hydraulic pressure and steam-cured, producing dense, rigid, lightweight panels that could withstand decades of extreme weather without rotting, warping, or requiring frequent repainting.

Prominent twentieth-century building manufacturers—including Johns-Manville, Keasbey & Mattison, National Gypsum (Gold Bond), and GAF—produced these shingles in a variety of decorative styles. Common profiles included 12x24 inch overlapping shingles featuring straight, wavy, or staggered lower butt edges, often embossed with a simulated wood-grain texture to mimic traditional cedar shakes.

Evaluating Friability and Health Hazards

Under environmental regulations, asbestos siding is categorized as a Category II non-friable asbestos-containing material. Because the mineral fibers are tightly bound and locked within a solid, hardened Portland cement matrix, normal weathering and outdoor exposure do not release significant airborne fibers into the surrounding environment. An undamaged exterior wall covered in asbestos shingles presents virtually zero inhalation risk to home occupants.

The danger arises when siding shingles are subjected to mechanical forces that crush, pulverize, or abrade the cement matrix. Power washing with high-pressure nozzles, sawing, cutting, drilling, or sanding asbestos siding shatters the cement bond, generating clouds of respirable crystalline dust containing millions of toxic fibers. Furthermore, severe weather damage, hail strikes, or structural building fires can crack shingles, rendering exposed edges friable and hazardous.

Remediation Approaches: Encapsulation versus Siding-Over versus Removal

Homeowners seeking to modernize their home's exterior generally choose between three remediation pathways: painting and maintenance, siding-over (encapsulation), or full professional abatement. If the existing shingles are intact and structurally sound, painting them with high-grade exterior elastomeric acrylic paint seals the surface, prevents cement chalking, and revitalizes aesthetic curb appeal at a fraction of removal costs.

Another popular, cost-effective solution is 'siding over,' where contractors install foam insulation board directly over the existing asbestos shingles, followed by modern vinyl, aluminum, or fiber-cement siding. This approach safely encapsulates the asbestos without disturbing it, saving thousands of dollars in hazardous removal fees. However, if the siding is extensively broken, loose, or full structural renovation is planned, complete removal by licensed abatement technicians using whole-shingle unfastening techniques is required.

How to Safely Maintain or Siding-Over Asbestos Siding

Follow these five tactical steps to evaluate, seal, or overlay existing exterior asbestos-cement shingles safely.

  1. Assess Shingle Integrity

    Walk the perimeter of the home to count any cracked, loose, or missing shingles that require stabilizing before project commencement.

  2. Perform Gentle Hand Cleaning

    Wash exterior surfaces using a garden hose and mild detergent with a soft bristle brush, strictly avoiding high-pressure washers.

  3. Replace Damaged Tiles with Replicas

    Replace any shattered shingles with modern non-asbestos fiber-cement replacement shingles (e.g. GAF Weatherside).

  4. Install Fanfold Foam Insulation

    If overlaying, install high-density fanfold foam insulation board directly over the flat shingles to create a smooth, sealed barrier.

  5. Fasten Modern Siding Securely

    Install modern vinyl or fiber-cement siding by securing fasteners through the foam into underlying wall studs without shattering tiles.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is asbestos siding dangerous to live with?

No, intact asbestos siding is non-friable and locked in durable cement, meaning it poses virtually zero health risk to occupants as long as it is not cut, drilled, or broken.

Q2: Can you power wash asbestos siding?

No, never power wash asbestos siding, as high-pressure water streams abrade the cement surface and blast millions of toxic fibers into the yard and neighboring air.

Q3: Can you install new siding over existing asbestos siding?

Yes, installing foam insulation board and new vinyl or fiber-cement siding directly over intact asbestos shingles is an approved, cost-effective encapsulation method.

Q4: How can I tell if my exterior siding contains asbestos?

Asbestos-cement siding is hard, rigid, typically measures 12x24 inches with a wood-grain texture, sounds like ceramic when tapped, and was installed mostly between 1930 and 1978.

Q5: Can I replace a single broken asbestos shingle?

Yes, you can purchase modern, non-asbestos fiber-cement replacement shingles—such as GAF Weatherside—that match the dimensions, wavy edges, and textures of historic asbestos tiles.

Q6: How much does it cost to remove asbestos siding?

Professional removal of asbestos siding typically costs between $3 and $8 per square foot, averaging between $5,000 and $12,000 for an entire residential home.

Q7: Do I have to disclose asbestos siding when selling my home?

Yes, most state real estate disclosure laws require sellers to disclose known environmental hazards, including the presence of asbestos-cement siding.

Q8: What is the safest paint for asbestos siding?

A high-quality 100 percent acrylic exterior latex paint or elastomeric masonry coating is ideal, as it binds surface particles and forms a durable, weatherproof seal.
