I Was Hit by a Driver with a Suspended License

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

If you were hit by a driver with a suspended license, you are dealing with a motorist who committed a crime by operating a motor vehicle unlawfully. While insurance companies often attempt to deny coverage for unlicensed drivers, you can secure full compensation through the vehicle owner's policy under negligent entrustment or through your own Uninsured Motorist (UM) coverage.

The Legal Gravity of Driving on a Suspended License

Discovering that the motorist who crashed into your automobile was driving with an officially suspended or revoked driver's license introduces intense anger and anxiety. A driver's license is not suspended arbitrarily; state departments of motor vehicles and criminal courts revoke driving privileges for severe, chronic behavioral failures, including prior convictions for driving under the influence (DUI), excessive reckless driving points, unpaid child support judgments, or driving without mandatory auto liability insurance.

When an individual makes the conscious, unlawful decision to ignore a judicial suspension order and operate a motor vehicle on public roads, they commit a misdemeanor or felony crime under state motor vehicle codes. From a civil tort perspective, operating a motor vehicle with a suspended license provides profound evidentiary leverage in establishing negligence per se. A driver who is legally prohibited from being on the road cannot claim they were exercising ordinary, law-abiding care.

However, the greatest hurdle confronting crash victims is not proving that the suspended driver was at fault—it is identifying an active insurance policy with sufficient funds to pay for hospital bills, vehicle repairs, and lost income. Unlicensed drivers frequently drive uninsured vehicles or borrow cars from friends and family members, triggering complex insurance coverage battles. Does Insurance Cover an Accident with a Suspended Driver?

When you file an insurance claim, the first question is whether the automobile policy attached to the vehicle covers a driver whose license was suspended. The outcome hinges on vehicle ownership and specific insurance policy exclusions.

If the suspended driver was operating their own registered vehicle, check whether their insurance policy was cancelled when their license was revoked. While some insurers automatically cancel policies upon receiving DMV suspension notices, other policies remain active if premiums were prepaid. However, if the policy contains an Unlicensed Driver Exclusion clause, the insurer may attempt to deny liability coverage, arguing that the policyholder breached policy terms by driving unlawfully.

Far more commonly, a driver with a suspended license is operating a car owned by a friend, relative, romantic partner, or employer. Under standard automobile insurance law, insurance follows the vehicle first, not the driver. If the vehicle owner granted the suspended driver permission to drive the car (permissive use), the vehicle owner's liability policy is legally obligated to cover damages to innocent third parties, regardless of the driver's licensing status, unless the suspended driver was explicitly listed as an Excluded Driver on the policy declarations page. Holding the Vehicle Owner Liable for Negligent Entrustment

If the insurance company successfully denies coverage because the suspended driver was an excluded operator, your attorney will pursue an independent civil lawsuit directly against the vehicle owner under the doctrine of Negligent Entrustment.

Under American tort law, a motor vehicle owner has a legal duty not to hand the keys of a deadly machine to someone they know—or reasonably should know—is incompetent, reckless, or legally prohibited from driving. To win a negligent entrustment lawsuit against the vehicle owner, you must establish that the owner permitted the driver to operate the car, that the driver was unlicensed or reckless, that the owner knew or had reason to know of the suspension, and that the driver's negligence caused your injuries.

Proving negligent entrustment pierces insurance exclusions in many jurisdictions and allows you to hold the vehicle owner personally liable for your medical expenses, pain, and property damage, accessing their personal assets, real estate equity, or umbrella insurance policies.

How to Recover Damages from an Unlicensed Driver Crash

Step-by-step procedure to establish liability and secure insurance payouts after being hit by a suspended driver.

  1. Verify License Status with Responding Police Officers

    Ensure police run the driver's license through state DMV databases and record the driving under suspension (DUS) citation in the crash report.

  2. Identify the Registered Title Owner of the Vehicle

    Check the police collision report to determine if the vehicle was owned by the driver or borrowed from a third-party owner or business.

  3. File Claims with Owner's Insurer and Your UM Carrier

    Open claims simultaneously with the vehicle owner's liability insurer and your personal Uninsured Motorist (UM) carrier to protect all bases.

  4. Assert Negligent Entrustment Claims Against the Owner

    Have your attorney investigate whether the vehicle owner knowingly handed car keys to an unlicensed driver to establish direct owner liability.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Does insurance pay if the at-fault driver had a suspended license?

Yes. If the driver was operating a borrowed vehicle with permission, the car owner's auto insurance policy typically covers the damages.

Q2: What is negligent entrustment of a motor vehicle?

A legal claim holding a vehicle owner liable for lending their car to someone they knew or should have known was unlicensed, intoxicated, or reckless.

Q3: Will my Uninsured Motorist (UM) insurance cover the crash?

Yes. If the suspended driver has no insurance and the car owner's insurer denies coverage, your own UM policy pays for all your damages.

Q4: Can a driver go to jail for hitting someone on a suspended license?

Yes. Driving on a suspended license is a criminal offense; causing a collision while suspended frequently leads to immediate arrest and jail time.

Q5: What is a named driver exclusion?

An endorsement where a policyholder explicitly agrees that a specific high-risk family member is not covered under the insurance policy.

Q6: Can I sue the suspended driver personally?

Yes, you can obtain a civil court judgment against them, though collecting funds directly may be difficult if they lack personal assets.

Q7: Will my insurance rates increase if an unlicensed driver hits me?

In most states, insurance regulations prohibit carriers from raising premiums for not-at-fault collisions involving uninsured or unlicensed drivers.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Being struck by a driver with a suspended license complicates an accident claim, but it does not prevent you from recovering full financial compensation. By investigating the vehicle owner's permissive use, establishing negligent entrustment claims, and coordinating your own Uninsured Motorist (UM) policy protections, an experienced personal injury attorney ensures that unlicensed wrongdoers and negligent vehicle owners are held fully accountable for your losses.
