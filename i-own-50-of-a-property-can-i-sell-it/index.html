I Own 50% of a Property: Can I Sell It?

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: October 1, 2026

Owning 50 percent of a property gives you the legal right to sell your fractional co-ownership equity share, but you cannot unilaterally sell the entire property or force a full title transfer without your co-owner's consent. If your co-owner refuses to sell or buy you out, you have two primary legal options: sell your partial tenancy-in-common share to an investor, or file a judicial partition action in civil court to compel a court-supervised sale.

Co-Ownership Forms: Tenancy in Common Versus Joint Tenancy Rights

When multiple individuals share legal ownership of real estate, their legal rights depend on how the title deed is structured. The two most common forms of shared ownership are Tenancy in Common (TIC) and Joint Tenancy with Right of Survivorship (JTWROS). In a tenancy in common, each owner holds a distinct, transferable percentage equity share in the property. A tenant in common possesses the legal right to sell, mortgage, gift, or bequeath their partial share to any third party without obtaining permission from the other owner.

Conversely, joint tenancy requires four unities: time, title, equity share, and possession. If one joint tenant conveys their fractional share to a third party, that conveyance automatically severs the joint tenancy, converting ownership into a tenancy in common. Property owners facing shared ownership dilemmas frequently examine guides on selling half of a jointly owned residential property to explore the financial dynamics of fractional ownership shares. While selling your half is legally permissible, finding retail buyers willing to purchase half of a house with an uncooperative co-owner is difficult.

Negotiating a Co-Owner Buyout or Voluntary Market Sale

Before initiating expensive court proceedings, co-owners should pursue negotiated settlement options. The most mutually beneficial solution is an internal co-owner buyout. In this scenario, the co-owner who wishes to retain the home purchases your fifty percent equity equity share. The parties commission an independent appraisal from a licensed real estate appraiser to establish fair market value, subtract remaining mortgage debt, and calculate the net equity payout.

If neither party has the financial resources or credit qualifications to refinance the mortgage and buy the other out, the next best alternative is agreeing to list the entire property for sale on the open real estate market. Selling together allows both owners to capture full retail market value, divide net proceeds equally, and avoid court costs. In challenging situations involving deferred maintenance or financial distress, co-owners often review strategies to liquidate a distressed property to evaluate fast cash disposition avenues.

Judicial Partition Lawsuits and Court-Supervised Property Auctions

When negotiations break down entirely and a co-owner refuses to buy out your share, sell the home, or cooperate on repairs, the law provides an absolute statutory remedy: a partition lawsuit. As a fifty percent tenant in common, you possess an unconditional legal right to petition the county court to partition the property. The court cannot force you to remain in an unwanted co-ownership relationship against your will.

Historically, courts ordered a partition in kind, physically dividing the land into separate parcels. However, with residential homes, commercial buildings, and single-family lots, physically dividing a home is impossible. Therefore, modern courts order a partition by sale. The judge appoints a neutral referee or real estate special master to market the property or sell it at a public court auction, pay off the mortgage, deduct court costs and referee commissions, and distribute remaining funds.

During a partition action, the court conducts an accounting of all historical property expenses. If one co-owner paid the entire mortgage, property taxes, structural protection, or necessary capital improvements while the other paid nothing, the paying owner can claim contribution credits. Conversely, an owner who had exclusive possession while locking the other out may be charged an offset for fair market rental value.

How to Sell Your 50 Percent Share in a Property

Step-by-step procedural roadmap for co-owners seeking to negotiate buyouts, sell fractional shares, or initiate court partition actions.

  1. Examine the Title Deed and Mortgage Ownership Documents

    Review the recorded deed at the county recorder's office to confirm whether title is held as Tenancy in Common, Joint Tenancy, or under an LLC operating agreement.

  2. Order an Independent Professional Real Estate Appraisal

    Hire a licensed residential real estate appraiser to establish an unbiased fair market valuation of the property to serve as the baseline for negotiations.

  3. Calculate Net Equity and Propose an Internal Buyout Agreement

    Subtract remaining mortgage balances from the appraised value to determine each owner's 50% equity share, offering your partner the opportunity to refinance and buy you out.

  4. Propose a Joint Open-Market Listing with a Licensed Realtor

    If a buyout is not feasible, draft a written agreement with your co-owner to list the entire property for sale and divide the net closing proceeds equally.

  5. Explore Direct Fractional Sale to Real Estate Investment Firms

    If your co-owner refuses to cooperate, solicit cash offers from specialized private investors who purchase fractional tenancy-in-common shares at a discount.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sell a house if I only own 50 percent of it?

You cannot sell the entire house by yourself because you cannot sign away your co-owner's title rights. However, you can sell your 50% fractional ownership equity, or file a partition lawsuit to force a sale of the whole property.

Q2: Can my co-owner stop me from selling my half of the property?

No, in a tenancy in common, your co-owner has no legal right to block you from selling or transferring your partial 50% equity to an outside party, unless a pre-existing co-ownership agreement restricts transfers.

Q3: What is a partition action in real estate?

A partition action is a court lawsuit filed by a co-owner asking a judge to order the division or sale of real estate. For residential homes that cannot be physically split, the judge orders a partition by sale.

Q4: Who pays the attorney fees in a partition lawsuit?

In many states, basic attorney and referee fees required to complete the partition sale are paid out of the gross property sale proceeds before net funds are divided between the co-owners.

Q5: Can one owner buy out the other owner's 50 percent share?

Yes, an internal buyout is the most common resolution. The retaining owner arranges a refinance financing to pay off the existing mortgage and pays the departing owner their 50% cash equity share.

Q6: What happens if one owner paid all the property taxes and mortgage?

During a partition lawsuit accounting, the paying owner can request contribution credits, requiring the non-paying co-owner to reimburse their 50% share of necessary mortgage, tax, and hazard coverage expenses from sale proceeds.

Q7: Can a cash investor buy my 50 percent ownership share?

Yes, specialized real estate investors purchase partial tenancy-in-common equitys. However, because dealing with an uncooperative co-owner carries legal risk, investors typically purchase shares at a 30% to 50% discount.

Q8: Does owning 50 percent give me the right to live in the home?

Yes, each 50% co-owner possesses an equal legal right to occupy and use the entire property. One co-owner cannot legally lock out or evict the other without a court order.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Owning fifty percent of a property gives you strong legal leverage, but liquidating that equity requires a methodical approach. While you cannot unilaterally sell the entire home without your partner's signature, you are never trapped. By proposing an internal buyout, agreeing to a joint market listing, exploring fractional sales to investors, or filing a partition lawsuit, you can unlock your equity and achieve financial closure.
