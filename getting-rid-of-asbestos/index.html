Getting Rid of Asbestos

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Getting rid of asbestos safely, legally, and permanently is a critical challenge confronting property owners managing aging residential, commercial, and institutional buildings. Because improper handling aerosolizes microscopic mineral fibers that cause fatal respiratory illnesses, eliminating asbestos requires a clear understanding of legal options, environmental testing, professional abatement methods, and permitted hazardous waste disposal channels.

Evaluating Your Options: Removal vs. In-Place Management

When property owners contemplate getting rid of asbestos, the immediate impulse is often complete physical removal. However, environmental health authorities, including the EPA, advise that complete removal is not always the best or safest initial course of action. If asbestos-containing building materials—such as basement floor tiles, siding shingles, or acoustic plasters—remain in good, undamaged condition and are not located in high-traffic friction areas, managing them in place is often preferable.

Complete physical removal becomes mandatory under three specific circumstances: when materials are severely water-damaged or crumbling (friable), when major architectural renovations will physically demolish walls and ceilings, or when a building is slated for total demolition. In situations where materials are intact, safe alternatives like chemical encapsulation (applying elastomeric bridging sealants) or structural enclosure (framing drywall around insulated pipes) safely eliminate hazards at a fraction of the cost.

The Step-by-Step Professional Abatement Process

Getting rid of asbestos through complete professional removal follows a disciplined environmental engineering sequence designed to guarantee zero cross-contamination. Licensed contractors begin by filing mandatory 10-day advance notifications with state environmental protection agencies. On project launch day, technicians isolate the work zone, covering all doors, windows, and HVAC registers with double layers of fire-rated six-mil polyethylene plastic.

Technicians establish negative air pressure using industrial HEPA-filtered air scrubbers that exhaust outside, ensuring air drafts constantly flow inward. Crews don tight-fitting P100 elastomeric respirators and protective Tyvek suits, saturate materials with surfactant-treated water, and manually extract components using hand tools. The wet waste is sealed inside labeled, double-layer six-mil poly bags, washed in decontamination showers, and loaded into permitted hazardous waste trucks.

Legal Waste Disposal, Manifests, and Air Clearance Verification

A critical phase in getting rid of asbestos is ensuring legal hazardous waste disposal. Property owners remain legally responsible for hazardous waste generated on their property from 'cradle to grave' under federal environmental statutes. Licensed asbestos haulers must transport sealed waste bags to state-permitted solid waste landfills authorized to accept friable construction asbestos. Every shipment must be accompanied by a signed EPA Waste Shipment Record (WSR) verifying final burial.

The concluding benchmark is independent third-party air clearance testing conducted before containment poly is disassembled. An independent environmental consultant performs aggressive air sampling using leaf blowers and high-volume air pumps. Samples are evaluated via Phase Contrast Microscopy (PCM) or Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM); only when airborne fiber levels fall strictly below legal clearance thresholds is the property certified clean and released for reoccupancy.

How to Safely and Legally Get Rid of Asbestos in Your Property

Follow these five tactical steps to evaluate, contract, and execute asbestos elimination.

  1. Obtain an Independent Asbestos Survey

    Hire an independent certified asbestos inspector to test all suspect materials and produce a detailed hazardous survey report.

  2. Determine Removal vs. Encapsulation

    Evaluate whether sound materials can be safely encapsulated or enclosed, or if complete physical removal is required.

  3. Hire a Licensed Abatement Contractor

    Select a contractor holding active state licensing, verified pollution insurance, and strong references for negative air work.

  4. Verify Regulatory Notification Filings

    Ensure the contractor submits required 10-day advance project notifications to state and local health departments.

  5. Obtain Final Clearance and Waste Manifests

    Secure passing independent laboratory air clearance certificates and signed hazardous landfill receipts before final payment.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What is the best way to get rid of asbestos?

Hiring a licensed asbestos abatement contractor equipped with negative air HEPA containment and wet extraction tools is the safest method.

Q2: Can I throw asbestos in regular household trash?

No, disposing of asbestos in standard trash or municipal dumpsters is strictly illegal and triggers severe environmental fines.

Q3: How much does it cost to get rid of asbestos in a house?

Residential costs range from $1,500 to $4,500 for small pipe or tile jobs, and $8,000 to $20,000+ for large whole-home abatements.

Q4: Can you bury asbestos on your own property?

No, burying asbestos on private property is illegal under federal and state environmental laws due to groundwater and future excavation risks.

Q5: Is it always necessary to get rid of asbestos completely?

No, intact, undamaged asbestos in low-traffic areas can often be safely encapsulated, enclosed, or managed in place.

Q6: Where does asbestos waste go after removal?

It is transported by permitted hazardous haulers to specialized landfills permitted by the EPA to bury asbestos construction waste.

Q7: Can I take down an asbestos popcorn ceiling myself?

While legal for homeowners in some states, DIY scraping is extremely hazardous and frequently causes catastrophic home contamination.

Q8: How do I know my home is clean after getting rid of asbestos?

An independent third-party environmental consultant must perform aggressive air clearance sampling and issue a passing laboratory report.
