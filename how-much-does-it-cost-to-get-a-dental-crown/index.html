How Much Does It Cost to Get a Dental Crown?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 16, 2026

A dental crown encases a compromised tooth to restore natural structural strength, chewing ability, and aesthetic appearance following extensive decay, fracture, or root canal therapy. If you are paying entirely out of pocket without dental insurance, getting a dental crown typically costs between $800 and $2,500 per tooth. When dental insurance benefits apply, out-of-pocket patient costs generally drop to between $400 and $1,200, as most dental PPO policies classify crowns as major restorative care covered at fifty percent after meeting your annual deductible.

Dental Crown Material Categories and Price Benchmarks

The material chosen for your dental crown serves as the primary determinant of both clinical longevity and final treatment cost. Traditional porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM) crowns offer a proven balance of internal metal strength and tooth-colored exterior aesthetics, typically costing between $850 and $1,500 per unit. While widely utilized for premolars and molars, PFM crowns can occasionally reveal a subtle dark metal margin along the gumline as soft tissue recedes over time.

Modern monolithic zirconia and lithium disilicate (E-max) all-ceramic crowns represent the premier standard in contemporary restorative dentistry, commanding prices between $1,100 and $2,200. Zirconia provides virtually indestructible fracture resistance that withstands heavy nocturnal bruxism and grinding forces on back molars. High-noble cast gold crowns remain the gold standard among dental purists for longevity and biocompatibility, costing between $1,200 and $2,500 due to fluctuating raw commodity market prices for pure dental gold.

Preparatory Procedures, Core Buildups, and Clinical Surcharges

The crown restoration itself is rarely the only item on your dental invoice. Severely compromised teeth lacking sound structural enamel require preliminary preparatory procedures before a crown can be seated. If significant tooth structure has been hollowed out by dental caries or endodontic access, the dentist must perform a composite core buildup costing between $200 and $450 to anchor the prosthetic crown securely.

In situations where an infected nerve requires root canal therapy, endodontic treatment adds between $700 and $1,500 depending on whether the tooth is a single-canal anterior incisor or a multi-canal molar. Furthermore, if a fracture extends below the gum margin, a periodontist may need to perform surgical crown lengthening costing $400 to $900 to expose healthy bone and enamel for an airtight margin seal.

Insurance Limitations, In-House Membership Plans, and Financing

Navigating dental insurance reimbursement for crowns requires understanding annual maximum limitations. Most conventional employer-sponsored dental plans impose an annual maximum benefit cap of $1,000 to $2,000 per calendar year, a threshold established decades ago that has failed to keep pace with inflation. Once a root canal and core buildup consume the bulk of your annual allowance, the remaining crown expense must frequently be satisfied out of pocket.

Patients lacking commercial dental insurance can reduce out-of-pocket expenses by enrolling in dental discount savings plans or in-house clinic dental membership programs. For an annual subscription of $200 to $400, in-house plans provide complimentary preventive cleanings and diagnostic x-rays, alongside guaranteed fifteen to thirty percent discounts on major procedures like crowns. Third-party healthcare credit providers like CareCredit offer zero-interest promotional payment terms to divide payments into manageable monthly installments.

How to Prepare for and Receive a Dental Crown Step by Step

Follow this clinical process to prepare for your dental crown appointments and ensure a comfortable, long-lasting restoration.

  1. Complete Diagnostic Exam and Digital Imaging

    Have your dentist take periapical and bitewing digital radiographs to assess bone density, nerve proximity, and underlying decay depth.

  2. Perform Tooth Preparation and Decay Removal

    Receive local anesthetic while the dentist gently excavates decayed dentin, contours the outer enamel walls, and places a foundation core buildup.

  3. Capture Digital Optical Scans or Impressions

    Undergo a 3D intraoral digital optical scan or physical elastomeric impression to send precise millimeter tooth specifications to the dental lab.

  4. Wear and Protect the Temporary Acrylic Crown

    Receive a custom temporary acrylic crown cemented with temporary paste to shield sensitive dentinal tubules while avoiding sticky, hard candies.

  5. Seat, Bond, and Verify Permanent Crown Occlusion

    Return for your final fitting where the dentist verifies margin adaptation, checks bite contact points, and permanently bonds the crown with resin cement.

Frequently Asked Questions (9 Questions Answered)

Q1: Does getting a dental crown hurt?

No, the tooth and surrounding gingival tissues are completely numbed with local anesthetic during tooth preparation and cementation, ensuring a pain-free experience.

Q2: How long does a dental crown last?

With excellent oral hygiene, daily flossing, and regular dental cleanings, high-quality crowns typically last between 10 and 20 years, with gold crowns often lasting 30 years or more.

Q3: Can a crowned tooth still get a cavity?

Yes, while the crown material itself cannot decay, natural tooth structure exposed at the margin along the gumline is vulnerable to plaque buildup and recurrent decay.

Q4: Why do dentists recommend a crown after a root canal?

Teeth lose their internal blood supply after a root canal, becoming dry and brittle; a crown distributes chewing forces to prevent catastrophic tooth fracture.

Q5: What is the difference between a same-day CEREC crown and a lab crown?

Same-day CEREC crowns are computer-milled from ceramic blocks in the dental office in two hours, while lab-crafted crowns are custom layered by ceramists over one to two weeks.

Q6: What should I do if my temporary crown falls off?

Contact your dental clinic immediately, gently clean the inside of the temporary crown, and temporarily seat it back on the tooth using over-the-counter temporary dental cement or toothpaste.

Q7: Can I get a white crown on a back molar?

Yes, solid monolithic zirconia crowns provide exceptional fracture strength while maintaining a natural tooth-colored appearance ideal for back molars.

Q8: How much does it cost to replace an old dental crown?

Replacing an existing crown costs the same as a new crown ($800 to $2,500), plus potential core buildup or decay excavation fees once the old crown is removed.

Q9: Are gold crowns more expensive than ceramic crowns?

High-noble cast gold crowns are generally comparable to or slightly more expensive than ceramic crowns due to the raw commodity price of precious dental gold alloys.
