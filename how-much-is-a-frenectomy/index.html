How Much Is a Frenectomy?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 16, 2026

If an infant struggles with breastfeeding or a child experiences speech delays and dental spacing issues due to tongue-tie or lip-tie, finding out how much is a frenectomy is a top parental priority. On average, an oral frenectomy costs between $400 and $1,200 for an infant or young child when paid out of pocket, and $600 to $1,800 for older children and adults requiring local anesthesia and complex releases. Prices vary depending on whether the release is performed via soft-tissue laser or traditional surgical scissors, whether multiple ties (lingual and labial) are corrected concurrently, and how dental or medical insurance policies apply benefits.

Procedure Types, Anatomy, and Cost Differences

A frenectomy is a minor surgical procedure designed to release an overly tight, thick, or short band of mucosal tissue known as a frenum that restricts anatomical movement. The two most common oral variations are the lingual frenum (connecting the underside of the tongue to the floor of the mouth, causing ankyloglossia or tongue-tie) and the labial frenum (connecting the upper lip to the gum tissue above the central incisors, causing lip-tie). While treating a single tie typically costs $400 to $800, pediatric dental specialists frequently discover concurrent lingual and labial ties, offering dual-release procedures for $700 to $1,400.

The clinical method selected plays a major role in overall procedural expense. Traditional surgical excision using sterile scissors or a scalpel followed by dissolvable sutures represents the classical approach, costing $350 to $700. However, modern pediatric dentists, oral surgeons, and ENT specialists predominantly utilize advanced soft-tissue lasers, such as CO2 or diode lasers, which range from $500 to $1,200. Laser frenectomies vaporize tissue with microscopic precision, instantly cauterize nerve endings and blood vessels, eliminate the need for sutures, and reduce post-operative swelling and bleeding.

Insurance Coverage Realities and Ancillary Therapy Costs

Navigating insurance coverage for a frenectomy can be notoriously complex because the procedure straddles the boundary between dental and medical health plans. In infants experiencing severe latch failure, poor weight gain, or painful maternal nursing, commercial medical insurance plans frequently cover the procedure under pediatric feeding impairment diagnosis codes (ICD-10 Q38.1 for ankyloglossia). Medical reimbursement rates typically offset 50 to 80 percent of allowable fees after deductibles. Conversely, when performed in older children for orthodontic reasons (such as closing a midline diastema) or speech articulation, dental insurance plans often cover 50 to 70 percent under basic oral surgery benefits.

Achieving complete functional success following a frenectomy often requires ancillary supportive therapies that carry separate out-of-pocket costs. For infants, working with an International Board Certified Lactation Consultant (IBCLC) pre- and post-procedure costs $150 to $350 per home or clinic visit to retrain the infant suckling swallow reflex. For older children and adults, myofunctional therapy—neuromuscular re-education of tongue and facial muscles—costs $100 to $200 per session over six to twelve weeks to prevent oral relapse and correct dysfunctional swallowing habits.

Healing Protocols, Post-Op Stretches, and Preventing Reattachment

The diamond-shaped surgical wound created by a frenectomy naturally attempts to heal by secondary intention, pulling edges together rapidly. Without diligent post-operative intervention, the raw edges can reattach and re-fuse, creating scar tissue that restricts tongue mobility just as severely as the original frenum. To guarantee permanent functional mobility, practitioners instruct parents or patients to execute active wound stretches every four to six hours, including through the night, for three to four weeks following surgery.

Pain management following a laser frenectomy is generally straightforward and gentle. Infants typically require only infant acetaminophen for the first 24 to 48 hours, alongside natural soothing methods like skin-to-skin contact, frequent nursing, or chilled breast milk popsicles. Older children and adults manage minor soreness with over-the-counter ibuprofen or acetaminophen. Complications such as heavy bleeding, infection, or salivary gland injury are exceedingly rare when performed by trained pediatric dental or surgical specialists.

How to Prepare for and Recover from an Oral Frenectomy

Follow these five clinical steps to prepare your child for a frenectomy, ensure safe recovery, and prevent tissue reattachment.

  1. Obtain Comprehensive Functional Evaluation

    Schedule a functional assessment with a pediatric dentist, ENT, or lactation consultant to verify structural restriction and symptoms.

  2. Verify Dual Dental and Medical Insurance

    Submit pre-treatment estimates to both health and dental carriers to determine which policy provides optimal surgical coverage.

  3. Administer Comfort Medications Pre-Op

    Provide infant acetaminophen approximately 30 to 45 minutes prior to the procedure if recommended by your pediatric provider.

  4. Perform Diligent Daily Wound Stretches

    Gently lift the tongue or lip 4 to 6 times daily for four weeks to lift the diamond-shaped wound and prevent tissue re-adhesion.

  5. Coordinate Functional Bodywork and Nursing

    Follow up with an IBCLC, craniosacral therapist, or myofunctional therapist to optimize tongue elevation and swallowing coordination.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is a laser frenectomy better than scissors?

Laser frenectomies offer superior precision, minimal bleeding, reduced post-operative pain, and instant tissue cauterization without sutures.

Q2: Does medical insurance cover an infant frenectomy?

Yes, many medical insurance plans cover infant tongue-tie release under pediatric feeding and nursing impairment codes.

Q3: How long does a laser frenectomy procedure take?

The actual laser application lasts only 30 to 90 seconds per tie, with the entire clinical appointment taking 20 to 30 minutes.

Q4: Can a tongue-tie grow back after a frenectomy?

The frenum does not regrow, but the surgical wound can reattach if diligent post-operative stretching exercises are neglected.

Q5: How soon can a baby nurse after a frenectomy?

Babies can nurse or take a bottle immediately after the procedure, which helps soothe the infant and stretch the newly released tissue.

Q6: At what age should a frenectomy be done?

It can be performed within days of birth for feeding difficulties, or during toddlerhood and adolescence for speech and dental spacing.

Q7: Do adults ever need a frenectomy?

Yes, adults undergo frenectomies to treat chronic neck tension, sleep-disordered breathing, orthodontic gaps, or speech limitations.

Q8: What are the side effects or risks of a frenectomy?

Risks are minimal but can include minor bleeding, transient discomfort, local swelling, or incomplete release requiring revision.
