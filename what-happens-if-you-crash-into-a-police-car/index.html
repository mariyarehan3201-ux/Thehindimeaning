What Happens If You Crash into a Police Car?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

If you crash into a police car, you will face an immediate on-scene multi-agency investigation, extensive documentation of physical damages, and strict insurance claim scrutiny. Depending on whether you or the police officer caused the collision, the case involves either personal automobile liability or specialized government tort claims acts governed by state sovereign immunity laws.

Immediate On-Scene Protocols and Multi-Agency Investigation

Crashing into a municipal, county, or state police vehicle is an intimidating and stressful experience for any motorist. When an accident involves an active law enforcement cruiser, standard post-accident procedures undergo immediate escalation. Because the local police department cannot impartially investigate a collision involving its own officers and municipal equipment, standard departmental operating procedures require an independent supervisor, county sheriff, or state highway patrol trooper to report to the scene to conduct a neutral traffic collision reconstruction.

At the scene, expect an exhaustive, high-intensity investigation. Responding investigators will measure skid marks with digital measuring wheels, inspect both vehicles' onboard event data recorders, download police cruiser dashcam and bodycam video, and interview independent civilian witnesses. If the police car was responding to an emergency call with its overhead emergency lights and siren activated, investigators will scrutinize whether you complied with statutory move-over and yield laws.

Motorists must remain calm, courteous, and cooperative, but exercise caution regarding statements of fault. While you must provide your driver's license, vehicle registration, and proof of active auto insurance, you are not required to admit guilt or make speculative statements such as 'I didn't see the flashing lights.' Any verbal admission will be recorded in the official collision narrative and utilized by city attorneys or insurance adjusters. What Happens If You Are at Fault for the Crash?

If the independent investigation concludes that you caused the accident—such as rear-ending a stopped police cruiser, failing to yield to an emergency vehicle running flashing lights and sirens, or making an illegal left turn across its path—your auto insurance carrier will be held responsible for all resulting damages. Your property damage liability coverage must pay to repair or replace the police cruiser, which often includes tens of thousands of dollars in specialized tactical electronics, dashcam systems, computer docks, radar units, and lightbars.

Additionally, your bodily injury liability policy must compensate the injured police officer for emergency room treatment, orthopedic surgeries, physical therapy, and lost wages. Because injured officers are also covered by municipal workers' compensation, the city's workers' comp carrier will assert a statutory subrogation lien against your auto insurance policy to recover all medical bills and wage benefits paid to the officer.

Beyond civil insurance liability, an at-fault driver faces significant traffic citations. Common charges include failure to yield to an authorized emergency vehicle, violation of the state Move-Over law, reckless driving, or speeding. In severe cases involving driving under the influence (DUI) of drugs or alcohol where a law enforcement officer is injured or killed, the motorist faces catastrophic felony charges, including aggravated vehicular assault or vehicular manslaughter, carrying mandatory multi-year state prison sentences. What Happens If the Police Officer Caused the Crash?

There are many instances where the police officer is legally at fault for the collision. Law enforcement officers frequently look down at onboard computer terminals (MDTs), make abrupt U-turns across highway medians without signaling, or blow through blind red lights without slowing down or verifying clear intersections. When an officer's negligence injures you or damages your vehicle, you have a legal right to compensation, but the process is governed by strict government immunity statutes.

Under the common law doctrine of sovereign immunity, government entities are historically shielded from lawsuits unless specific statutory waivers apply. Every state has enacted a Government Tort Claims Act (such as the California Tort Claims Act or Texas Tort Claims Act) that waives sovereign immunity when government employees cause motor vehicle accidents while acting within the scope of their employment. However, these statutes impose draconian procedural rules. Instead of having two to three years to sue, you must file a formal administrative Notice of Claim with the municipality, county, or state within a very narrow window—often between 30 and 180 days from the accident date.

How to Handle a Collision Involving a Police Vehicle

Step-by-step procedure to protect your legal and financial rights after an accident with a law enforcement cruiser.

  1. Remain at the Scene and Ensure Safety

    Bring your vehicle to a safe stop, turn on hazard flashers, remain in your vehicle unless unsafe, and check all passengers for injuries.

  2. Request an Independent Investigating Agency

    Ask that a supervisory officer, state highway patrol trooper, or county sheriff be dispatched to draft an independent, neutral crash report.

  3. Document Physical Evidence and Badge Numbers

    Photograph vehicle damage, skid marks, traffic signals, and weather conditions, noting the officer's name, badge number, and cruiser unit number.

  4. File Government Notice of Claim Promptly (If Officer at Fault)

    If the officer caused the crash, file a formal administrative notice of claim with the city or county clerk within the statutory deadline (often 30 to 90 days).

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I get arrested for accidentally hitting a police car?

If the crash was a genuine civil accident caused by simple negligence, you will not be arrested. However, if alcohol, reckless driving, or hit-and-run is involved, you will face immediate arrest.

Q2: Does my car insurance cover damages to a police cruiser?

Yes. Your auto policy's property damage liability coverage pays to repair or replace the police cruiser and its installed electronic equipment up to your policy limit.

Q3: Can I sue the police department if the officer was at fault?

Yes, under state government tort claims acts, you can sue the municipal or state government, provided you file a formal notice of claim within the strict statutory deadline.

Q4: Do police cars have dashcams that record the crash?

Yes. Almost all modern police cruisers are equipped with continuous dashcams and bodycams that record audio and video, which can be subpoenaed as evidence.

Q5: What is the Move-Over law?

A traffic safety statute in all 50 states requiring drivers approaching stopped emergency vehicles with flashing lights to move over one lane or slow down significantly.

Q6: What if the damages to the police cruiser exceed my insurance limit?

If damages exceed your policy limit, the city or county can pursue a civil judgment against your personal assets, bank accounts, or future wages.

Q7: Does a police officer have to use sirens when speeding to an emergency?

In most jurisdictions, emergency vehicle exemptions apply only when the officer activates both visual emergency lights and audible sirens simultaneously.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Crashing into a police car triggers immediate multi-agency investigation, strict traffic enforcement, and complex insurance negotiations. If you caused the collision, notify your insurance carrier immediately and consult a traffic defense attorney to protect your driving privileges. If the officer caused the crash, retain a personal injury lawyer immediately to ensure you meet the strict administrative notice deadlines under your state's government tort claims statutes.
