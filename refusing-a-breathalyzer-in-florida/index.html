Refusing a Breathalyzer in Florida

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Refusing a breathalyzer in Florida triggers severe administrative and criminal penalties under Florida's Implied Consent Law (Florida Statute § 316.1932). A first-time refusal results in an immediate mandatory one-year driver license suspension. A second or subsequent refusal is classified as a separate First-Degree Misdemeanor crime carrying up to one year in jail.

The Statutory Doctrine of Florida's Implied Consent Law

When you sign the paperwork to obtain or renew a driver license with the Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles (FLHSMV), you enter into a binding statutory contract with the State of Florida. Operating a motor vehicle on public roads is legally classified as a conditional privilege, not an absolute constitutional right.

Under Florida Statute § 316.1932—widely known as Florida's Implied Consent Law—any person who accepts the privilege of operating a motor vehicle within the state is deemed by law to have given their implied consent to submit to an approved chemical test of their breath, urine, or blood if they are lawfully arrested for any offense allegedly committed while driving under the influence.

Crucially, Florida's implied consent statute applies only after a lawful DUI arrest has taken place. Before demanding a breath test, the law enforcement officer must have probable cause to arrest you, place you under formal custody, and read the official Florida Implied Consent Warning from a pre-printed departmental form. Refusing the test after this warning is read triggers immediate state sanctions. The table below delineates the statutory penalties for refusing a chemical breath test in Florida.

Roadside Portable Breath Tests (PBT) vs the Intoxilyzer 8000

One of the most widespread sources of legal confusion among Florida motorists is the physical difference between a roadside handheld breath tester and the official desktop breath machine at the police station.

During an active traffic stop on the roadside, a police officer or Florida Highway Patrol (FHP) trooper may ask you to blow into a small, battery-operated handheld gadget called a Preliminary Breath Test (PBT). Under Florida administrative law, PBT handheld devices are NOT approved by the Florida Department of Law Enforcement (FDLE) for evidentiary use in court. You hold the absolute legal right to politely decline the roadside PBT; declining a roadside PBT carries zero license suspension and zero criminal penalties.

Conversely, once an officer places you under formal arrest and transports you to the county jail or DUI Central Breath Testing facility, you are presented with the Intoxilyzer 8000—an FDLE-certified, infrared spectrophotometer. It is the refusal of this certified evidentiary machine—after being read formal implied consent warnings on video—that triggers the mandatory one-year suspension and misdemeanor prosecution. The Criminalization of Second Refusals: Florida Statute § 316.1939

While a first-time breath test refusal is classified purely as an administrative civil offense handled by the Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles, Florida law takes an exceptionally harsh approach to repeat refusals.

Under Florida Statute § 316.1939, it is a standalone First-Degree Misdemeanor crime to refuse a lawful chemical breath, urine, or blood test if your driving privilege has been previously suspended for refusing a test. This means that if you have a prior refusal on your driving record and you refuse the Intoxilyzer 8000 a second time, you will be hit with two separate criminal charges: Count 1: Driving Under the Influence, and Count 2: Unlawful Refusal to Submit to Chemical Testing.

A conviction under § 316.1939 carries penalties including up to one year in county jail, one year of probation, and a $1,000 criminal fine. Furthermore, the prosecutor in your criminal DUI trial will enter your refusal into evidence under Fla. Stat. § 316.1932(1)(a), arguing to the jury that your refusal demonstrates 'consciousness of guilt'—insisting that you refused because you knew you were intoxicated.

How to Protect Your Driving Privileges After a Florida Refusal

Critical 10-day legal defense protocol following a breath test refusal in Florida.

  1. Request an Administrative Formal Review Hearing Within 10 Days

    File a formal hearing request with the FLHSMV Bureau of Administrative Reviews within 10 calendar days of arrest to secure a 42-day driving permit.

  2. Elect Immediate Business Purpose Only (BAP) Waiver

    First-time refusal offenders can waive the hearing to receive an immediate hardship driving permit for work and school without waiting 90 days.

  3. Obtain the Video Recording of the 20-Minute Observation Period

    Have your attorney subpoena the breath room video to verify the breath technician maintained a strict 20-minute observation period before the refusal.

  4. Challenge the Lawfulness of the Initial Vehicle Traffic Stop

    File a Motion to Suppress in criminal court; if the officer lacked reasonable suspicion to stop your car, the entire arrest and refusal are dismissed.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I refuse a breathalyzer test in Florida?

You can physically refuse, but under Florida's implied consent law, refusing results in an immediate 1-year driver license suspension.

Q2: Is refusing a breathalyzer a crime in Florida?

A first refusal is a civil administrative penalty; however, a second or subsequent refusal is a First-Degree Misdemeanor punishable by up to 1 year in jail.

Q3: Can I refuse the roadside breath test in Florida?

Yes. Portable roadside breath tests (PBT) are voluntary and not certified for court; you can refuse them without any license suspension.

Q4: What is the 10-day rule after a Florida DUI refusal?

You have exactly 10 calendar days from the date of arrest to request a formal review hearing or apply for a business purpose hardship driving permit.

Q5: Can prosecutors use my refusal against me in court?

Yes. Under Florida law, prosecutors are permitted to argue to the jury that your refusal shows 'consciousness of guilt.'

Q6: Can police force you to take a breathalyzer in Florida?

Police cannot physically force a breath test, but they can obtain a judicial search warrant for a forced blood draw in crashes involving serious injury or death.

Q7: Can I get a hardship license after refusing a breath test?

First-time refusal offenders can obtain a Business Purpose Only (BAP) license, but second-time refusals face a strict 18-month hard suspension.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Refusing a breathalyzer in Florida carries severe, immediate administrative consequences and can elevate into a separate criminal misdemeanor on a second offense. While declining roadside field sobriety exercises and portable PBT devices is legally permitted without penalty, refusing the official Intoxilyzer 8000 at the station guarantees an automatic one-year license suspension. If arrested, request an immediate administrative formal review hearing within 10 days and retain an experienced Florida DUI defense attorney to challenge the lawfulness of the initial stop.
