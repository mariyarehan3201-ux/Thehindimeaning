Can Someone Sue You After Car Crash?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, someone can sue you after a car accident if they believe your driving negligence caused their bodily injuries or property damage. However, if you maintain valid auto insurance, your insurance company is legally bound under its 'Duty to Defend' to hire and pay for an experienced litigation defense attorney to protect you at zero out-of-pocket cost and pay settlements up to your policy limits.

The Threat of Litigation: What Being Sued Actually Means

Opening your front door to find a process server or county sheriff handing you a formal legal Summons and Complaint naming you as a Defendant in a personal injury lawsuit is an alarming, anxiety-inducing experience. Your mind immediately races to catastrophic scenarios: 'Will I lose my home? Will the plaintiff garnish my savings accounts? Will my wages be seized?'

To navigate this legal crisis calmly and strategically, you must understand how civil automotive litigation actually functions in the United States. In civil tort law, any individual involved in a traffic accident possesses the legal right to file a lawsuit in county or district civil court at any time within your state's statutory deadline (the Statute of Limitations, typically two to three years from the date of the crash).

However, the overwhelming majority of car accident lawsuits are not personal vendettas aimed at seizing your personal home or savings. Instead, filing a lawsuit is the standard procedural mechanism personal injury attorneys utilize to break insurance claim deadlocks, force insurance defense adjusters to take claims seriously, and extract maximum financial settlement payouts from your auto insurance policy. The Insurer's Sacred Legal Duties: Duty to Defend and Indemnify

The moment you purchase a personal auto insurance policy from an established carrier (such as State Farm, GEICO, Progressive, Allstate, or Travelers), you are purchasing far more than mere liability financial payout coverage. You are purchasing a comprehensive legal defense team.

Under American insurance contract law, your auto insurance policy imposes two foundational, legally binding obligations upon the insurance carrier: the Duty to Defend and the Duty to Indemnify.

The Duty to Defend is broad and paramount. Even if the plaintiff's lawsuit allegations are exaggerated, groundless, or fraudulent, your insurance carrier is contractually obligated to assign an experienced insurance defense litigation attorney to represent you. The insurance company pays all defense legal bills, hourly attorney fees, court filing costs, deposition court reporter charges, and expert witness fees out of its own corporate reserves—at zero cost to you. Furthermore, under the Duty to Indemnify, the insurer must pay any negotiated financial settlement or final jury judgment up to your policy's Bodily Injury and Property Damage liability limits. The table below compares the duty to defend against the duty to indemnify.

Duty to Defend Provide competent legal counsel; defend all claims in court Saves policyholder tens of thousands in attorney hourly fees
Duty to Indemnify Pay final settlements or court verdicts up to policy limits Insulates personal bank accounts from crash liability
Duty of Good Faith Must settle claims within policy limits when opportunity arises Protects policyholder from excess verdict exposure

The Dangerous Threat of an Excess Judgment

While auto insurance shields policyholders in more than ninety-eight percent of accident lawsuits, a genuine financial risk arises if the plaintiff's damages vastly exceed your policy limits—a situation known as an Excess Judgment.

If you carry only your state's statutory minimum liability coverage (such as $25,000 per person) and you cause a catastrophic crash that inflicts $500,000 in traumatic brain surgeries and permanent disability, the plaintiff's attorney will demand your full $25,000 policy limit. If your insurer pays the $25,000, the plaintiff's attorney will typically require the injured party to sign a General Release of All Claims, legally extinguishing your personal liability.

However, if the insurance company unreasonably refuses to settle within policy limits, or if the plaintiff rejects the limits and proceeds to trial, a jury may enter a $500,000 verdict against you personally. In this scenario, your insurer pays its $25,000 limit, leaving you personally responsible for the remaining $475,000 balance. Fortunately, under the legal doctrine of Insurance Bad Faith (such as the Stowers Doctrine in Texas), if an insurer recklessly refused a reasonable opportunity to settle within policy limits, the insurer can be held legally liable to pay the entire excess judgment, protecting your personal assets.

What to Do If You Are Served with a Car Accident Lawsuit

Critical immediate legal defense checklist upon being served with a summons and complaint.

  1. Note the Exact Date, Time, and Method of Legal Service

    Write down the exact date and time the process server handed you the papers; state civil procedure rules establish strict 20 to 30-day deadlines to answer.

  2. Transmit Complete Lawsuit Papers to Your Auto Insurer

    Scan and email the complete Summons and Complaint immediately to your auto insurance claims adjuster; request immediate confirmation of receipt.

  3. Connect with Your Assigned Insurance Defense Attorney

    Schedule an intake interview with the litigation attorney retained by your insurer to review the crash facts and draft the formal legal Answer.

  4. Preserve All Personal Crash Documentation and Photos

    Gather your crash scene photos, vehicle damage estimates, dashcam files, and notes to assist your defense counsel in dismantling the lawsuit.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can someone sue me if I have car insurance?

Yes. In personal injury lawsuits, the injured person sues you by name as the driver, but your insurance company steps in to defend and pay the claim.

Q2: How long after an accident can someone sue you?

The statute of limitations typically ranges from two to three years from the date of the crash, depending on your state's civil practice codes.

Q3: Can they take my house or savings if I get sued?

In the vast majority of cases, no; claims settle within insurance policy limits, and state homestead exemption laws protect primary residences.

Q4: Do I have to pay for my own lawyer if I get sued?

No. Under your policy's 'Duty to Defend,' your auto insurance company hires and pays for your litigation defense attorney at zero cost to you.

Q5: What happens if the lawsuit is for more than my insurance coverage?

Your insurer will attempt to settle within limits; if they fail in bad faith, the insurer may be forced to pay the full excess verdict.

Q6: What happens if I ignore a court summons?

Never ignore a summons! If you fail to file an Answer within 20 to 30 days, the court will enter a Default Judgment against you automatically.

Q7: Can someone sue you if police said you were not at fault?

Yes. Police crash reports are investigative opinions and generally inadmissible in civil court; civil juries make the final fault determination.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Being sued after a car accident is a stressful experience, but maintaining adequate auto insurance provides comprehensive legal protection. Your insurer is legally obligated to hire and pay for your defense litigation attorney and resolve claims within your coverage limits. The moment you are served with lawsuit papers, transmit them immediately to your insurance carrier, cooperate fully with your appointed defense lawyer, and consider carrying high liability limits and umbrella insurance to protect your family's financial future.
