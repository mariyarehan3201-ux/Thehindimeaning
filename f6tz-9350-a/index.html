F6tz 9350 A

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 12, 2026

Part number F6TZ-9350-A is the legendary genuine Ford OEM mechanical two-stage fuel pump engineered for 1994.5 to 1997 Ford F-250, F-350, and Super Duty trucks powered by the iconic 7.3-liter Powerstroke direct-injected turbo diesel engine. Mounted deep in the engine valley beneath the turbocharger, this tandem mechanical lift pump is driven directly by a dedicated lobe on the engine camshaft. Understanding the operation, common failure modes, fuel weeping leak symptoms, and meticulous replacement procedures associated with F6TZ-9350-A is essential for every Old Body Style (OBS) Ford diesel enthusiast and diesel mechanic.

Engineering Principles of the Two-Stage Mechanical Fuel Pump

The first-generation 7.3L Powerstroke diesel platform utilized a distinctive fuel supply architecture that relied on the mechanical F6TZ-9350-A pump rather than an in-tank electric lift pump. The pump features a dual-stage, dual-diaphragm mechanical design operated by a heavy-duty steel pushrod that rides continuously against the engine camshaft. The low-pressure stage draws diesel fuel forward from the selected chassis fuel tank, pulling fuel at approximately four to seven pounds per square inch through the fuel selector valve and into the frame fuel lines.

Once fuel reaches the pump, it passes into the fuel filter and water separator bowl assembly mounted in the engine valley. After filtering, the second high-pressure stage of the F6TZ-9350-A pump compresses the fuel to between forty-five and sixty-five pounds per square inch. This pressurized diesel is then delivered through rigid steel fuel jumper lines into the cylinder head fuel rails, ensuring adequate supply pressure for the hydraulically actuated electronically controlled unit injectors (HEUI) under full throttle acceleration.

Failure Symptoms, Weep Hole Leaks, and Diagnostics

Due to decades of thermal cycling, vibration, and ultra-low sulfur diesel fuel degradation, original mechanical fuel pumps eventually succumb to internal seal failure. The most universal symptom of a failing F6TZ-9350-A pump is diesel fuel pooling in the engine valley. The pump incorporates an engineered atmospheric weep hole on the underside of its casting. When the internal rubber diaphragms fatigue and tear, diesel fuel leaks past the seal and drips out of this weep hole, filling the engine valley before running down the back of the engine block and transmission bellhousing onto the driveway.

Beyond visible fluid leaks, a degraded fuel pump impairs engine performance and drivability. As the high-pressure diaphragm loses tension, fuel rail pressure drops below the forty-five PSI minimum threshold, causing hard starting, engine hesitation under load, loss of horsepower when pulling trailers, and injector cavitation. In severe failure cases, a ruptured lower diaphragm can allow diesel fuel to leak downward past the pump mounting gasket and pushrod bore directly into the engine crankcase, diluting engine motor oil and risking catastrophic bearing failure if left unaddressed.

Replacement Challenges and the Pushrod Precaution

Replacing the F6TZ-9350-A mechanical fuel pump is notoriously labor-intensive due to its cramped location directly underneath the turbocharger pedestal and fuel filter bowl. Experienced mechanics typically remove the turbocharger compressor inlet pipes, turbo downpipe clamps, and the entire fuel filter bowl assembly to gain unhindered access. Two hold-down bolts secure the pump body to the engine block, requiring a slim ratchet and long extension to navigate between rigid steel fuel supply lines without stripping the bolt heads.

The most critical procedural precaution involves the mechanical drive pushrod. When lifting the old pump out of the block, the steel pushrod can adhere to the pump lever via oil suction and inadvertently drop into the dark camshaft gallery inside the engine crankcase. Dropping the pushrod into the oil pan turns a half-day repair into a massive engine disassembly job. Technicians use a magnetic wand or mechanical fingers to capture the pushrod immediately, inspecting it for excessive end wear before reinserting it and torquing the new OEM pump into place.

How to Replace Ford 7.3L Fuel Pump F6TZ-9350-A in 5 Steps

Follow this comprehensive diesel repair protocol to replace the valley-mounted mechanical fuel pump safely.

  1. Relieve Pressure and Remove Valley Accessories

    Disconnect both vehicle batteries, drain the fuel filter bowl using the drain valve, and remove intake piping and fuel bowl for access.

  2. Disconnect Fuel Lines and Rear Banjo Fitting

    Carefully remove the front steel fuel lines and unthread the rear 1-1/4 inch fuel banjo bolt on the back of the pump body.

  3. Unbolt the Pump and Secure the Pushrod

    Unbolt the two 10mm mounting bolts, lift the pump slowly, and immediately secure the internal steel pushrod with a magnetic wand.

  4. Prep the Block Surface and Position the New Gasket

    Clean all old gasket material from the engine block deck, apply a light coat of clean engine oil to the new gasket, and reseat the pushrod.

  5. Torque New Pump and Reassemble Fuel System

    Install the new OEM F6TZ-9350-A pump, torque bolts to 18 lb-ft, reattach banjo fittings with new washers, reinstall the bowl, and prime fuel.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What vehicle engines use the F6TZ-9350-A fuel pump?

It is used exclusively on 1994.5 to 1997 Ford OBS trucks equipped with the 7.3-liter Powerstroke turbo diesel engine.

Q2: Why is fuel leaking into the valley of my 7.3 Powerstroke?

The most common culprit is the mechanical fuel pump internal weep hole leaking diesel when its rubber diaphragm fails.

Q3: What is the fuel pressure specification for a 7.3L mechanical pump?

Normal operating fuel pressure ranges between 45 and 65 PSI measured at the fuel filter bowl Schrader test port.

Q4: Can a bad mechanical fuel pump put diesel fuel into the engine oil?

Yes, if the lower diaphragm ruptures, diesel can bypass the pushrod opening and dilute engine motor oil in the crankcase.

Q5: Why is it important to watch the fuel pump pushrod during removal?

If the pushrod slips out of the pump arm, it can fall into the engine crankcase, necessitating pulling the oil pan.

Q6: Should I replace the mechanical pump with an electric fuel system?

Many owners convert to an electric fuel (e-fuel) system, but genuine OEM mechanical pumps offer proven reliability at lower cost.

Q7: How long does a genuine Ford F6TZ-9350-A fuel pump last?

A genuine OEM mechanical fuel pump typically provides reliable service for 100,000 to 150,000 miles before diaphragm fatigue.

Q8: Do I need to replace the copper crush washers on the banjo bolt?

Yes, always install new copper crush washers on the rear banjo fitting to prevent high-pressure fuel leaks behind the pump.
