Can You Sue a Trust for Personal Injury?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

When you suffer an injury on a commercial property, apartment complex, or residential home owned by a trust, determining who to sue can appear legally complex. A trust is not an independent corporate person or distinct business entity like a corporation or LLC; instead, it is a fiduciary legal arrangement wherein a trustee holds legal title to property for the benefit of designated beneficiaries. Under modern personal injury and trust jurisprudence, an injured plaintiff can indeed recover damages from trust assets by naming the trustee in their representative fiduciary capacity.

The Legal Mechanics of Trustee Liability and Trust Property Torts

In common law jurisprudence, a trust cannot be sued as an independent entity in the exact manner one would file a complaint against a corporation or an individual. Because a trust is fundamentally a legal relationship rather than an artificial person, title to real estate and property assets is held directly in the name of the trustee. Consequently, when an individual suffers an injury on trust-owned real property, the injured claimant must initiate legal action by naming the trustee in their representative capacity, such as Jane Doe, as Trustee of the Oakridge Family Trust.

The modernization of trust law through the Uniform Trust Code (UTC), adopted in the majority of United States jurisdictions, has clarified and streamlined the tort liability of trusts. Under Uniform Trust Code Section 1010, a claim based on a tort committed in the course of administering a trust may be asserted against the trustee in their fiduciary capacity, regardless of whether the trustee is personally at fault. If the trustee did not commit intentional misconduct or gross personal negligence, any civil judgment or settlement is paid directly from the trust property assets and commercial liability insurance policies, shielding the trustee personal bank accounts.

However, if the trustee was personally negligent, such as personally performing dangerous, unpermitted electrical repairs that caused a fire or ignoring hazardous structural conditions, the trustee can be sued in both their representative capacity and their individual personal capacity. In such cases, both the trust estate assets and the trustee personal wealth become exposed to civil execution. Premises Liability Standards on Trust-Owned Real Estate

When an injury occurs on trust-owned real estate, the underlying legal doctrine governing the case is premises liability. Trustees who manage residential apartment buildings, commercial retail strips, or rental single-family homes owe a strict legal duty of care to lawful visitors, tenants, and business invitees. This duty requires the trustee to maintain the premises in a reasonably safe condition, conduct regular property inspections, promptly repair known safety hazards, and provide adequate warnings regarding concealed dangers.

Typical premises liability claims involving trust properties encompass slip-and-fall incidents on icy sidewalks, trip-and-falls caused by broken pavement, structural stair collapses, toxic mold contamination, and negligent security claims resulting in physical assaults. In almost all well-administered trusts, the trustee purchases comprehensive Commercial General Liability (CGL) insurance or landlord liability policies that name both the trustee and the trust estate as insured entities. When a personal injury attorney files a claim, the insurance carrier provides legal defense counsel and negotiates settlement payouts up to policy limits.

Identifying the specific property management structure is critical during early litigation discovery. If the trustee hired an independent property management company to oversee daily operations, both the management firm and the trustee are named as co-defendants. Navigating Revocable vs Irrevocable Trusts in Personal Injury Claims

The specific structure of the trust plays a significant role in determining how a plaintiff recovers financial compensation. In a Revocable Living Trust, the grantor who created the trust typically retains complete control over the trust assets, possessing the power to amend, revoke, or withdraw funds at any time. Under statutory creditor laws, assets in a revocable trust are considered the personal property of the grantor. Therefore, if a grantor-trustee causes an injury or owns property where someone is hurt, the entire corpus of the revocable trust is fully accessible to satisfy court judgments.

Conversely, Irrevocable Trusts involve a permanent transfer of ownership to an independent trustee, commonly designed to minimize estate taxes or protect assets from future creditors. Reaching the underlying corpus of an irrevocable trust is more complex because the grantor no longer legally owns the assets. However, if the injury occurred directly on real estate held inside the irrevocable trust, the trust itself is the primary tortfeasor asset pool, and its commercial insurance policies and real estate equity can be attached to satisfy judgments. Furthermore, if a debtor transferred property into an irrevocable trust after an accident occurred to avoid paying damages, courts will void the transfer as a fraudulent conveyance.

How to Pursue a Personal Injury Lawsuit Involving Trust Property

Follow this systematic legal procedure to identify trust ownership, name the appropriate trustee defendant, and recover fair compensation.

  1. Perform a County Deed Search to Confirm Title Ownership

    Search local county land recording records to obtain the current deed, verifying whether the property is titled in the name of a trustee or specific trust entity.

  2. Identify the Trustee and Their Exact Representative Capacity

    Review public documents or probate records to locate the full legal name of the active trustee who administers the trust holding title to the real estate.

  3. Request Commercial or Homeowner Liability Insurance Details

    Send a formal notice of representation to the property owner requesting declarations pages for all commercial general liability, landlord, and umbrella insurance policies.

  4. Issue a Formal Notice of Claim to the Trustee and Insurer

    Draft an itemized legal notice outlining the date of injury, mechanism of the accident, premises defects, and preliminary medical expenses incurred.

  5. File a Timely Complaint Naming the Trustee in Fiduciary Capacity

    If settlement negotiations fail, file a formal civil lawsuit within the statute of limitations, naming the trustee as defendant in their official fiduciary capacity.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is a trust considered a separate legal entity in a personal injury lawsuit?

No. Unlike a corporation or LLC, a trust is not a separate legal entity. A trust is a fiduciary relationship where a trustee holds legal title to property, meaning lawsuits must be filed against the trustee in their representative capacity.

Q2: Do you sue the trust directly or sue the trustee?

You sue the trustee in their fiduciary capacity, designated as Trustee of the Named Trust. Under the Uniform Trust Code, this allows the plaintiff to reach the trust property assets and commercial insurance coverage.

Q3: Can a trustee be held personally liable for an injury on trust property?

A trustee is generally not personally liable unless they were personally at fault through gross negligence or intentional misconduct. If standard premises negligence occurred without personal fault, only trust assets and insurance pay the claim.

Q4: Does homeowner or commercial insurance cover personal injuries on trust property?

Yes. Prudent trustees maintain commercial general liability or landlord insurance policies that name both the trust and trustee as insureds. These policies provide legal defense and fund personal injury settlement payouts.

Q5: Can trust assets be seized to satisfy a personal injury judgment?

Yes. If a tort judgment is entered against the trustee in their fiduciary capacity for an injury that occurred on trust property, the court can order trust real estate, bank accounts, and assets liquidated to satisfy the judgment.

Q6: What is the difference between suing a revocable trust and an irrevocable trust?

Assets in a revocable living trust are fully accessible because the grantor retains ownership control. An irrevocable trust protects assets from unrelated outside claims, but injuries that happen on the irrevocable trust property itself can reach those trust assets.

Q7: What happens if a property is transferred into a trust after an injury occurs?

If an owner transfers property into a trust after an accident occurs to shield assets from an anticipated lawsuit, courts will invalidate the transfer under the Uniform Voidable Transactions Act as a fraudulent conveyance.

Q8: What is the statute of limitations to sue a trustee for personal injury?

The statute of limitations is the standard personal injury timeline established by state law, which typically ranges from two to three years from the date the injury occurred on the trust property.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Suing a trust for personal injury requires a clear understanding of trust law, premises liability standards, and procedural civil rules. While you do not sue the trust document itself, naming the trustee in their representative fiduciary capacity allows injured victims to access commercial insurance policies and substantial trust property assets. By conducting thorough county land record searches and engaging skilled personal injury attorneys , injured claimants can navigate complex trust structures and recover complete financial compensation for their medical care and suffering.
