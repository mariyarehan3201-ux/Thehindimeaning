Can You Sue Someone for Murdering Family?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Losing a beloved family member to an intentional act of violence or criminal homicide is an unimaginable tragedy that shatters lives, leaves deep psychological trauma, and brings overwhelming financial instability. While the criminal legal system investigates, arrests, and prosecutes the accused perpetrator on behalf of the state to enforce societal punishment, it does not provide financial restitution or direct civil accountability to the grieving survivors. Many families wonder whether they have the statutory right to bring a civil action directly against the individual responsible for taking their loved one life. The answer under United States tort law is yes: surviving family members and the decedent estate can pursue a wrongful death lawsuit and a survival action in civil court, seeking monetary damages for emotional loss, economic destruction, and the conscious pain endured by the victim.

Criminal Justice Versus Civil Wrongful Death Litigation

Following a violent crime, surviving family members often assume that the criminal trial represents the sole legal mechanism for holding a killer accountable. However, American jurisprudence maintains a strict jurisdictional and philosophical division between the criminal justice system and the civil court system. In a criminal prosecution, the state or federal government initiates charges against the defendant on behalf of public order and society. The surviving family is not a party to the criminal lawsuit; rather, the state district attorney or prosecutor controls all legal decisions, plea negotiations, and sentencing requests. If convicted, the defendant faces penal consequences, including state imprisonment, lifetime incarceration without parole, or capital punishment. Yet criminal courts rarely award meaningful monetary compensation to grieving survivors beyond modest contributions from state crime victim compensation funds.

Conversely, a civil lawsuit for wrongful death is brought directly by the decedent surviving family members or the court-appointed administrator of the decedent probate estate. The primary objective of civil homicide litigation is not incarceration, but financial accountability and civil liability. In civil court, the plaintiff seeks monetary damages to compensate for the economic devastation, lost future earnings, loss of guidance, and emotional anguish caused by the unlawful killing. While criminal defendants enjoy extensive constitutional protections—including immunity from being compelled to testify under the Fifth Amendment—civil defendants do not enjoy total silence without consequence. In a civil trial, if the defendant refuses to answer questions regarding the homicide, the civil jury is legally permitted to draw an adverse inference that truthful answers would have been incriminating.

Most importantly, the burden of proof differs drastically between the two court systems. In a criminal homicide case, the state must prove the defendant guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, which represents the highest evidentiary standard in modern law. Even when prosecutors fail to secure a criminal conviction—whether due to procedural evidentiary exclusions, hung juries, or reasonable doubt—the family can still succeed in civil court. A civil wrongful death case requires only a preponderance of the evidence, meaning the plaintiffs must demonstrate that it is more likely than not (greater than a fifty percent probability) that the defendant caused the fatal harm. The most famous modern illustration is the civil litigation against O.J. Simpson, who was acquitted of double murder in criminal court but subsequently found liable by a civil jury for thirty-three million five hundred thousand dollars in compensatory and punitive damages.

When unexpected fatalities arise within institutional care environments, families routinely investigate wrongful death claims against facilities to demand accountability for organizational negligence. Similarly, when an individual intentionally attacks and terminates a human life, civil law treats the act as an intentional tort comprising battery, assault, and intentional infliction of emotional distress resulting in wrongful death. This distinct legal path empowers grieving families to demand answers, subpoena bank records, and compel depositions even if the criminal prosecution stalls or results in an unexpected acquittal.

Legal Standing and Who Is Entitled to File

Under statutory tort law, not every grieving friend, extended relative, or romantic partner has the lawful authority to initiate a civil lawsuit for murder. This threshold requirement is known as legal standing. Because wrongful death actions did not exist under ancient English common law, they are strictly defined by modern state statutes. Each jurisdiction enforces a statutory hierarchy designating which specific relatives are authorized to bring the claim and receive the distributed settlement or trial verdict proceeds.

In virtually all states, the primary tier of eligible plaintiffs consists of the decedent surviving spouse and surviving biological or legally adopted children. If the decedent was unmarried and had no descendants, standing typically ascends to the surviving parents. In cases involving deceased minors, both parents generally share the legal right to pursue civil litigation. In certain jurisdictions, financially dependent stepchildren, domestic partners, or siblings may be permitted to join the lawsuit if they can document total or substantial economic dependence on the deceased victim at the time of the homicide.

Beyond individual family members, the formal personal representative or executor of the decedent probate estate possesses independent standing to file what is known as a survival action. When someone dies suddenly from violent trauma, probate courts appoint an executor or administrator to manage estate affairs. Alongside addressing practical requirements such as securing personal belongings or commissioning certified biohazard cleanup after a death, the estate administrator handles the decedent outstanding liabilities and pursues claims that accrued to the deceased person prior to their passing.

Navigating these standing rules requires precision, because filing under the wrong legal capacity or omitting an indispensable statutory beneficiary can result in immediate procedural dismissal. Attorneys must examine the decedent family tree, verify the status of pending probate petitions, and ensure that all qualifying heirs are properly notified and represented before serving the civil summons and complaint upon the perpetrator.

Recoverable Damages and Survival Action Differences

When litigating a civil action following an intentional homicide, plaintiffs typically pursue two distinct legal claims simultaneously: a statutory wrongful death claim and a common-law survival action. Although both arise from the same homicidal act, they address separate categories of injury, belong to different legal entities, and provide distinct measures of financial recovery.

A wrongful death action compensates the surviving family members for their personal losses resulting from the death of their loved one. Economic damages under this claim encompass the loss of financial support the decedent would have provided throughout their anticipated working lifespan, based on actuarial life expectancy, career trajectory, and projected wage growth. It also covers the value of lost household services, health insurance benefits, and retirement pensions. Non-economic damages compensate the family for the profound emotional void left behind, legally categorized as loss of consortium, loss of companionship, loss of parental guidance, and mental anguish.

A survival action, by contrast, belongs to the decedent probate estate and seeks compensation for the injuries and suffering the victim endured before passing away. If the victim did not die instantaneously—for example, if they survived several minutes, hours, or days following a physical assault or shooting—the estate can recover substantial damages for the victim conscious physical pain, emotional agony, and impending fear of death. The estate can also recover all emergency medical expenses, trauma surgery costs, and lost wages incurred between the assault and the time of death.

In civil homicide lawsuits, plaintiffs are also entitled to demand punitive damages, sometimes referred to as exemplary damages. While compensatory damages restore financial loss, punitive damages are specifically intended to punish the perpetrator for outrageous, malicious, or intentional wrongdoing and to deter similar acts. While comparative cases involving medical negligence or institutional liability often examine suing for wrongful death to calculate compensatory caps, intentional homicide claims rarely face statutory non-economic damage limitations, empowering civil juries to levy monumental punitive awards against murderers.

Asset Recovery, Insurance Exclusions, and Judgment Enforcement

Securing a multi-million-dollar civil judgment against a murderer in a court of law is a powerful emotional vindication, but collecting that money presents substantial practical hurdles. In legal terminology, many violent criminals are judgment-proof, meaning they possess little or no lawful income, have no bank balances, and are facing decades or lifetimes in prison. Therefore, an experienced civil litigation attorney must conduct an exhaustive financial and asset investigation before proceeding.

A central obstacle in civil homicide lawsuits is the universal intentional acts exclusion found in residential homeowners insurance, renters insurance, and commercial general liability policies. Standard insurance contracts explicitly exclude coverage for bodily injury or death caused intentionally by an insured person. As a result, the perpetrator insurance company will not pay the judgment or settlement for an intentional murder. However, strategic civil attorneys investigate potential third-party liability to identify solvent, insured parties whose negligence contributed to the tragedy:

First, premises liability claims against property owners. If the murder occurred at an apartment complex, shopping mall, hotel, university campus, parking garage, or entertainment venue that lacked adequate security, broken security gates, non-functioning surveillance cameras, or absent security guards despite a history of violent crime, the property owner can be sued for negligent security.

Second, employer liability under negligent hiring, supervision, or retention. If an employer hired a dangerous felon without conducting mandatory background checks, armed them, or failed to terminate them despite credible threats of workplace violence, the corporate employer and its commercial liability insurance can be held liable.

Third, direct asset tracing of the perpetrator. If the killer possesses significant personal wealth, real estate holdings, private business equity, stock portfolios, or anticipated inheritances, a civil judgment can be secured via judgment liens, wage garnishments, bank levies, and property seizures. Civil wrongful death judgments stemming from willful and malicious injury cannot be discharged in personal bankruptcy under Section 523(a)(6) of the United States Bankruptcy Code. Furthermore, civil judgments attach to any future earnings, book royalties, or media rights, reinforcing statutory Son of Sam laws that prohibit criminals from profiting from their violent deeds.

Statutes of Limitations and Legal Procedure

Every state enforces a strict statutory deadline, known as the statute of limitations, within which a wrongful death lawsuit must be filed. In most jurisdictions, this window ranges from one to three years from the exact date of death. Failing to file the lawsuit within this prescribed statutory window forever bars the surviving family from recovering damages, regardless of how overwhelming the evidence of the defendant guilt may be.

However, when a death involves criminal homicide, several vital procedural exceptions often apply. Many state codes provide for the statutory tolling (pausing) of the civil statute of limitations while the criminal investigation and prosecution are actively pending. Tolling ensures that the family does not face an unfair procedural expiration while police detectives and prosecutors build their case. Furthermore, if the killer concealed their identity or fled from law enforcement, the statute of limitations is routinely suspended until the suspect is identified, apprehended, or brought to trial.

Waiting for the criminal trial to conclude before proceeding with the civil suit can provide a massive strategic advantage through the doctrine of collateral estoppel (also known as issue preclusion). If the defendant is convicted of murder in criminal court, that criminal conviction conclusively establishes their civil liability for the killing. Because the defendant was already proven guilty beyond a reasonable doubt, the civil judge can grant partial summary judgment on the question of liability, leaving the civil trial to focus solely on calculating the amount of monetary damages the defendant must pay to the family.

How to Pursue a Civil Lawsuit Against a Perpetrator of Homicide

A structured guide outlining the procedural steps surviving family members take to file a civil wrongful death action following a criminal homicide.

  1. Establish Estate Representation in Probate Court

    Petition the county probate court to open the decedent estate and formally appoint an executor or administrator with legal standing to pursue wrongful death and survival actions.

  2. Retain a Civil Litigation Attorney Specializing in Intentional Torts

    Engage an experienced civil trial attorney on a contingency basis who has a proven background in litigating intentional homicide, violent crimes, and negligent security claims.

  3. Coordinate with Criminal Prosecutors and Monitor Trial Proceedings

    Work with the district attorney office to monitor evidence collection and witness statements, evaluating whether to pause the civil suit until the criminal conviction is secured.

  4. Conduct Comprehensive Financial Discovery and Third-Party Investigations

    Perform forensic asset searches on the perpetrator real estate and bank holdings while investigating potential third-party premises liability or negligent security claims against commercial property owners.

  5. File the Civil Summons and Complaint to Enforce Judgment Remedies

    File the formal civil action within the applicable statute of limitations, obtain a civil verdict or settlement, and place permanent judgment liens on the defendant assets to prevent bankruptcy discharge.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can you sue someone for murder if they were acquitted in criminal court?

Yes, you can sue someone for wrongful death even if they were acquitted in criminal court. Criminal courts require proof beyond a reasonable doubt, whereas civil courts require only a preponderance of the evidence, meaning it is more likely than not that the defendant caused the death. The civil trial against O.J. Simpson is the classic example of an individual acquitted in criminal court but held civilly liable.

Q2: Who receives the money from a wrongful death lawsuit for homicide?

The distribution of wrongful death proceeds is dictated by state statute. Generally, proceeds are distributed directly to statutory beneficiaries such as the surviving spouse, children, or parents. Damages recovered through a companion survival action are paid into the decedent probate estate and distributed according to the decedent valid will or state intestacy laws.

Q3: Does homeowners insurance pay for a wrongful death lawsuit involving murder?

No, standard homeowners and personal liability insurance policies contain strict intentional acts exclusions, meaning they do not cover deliberate violent crimes or intentional homicide. However, civil attorneys frequently investigate whether negligent third parties, such as property owners with inadequate security or commercial employers, contributed to the circumstances of the crime.

Q4: What happens if the murderer has no money or assets?

If the perpetrator has no assets or income, they are considered judgment-proof, meaning that while you can win a civil verdict, collecting the funds directly from the individual may be impossible. In such scenarios, attorneys look for viable third-party liability claims against businesses or property owners whose negligence allowed the crime to happen.

Q5: Can a civil judgment for wrongful death be discharged in bankruptcy?

No. Under Section 523(a)(6) of the United States Bankruptcy Code, debts arising from willful and malicious injury caused by the debtor to another entity or the property of another entity cannot be discharged in bankruptcy. A civil judgment for intentional homicide remains permanently enforceable against the perpetrator.

Q6: How long do you have to sue someone for murdering a family member?

The statute of limitations for wrongful death generally ranges from one to three years from the date of death, depending on the state. However, many states toll or pause the civil statute of limitations while the criminal case is pending, or extend the deadline indefinitely when intentional criminal homicide has occurred.

Q7: Can the defendant plead the Fifth Amendment in a civil wrongful death trial?

A defendant in a civil lawsuit can invoke the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination to avoid answering questions. However, unlike in a criminal trial where silence cannot be held against the accused, a civil jury is legally allowed to draw an adverse inference that the defendant truthful answers would have been incriminating.

Q8: Are punitive damages available in civil homicide lawsuits?

Yes, punitive damages are routinely awarded in civil lawsuits involving intentional violent crimes and murder. Punitive damages are designed specifically to punish the perpetrator for malicious, willful, and egregious misconduct and to deter others from committing similar acts.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Enduring the murder of a family member is an agonizing journey that tests the emotional and physical resilience of any household. While nothing can restore the life that was taken, the civil justice system offers grieving survivors an independent pathway to confront the perpetrator directly, reveal the full scope of their wrongful acts, and demand profound financial accountability. By understanding the critical differences between criminal and civil proceedings, establishing proper estate standing, exploring third-party premises liability, and securing un-dischargeable civil judgments, families can obtain a measure of tangible justice and financial security as they rebuild their lives.
