Non Friable Asbestos Removal

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Non friable asbestos removal encompasses the specialized regulatory procedures, mechanical engineering techniques, and safety protocols governing the remediation of bonded asbestos-containing building materials. Under federal EPA NESHAP and OSHA regulations, non-friable materials are defined as products where mineral fibers are tightly bound inside a solid matrix—such as vinyl, asphalt, cement, or rubber—and cannot be crumbled, pulverized, or reduced to powder by hand pressure when dry. Removing these resilient materials requires meticulous work practices to prevent mechanical damage from transforming them into hazardous friable dust.

Regulatory Classification: Category I vs. Category II Non-Friable Materials

Under the EPA Clean Air Act National Emission Standards for Hazardous Air Pollutants (NESHAP, 40 CFR Part 61, Subpart M), non-friable asbestos materials are divided into two distinct legal categories with differing regulatory requirements. Category I non-friable asbestos-containing materials (ACM) include resilient floor coverings (such as 9x9 vinyl asbestos tile and vinyl composition tile), asphalt roofing products, packings, and compressed gaskets. Because these materials feature durable organic binders, NESHAP regulations generally exempt intact Category I ACM from strict demolition removal requirements, provided they are not subjected to sanding, grinding, mechanical chipping, or burning.

In contrast, Category II non-friable ACM includes all other non-friable materials, most notably transite asbestos-cement shingles, corrugated exterior siding panels, and transite water conduits. Because cementitious binders become brittle with weathering and UV radiation exposure, Category II materials have a significantly higher probability of shattering, crushing, or crumbling during structural demolition. Consequently, NESHAP mandates that Category II materials must be completely removed prior to demolition if there is a reasonable probability they will become crumbled or pulverized during the process.

Work Practices: Manual Unbolting, Wet Stripping, and Chemical Dissolution

Executing non friable asbestos removal safely requires avoiding any high-speed mechanical force that could fracture the binding matrix. For exterior transite cement siding and roofing panels, work crews utilize non-destructive manual unbolting techniques. Technicians apply amended water mist to fastener heads and panel seams, carefully unscrewing fasteners or cutting nail shanks with manual hand clippers without cracking the rigid cement panels. Whole panels are lowered to the ground using mechanical hoists rather than being dropped, and immediately wrapped in 6-mil polyethylene sheeting.

For resilient floor coverings, contractors employ chemical mastic dissolution and infrared radiant tile poppers rather than abrasive floor scrapers or power grinders. Infrared heating units gently warm the vinyl tile, softening the underlying black cutback mastic and allowing intact tiles to be lifted off cleanly with a flat putty knife. To remove the remaining black mastic adhesive, technicians apply low-VOC, soybean-based chemical dissolvers that liquefy the asphalt matrix into a wet sludge that is squeegeed into disposal drums, completely eliminating airborne dust generation.

OSHA Class II Standards, Air Monitoring, and Waste Manifesting

Under OSHA Construction Standard (29 CFR 1926.1101), non-friable asbestos removal is officially categorized as Class II asbestos work. Although Class II operations on intact non-friable materials may not require the elaborate multi-stage negative-pressure containment mandated for Class I friable work, employers must still implement strict engineering controls. These include establishing a regulated work area, enforcing personal breathing-zone air monitoring, using drop cloths and continuous wet misting, and equipping workers with half-mask P100 HEPA respirators.

Disposal requirements for non-friable asbestos depend on whether the material remained intact throughout removal. Under EPA and state solid waste regulations, intact Category I and Category II non-friable materials can frequently be disposed of in approved construction and demolition (C&D) landfills, provided they are packaged to prevent breakage during transport. However, if non-friable materials become broken, sawed, or crumbled during removal, they are legally transformed into Regulated Asbestos-Containing Material (RACM), requiring double 6-mil poly bagging, hazardous warning labels, and disposal in a permitted asbestos landfill under waste manifest.

How to Safely Execute Non Friable Asbestos Removal

Follow these five professional steps to safely and legally remove non-friable asbestos materials like floor tiles or transite siding.

  1. Verify Non-Friable Status with an Inspector

    Have a certified asbestos inspector assess the material condition to confirm it is non-friable and not crumbling.

  2. Establish Regulated Work Zone and Drop Poly

    Isolate the work area, restrict unauthorized entry, and lay 6-mil poly drop cloths beneath the removal zone.

  3. Utilize Continuous Wet Misting and Hand Tools

    Saturate materials with amended water and use only manual hand tools; never use circular saws, sanders, or power chippers.

  4. Carefully Wrap and Seal Intact Materials

    Wrap intact transite panels or package lifted vinyl tiles in double 6-mil poly sheeting, sealing edges with duct tape.

  5. Transport Waste Under Proper Landfill Manifest

    Deliver packaged non-friable waste to an authorized landfill permitted to receive asbestos construction debris.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What is non-friable asbestos?

Non-friable asbestos refers to materials where mineral fibers are firmly locked inside a solid matrix (like vinyl or cement) and cannot be crumbled into powder by hand pressure.

Q2: What are examples of non-friable asbestos materials?

Common examples include 9x9 vinyl asbestos floor tiles, black asphalt cutback mastic, transite cement siding and roofing shingles, and automotive brake pads.

Q3: Can non-friable asbestos become friable?

Yes, if non-friable materials are sanded, ground, sawed, crushed, or severely weathered by age, the matrix breaks down and releases dangerous friable airborne fibers.

Q4: Do you need negative air containment to remove non-friable asbestos?

Not always; if intact non-friable materials are removed using manual hand tools and wet methods without breakage, modified OSHA Class II containment may be used.

Q5: What tools are strictly prohibited during non-friable asbestos removal?

High-speed power saws, abrasive grinders, pneumatic chipping hammers, and dry floor sanders are strictly illegal because they pulverize the matrix into dust.

Q6: How are asbestos floor tiles removed without creating dust?

Contractors use infrared tile lifters to gently heat and soften the mastic, allowing whole tiles to be popped off cleanly with a putty knife without breaking.

Q7: Where can non-friable asbestos waste be disposed of?

Intact non-friable asbestos can often be disposed of in designated construction landfills, but if broken or crumbled, it must go to a regulated asbestos landfill under manifest.

Q8: Is non-friable asbestos dangerous if left alone?

Undisturbed, intact non-friable asbestos poses virtually zero health risk to building occupants because the fibers are encapsulated within the solid binder.
