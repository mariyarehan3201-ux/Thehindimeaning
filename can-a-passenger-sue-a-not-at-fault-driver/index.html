Can a Passenger Sue a Not at Fault Driver?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

No, a passenger cannot successfully sue a driver who is truly 100 percent not at fault for a car accident because civil personal injury lawsuits require proof of negligence. However, a passenger can file first-party insurance claims (such as MedPay or PIP) against the not-at-fault driver's policy for medical bills, or sue them if comparative negligence investigations show they shared partial blame.

The Legal Core: Why Fault Dictates Lawsuit Viability

When an automobile collision occurs, passengers frequently suffer severe bodily injuries while riding in a vehicle operated by a friend, family member, coworker, or rideshare driver. If the other vehicle's driver ran a blatant red light, violated right-of-way rules, or was intoxicated, the host driver who carried the passenger may have had zero control over the impending disaster. In such scenarios, injured passengers often wonder: can I sue the driver of the car I was riding in, even if they were not at fault?

In American civil tort jurisprudence, an injured plaintiff cannot win a lawsuit against an individual simply because they were involved in an unfortunate incident. To prevail in a personal injury action against any motorist, the plaintiff bears the mandatory legal burden of proving the four foundational elements of negligence: duty of care, breach of duty, proximate causation, and actual damages. If a motorist exercised reasonable care, obeyed all traffic regulations, maintained a safe speed, and did nothing to cause the collision, there is no breach of duty. Suing a motorist with zero legal fault will result in prompt summary dismissal by the court.

However, there is a profound difference between suing a driver for civil liability and collecting first-party no-fault insurance benefits under that driver's automobile insurance policy. Furthermore, police reports declaring a driver 'not at fault' are preliminary administrative opinions that do not bind civil courts or insurance claims adjusters. The table below delineates the critical distinction between liability lawsuits and first-party insurance claims for passengers.

The Reality of Comparative Fault: Is the Driver Truly 0% at Fault?

While an innocent driver cannot be held liable, an injured passenger's legal counsel will rigorously investigate whether the host driver was truly zero percent at fault. In complex multi-vehicle collisions, liability is rarely an absolute 100-to-0 proposition. Under the legal doctrines of pure comparative negligence or modified comparative negligence recognized in nearly all states, fault can be apportioned between multiple drivers.

For example, suppose Driver A ran a red light at an intersection and collided with the host vehicle driven by Driver B. While Driver A committed the primary traffic violation, forensic accident reconstruction or dashcam telematics might reveal that Driver B was traveling 15 miles per hour over the posted speed limit, looking down at a GPS smartphone, or had plenty of time to honk or brake to avoid the collision. In such a scenario, a jury may allocate 85 percent of fault to Driver A and 15 percent of fault to Driver B.

Because passengers bear zero responsibility for vehicle operation, an injured passenger can name both drivers as co-defendants in a personal injury lawsuit. Under joint and several liability rules in many jurisdictions, establishing even 10 to 20 percent fault against the host driver opens up their bodily injury liability policy limits to compensate the passenger. The table below outlines how comparative negligence frameworks impact passenger recoveries across multi-driver collisions.

Accessing MedPay and PIP Without Suing the Driver

One of the most compassionate and practical features of automobile insurance is that an injured passenger can receive substantial financial compensation from the host driver's policy without ever suing them or asserting that they did anything wrong. This is accomplished through first-party Medical Payments (MedPay) or Personal Injury Protection (PIP) endorsements.

MedPay and PIP are contractual no-fault coverages built directly into the policy of the vehicle in which you were traveling. When an accident occurs, any occupant in the vehicle is an insured person under these provisions. Filing a MedPay claim simply involves submitting your emergency room and doctor bills to the host driver's insurance adjuster. The insurer pays medical providers directly up to the policy limit (commonly $5,000, $10,000, or $25,000) regardless of who caused the wreck. This process does not involve filing a lawsuit, does not require the driver to appear in court, and under state insurance regulations, cannot cause the host driver's insurance rates to increase if they were not at fault.

How an Injured Passenger Can Recover Medical Compensation

Step-by-step procedure for an injured passenger to recover compensation across all available auto insurance policies.

  1. File First-Party PIP / MedPay with Host Vehicle Insurer

    Submit your immediate hospital bills to the insurance carrier of the car you were riding in to tap into no-fault MedPay or PIP benefits.

  2. File Bodily Injury Liability Claim Against At-Fault Driver

    Open a formal liability claim against the negligent driver whose speeding, red light running, or distraction caused the collision.

  3. Investigate Potential Comparative Fault of Host Driver

    Have your personal injury attorney review intersection video, witness testimony, and skid marks to confirm if the host driver shared any percentage of blame.

  4. Access Household Underinsured Motorist (UIM) Coverage

    If total damages exceed the at-fault driver's policy limits, submit an underinsured motorist claim through your own household auto policy.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Will suing a host driver ruin their credit or take their house?

No. Personal injury claims are paid by the automobile insurance company up to the policy limits, not directly out of the driver's personal savings or home equity.

Q2: Can I collect MedPay from a friend's car without suing them?

Yes. MedPay is a no-fault coverage designed to pay medical bills for passengers automatically without any lawsuit, court appearance, or allegation of fault.

Q3: What if the police report says the host driver had zero fault?

Police reports are administrative summaries and are generally inadmissible to prove civil fault. Independent legal investigation may reveal shared comparative negligence.

Q4: Does an injured passenger ever bear comparative fault?

Only in rare circumstances, such as knowingly riding with an obviously intoxicated driver, grabbing the steering wheel, or failing to wear a seatbelt.

Q5: Can I sue both drivers if they both made mistakes?

Yes. In multi-car accidents, an innocent passenger routinely names both drivers as co-defendants to ensure full compensation from all available insurance policies.

Q6: Can a family member passenger sue the driver if they live in the same house?

In some states, household exclusion clauses in insurance policies limit or bar family members residing together from suing each other's liability policies.

Q7: What if the at-fault driver has no insurance at all?

The passenger can recover through the host vehicle's Uninsured Motorist (UM) policy or their own personal household auto insurance policy.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

You cannot successfully sue a driver who possesses zero legal fault for a car accident because liability requires proving actionable negligence. However, an injured passenger can access the host driver's no-fault MedPay or PIP benefits immediately to cover medical bills, tap into their Underinsured Motorist policy if the at-fault driver carried insufficient insurance, or name the host driver if independent evidence reveals they shared comparative blame. Consulting a personal injury lawyer ensures all insurance sources are maximized while maintaining personal relationships.
