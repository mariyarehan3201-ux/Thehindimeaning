Is a DUI a Misdemeanor in Florida?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

In Florida, driving under the influence is generally charged as a misdemeanor for first and second offenses that do not involve aggravating circumstances such as serious bodily injury or extensive property destruction. However, the legal classification of a Florida DUI is highly conditional and governed strictly by Florida Statute Section 316.193. Under specific statutory enhancements, repeated infractions or severe harm automatically elevate what is typically a misdemeanor charge into a serious third-degree or second-degree felony punishable by multi-year prison sentences.

Understanding Florida DUI Statutes and Misdemeanor Classifications

Under Florida Statute Section 316.193, an individual commits the offense of driving under the influence if they operate or are in actual physical control of a motor vehicle while impaired by chemical substances, controlled substances, or alcoholic beverages, or when maintaining an unlawful blood alcohol level or breath alcohol level of 0.08 grams or higher per 100 milliliters of blood or 210 liters of breath. For the overwhelming majority of first-time offenders, Florida law classifies the violation as a first-degree misdemeanor.

Although categorized as a misdemeanor, the statutory penalties for a Florida DUI conviction are among the most stringent in the nation. A first-time conviction carries up to six months of imprisonment in county jail, monetary fines ranging between $500 and $1,000, a minimum six-month driver license revocation, fifty hours of mandatory community service, completion of a state-approved substance education program, and twelve months of probation. If the drivers blood alcohol concentration measures 0.15 percent or higher, or if a minor passenger occupied the vehicle at the time of the offense, statutory enhancements raise maximum jail exposure to nine months and baseline fines up to $2,000.

A second DUI offense occurring outside a five-year statutory window from a previous conviction also remains a first-degree misdemeanor, though judges face increased minimum penalties including up to nine months in jail and mandatory installation of an ignition interlock device on all vehicles operated by the offender. When Does a Florida DUI Escalate to a Felony?

While the baseline charge remains a misdemeanor, Florida law outlines specific statutory triggers that immediately transform an arrest into a felony prosecution. The most frequent catalyst for felony elevation is prior conviction history. When a driver is convicted of a third DUI within ten years of a prior conviction date, Florida Statute Section 316.193(2)(b) reclassifies the offense as a third-degree felony. A third-degree felony conviction carries a potential penalty of up to five years in state prison, a mandatory thirty-day jail term, fines up to $5,000, and a ten-year driver license revocation.

Furthermore, any fourth or subsequent DUI conviction within a motorists lifetime is automatically prosecuted as a third-degree felony, regardless of how many decades have elapsed between infractions. Beyond prior convictions, physical injury enhancements fundamentally alter the nature of the charges. If an impaired motorist causes an accident resulting in serious bodily injury to another human being defined as physical impairment creating a substantial risk of death, serious personal disfigurement, or protracted loss of bodily function the offense is charged as a third-degree felony with severe Florida Criminal Punishment Code sentencing scoresheet consequences.

The most catastrophic escalation occurs when an impaired driving collision results in a fatality. Florida prosecutes DUI manslaughter as a second-degree felony punishable by up to fifteen years in state prison, mandatory minimum incarceration terms, and permanent lifetime driver license revocation. Administrative Versus Criminal Suspensions: The Critical 10-Day Rule

An arrest for DUI in Florida initiates two parallel, independent legal proceedings: the criminal case adjudicated in county or circuit court, and the administrative license suspension managed by the Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles (FLHSMV). Under Floridas implied consent statute, any motorist operating a vehicle within the state is deemed to have consented to approved breath, urine, or blood testing upon lawful request by a law enforcement officer having probable cause.

If an arrested driver registers a breath alcohol concentration of 0.08 or higher, the administrative suspension lasts for six months. If the driver refuses testing, the administrative suspension spans one full year for a first refusal, or eighteen months with a concurrent misdemeanor charge for a second refusal. Motorists have exactly ten calendar days from the date of arrest to challenge this suspension by filing for a formal administrative review hearing or waiving review to obtain an immediate business-purpose hardship permit. Missing this ten-day deadline results in a hard suspension period during which no driving privileges exist.

Effective Defense Strategies Against Florida DUI Charges

Facing a misdemeanor DUI charge does not mean a conviction is inevitable. Seasoned Florida criminal defense lawyers utilize rigorous analytical defenses to dismantle the states case before trial. First, defense counsel scrutinizes the initial traffic stop. If law enforcement lacked reasonable articulable suspicion of an ongoing traffic violation or criminal activity, all downstream evidence obtained during the encounter including chemical breath tests and field sobriety performance is subject to suppression under Fourth Amendment exclusionary doctrines.

Second, attorneys challenge the scientific reliability of standardized field sobriety exercises, such as the horizontal gaze nystagmus, walk-and-turn, and one-leg stand. Environmental elements like uneven road surfaces, high winds, poor lighting, or physical medical conditions such as vertigo, orthopedic knee injuries, or advanced age routinely skew test performance. Third, defense teams inspect maintenance records, calibration logs, and software agency inspection reports for the Intoxilyzer 8000 breath testing machine, looking for instrument deviations or non-compliance with Florida Administrative Code Rule 11D-8 requirements.

    Exercise your constitutional rights during traffic stop questioning and booking; politely decline voluntary questioning without counsel present.

    Submit a formal request for an administrative hearing or select the eligibility waiver for an immediate hardship license within ten calendar days of arrest.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What is the legal blood alcohol limit for a misdemeanor DUI in Florida?

The legal threshold for driving under the influence in Florida is a blood alcohol concentration or breath alcohol concentration of 0.08 percent or higher. Operating a vehicle at or above this standard allows prosecutors to establish per se impairment under Florida Statute Section 316.193.

Q2: Can a first-time misdemeanor DUI charge in Florida be dismissed or reduced?

Yes, many Florida judicial circuits provide first-time misdemeanor offender diversion initiatives, often called Back on Track or Tiered DUI Diversion. Successful completion of community service, education, and probation can result in charges being reduced to reckless driving or dismissed entirely.

Q3: How long does a misdemeanor DUI conviction remain on your Florida record?

Under Florida law, a DUI conviction can never be expunged or sealed. It remains on your permanent criminal background record and your public Florida driving history for seventy-five years, making early legal defense essential.

Q4: Is jail time mandatory for a first misdemeanor DUI conviction in Florida?

Jail time is not mandatory for a standard first-time DUI conviction where blood alcohol is below 0.15 and no injuries occurred. However, judges possess statutory authority to sentence a first-time misdemeanor offender to up to six months in county jail.

Q5: What triggers a felony DUI charge under Florida law?

A Florida DUI becomes a felony if it is a third offense occurring within ten years of a prior conviction, a fourth or subsequent lifetime offense, or any DUI incident that results in serious bodily injury or death to another individual.

Q6: How does a high blood alcohol concentration affect misdemeanor DUI penalties?

If an offenders blood or breath alcohol level tests at 0.15 or higher, the offense remains a first-degree misdemeanor, but statutory penalties escalate significantly. Maximum jail exposure rises from six to nine months, fines increase to between $1,000 and $2,000, and an ignition interlock device becomes mandatory.

Q7: What is the 10-day rule following a DUI arrest in Florida?

The 10-day rule refers to the strict statutory deadline requiring drivers to challenge their administrative driver license suspension with the Florida Department of Highway Safety and Motor Vehicles within ten calendar days of their arrest date.

Q8: Can I refuse a breathalyzer test during a Florida DUI stop?

Under Floridas implied consent law, refusing a lawful breath test results in an immediate administrative driver license suspension of one year for a first refusal. A second refusal constitutes a separate first-degree misdemeanor charge punishable by up to a year in jail.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

While a standard first or second driving under the influence charge in Florida is legally classified as a first-degree misdemeanor, treating it lightly is a grave mistake. The financial penalties, mandatory driver license revocations, vehicle impoundment s, and permanent criminal history attached to a misdemeanor conviction can derail professional careers and personal independence. Understanding the critical dividing line between misdemeanors and felonies empowers motorists to seek immediate, aggressive legal defense.
