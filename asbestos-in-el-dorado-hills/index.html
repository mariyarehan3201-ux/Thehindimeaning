Asbestos in El Dorado Hills: NOA Guide

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 23, 2026

Asbestos in El Dorado Hills, California, represents one of the most prominent cases of naturally occurring asbestos (NOA) contamination and environmental regulation in the United States. Located in the Sierra Nevada foothills of El Dorado County east of Sacramento.

El Dorado Hills experienced explosive suburban master-planned residential growth beginning in the 1990s. However, the region sits directly on a geological belt of ultramafic serpentine rock formations laced with extensive natural veins of amphibole asbestos, predominantly tremolite and actinolite. Unlike manufactured building products installed indoors, naturally occurring asbestos is embedded in native soils, roadbeds, and rock outcroppings.

When residential housing tracts, community parks, school athletic fields, and road infrastructure were graded and excavated throughout the late 1990s and 2000s, heavy machinery crushed serpentine rock, releasing respirable amphibole dust into ambient air basins. This environmental crisis prompted extensive federal EPA investigations and led to landmark air quality ordinances governing land development across Northern California.

Geological Formations and the EPA El Dorado Hills Exposure Study

The controversy surrounding El Dorado Hills centered on the physical differences between common commercial chrysotile and toxic amphibole minerals. The table below delineates the geological mineralogy and the findings of the landmark 2004-2005 EPA air monitoring study.

Investigation Parameter Geological / Technical Characteristic EPA Study Findings (2004 - 2005) Health & Environmental Significance
Dominant Mineral Variety Amphibole group (Tremolite, Actinolite) Rigid, sharp, needle-like silicate microfibers Significantly more biopersistent in lung tissue than chrysotile
Geological Host Matrix Serpentinite and ultramafic rock formations Veins intersect surface soils and hillside cuts Easily crushed by bulldozers, bicycle tires, or garden tillers
Athletic & Playground Testing Simulated sports activities (baseball, soccer) Personal air monitors recorded substantial fiber spikes Demonstrated personal activity creates high localized breathing zones
School Environmental Audits Oak Ridge High School campus remediation Unpaved sports fields contaminated with raw veins Required multimillion-dollar soil capping and synthetic turf replacement

In 2004, the EPA Region 9 Superfund division conducted an intensive, simulated-activity air monitoring study across El Dorado Hills public parks, trails, and school grounds. Environmental technicians wore personal air sampling pumps while jogging, playing basketball, and sliding on baseball diamonds. The laboratory analysis revealed elevated concentrations of airborne amphibole asbestos fibers in the personal breathing zones of participants, far exceeding ambient background levels.

The findings demonstrated that while stationary ambient air monitors in a neighborhood might register low fiber counts, any physical activity that disturbs dry surface soil—such as children playing sports, lawnmowers kicking up dust, or mountain biking along dirt trails—creates a high-density dust plume directly in the individual's breathing zone.

El Dorado County Dust Mitigation Ordinances and Land-Use Rules

In response to the environmental crisis, the El Dorado County Air Quality Management District (EDCAQMD) and the California Air Resources Board (CARB) enacted strict dust control ordinances. The table below outlines these regulatory standards.

Regulatory Rule Governing Agency Target Operations Mandatory Engineering Controls
Rule 223-2 (Asbestos Dust Mitigation) El Dorado County AQMD Construction & earthmoving in NOA zones Approved Dust Mitigation Plan (DMP), water trucks, speed limits < 15 mph
CARB Asbestos Airborne Toxic Control Measure California Air Resources Board Road surfacing, quarrying, site grading Zero use of serpentine aggregate with > 0.25% asbestos on unpaved roads
Soil Capping & Revegetation Standard El Dorado County Environmental Mgmt. New residential & commercial parcels Minimum 3 to 6 inches of clean soil cover, landscaping, or hardscape paving
Property Real Estate Disclosure California Civil Code § 1102 Residential real estate property sales Mandatory written disclosure informing buyers of NOA zone location

Under EDCAQMD Rule 223-2, developers disturbing more than 20 cubic yards of earth in designated asbestos zones must author and submit a comprehensive Asbestos Dust Mitigation Plan (DMP). The plan mandates continuous water spraying during excavation, mandatory tire-washing stations for dump trucks leaving construction sites, perimeter air monitoring stations, and permanent soil capping.

For individual homeowners in El Dorado Hills, practical landscape maintenance rules apply: unpaved driveways must be covered with clean non-asbestos gravel, bare backyard dirt must be planted with grass or covered with mulch, and dry leaf blowing or sweeping of unpaved surfaces is prohibited to prevent kicking up mineral dust.

How Residents and Builders Manage Soil in El Dorado Hills

Standard environmental operating practices for handling soil in naturally occurring asbestos zones.

  1. Verify Property Location on County NOA Map

    Consult the El Dorado County Naturally Occurring Asbestos Review Map to determine whether your parcel is in an area known to contain NOA.

  2. Submit Asbestos Dust Mitigation Plan (DMP)

    Prior to any grading or earthmoving, submit a formal DMP to the El Dorado County Air Quality Management District for permit approval.

  3. Enforce Continuous Wet Earthmoving Practices

    Operate water trucks continuously during digging, grading, and trenching to maintain damp soil conditions and prevent dust plumes.

  4. Install Clean Soil and Hardscape Coverings

    Cap exposed graded soils with at least 3 to 6 inches of clean imported topsoil, dense sod, heavy wood mulch, or concrete flatwork.

  5. Avoid High-Dust Outdoor Landscaping Practices

    Never use power leaf blowers on dry dirt or unpaved driveways; wet down garden beds before planting, and wash dusty outdoor gear outside.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: What is naturally occurring asbestos in El Dorado Hills?

Naturally occurring asbestos (NOA) in El Dorado Hills refers to natural geological veins of tremolite and actinolite amphibole asbestos embedded within native serpentinite rock and soil.

Q2: Is it safe to live in El Dorado Hills with asbestos in the soil?

Yes. Paved roadways, landscaped yards, clean topsoil capping, and county dust mitigation ordinances prevent soil fibers from becoming airborne, keeping ambient air levels low.

Q3: What did the EPA study in El Dorado Hills find?

The 2004-2005 EPA study found that active outdoor sports on unpaved fields stirred up personal dust clouds with elevated amphibole fibers, leading to extensive campus soil capping.

Q4: What kind of asbestos is found in El Dorado Hills?

The area contains amphibole asbestos, predominantly tremolite and actinolite, which form straight, sharp needle-like fibers that are more biopersistent in lung tissue than chrysotile.

Q5: Are real estate disclosures required for asbestos in El Dorado Hills?

Yes. California real estate disclosure laws require property sellers to explicitly inform buyers in writing if a home is located within a designated naturally occurring asbestos zone.

Q6: Can I use a leaf blower in El Dorado Hills?

County air ordinances strongly advise against using power leaf blowers on unpaved dirt, gravel driveways, or dusty surfaces, as the high-velocity air blast aerosolizes soil fibers.

Q7: What is an Asbestos Dust Mitigation Plan in El Dorado County?

A Dust Mitigation Plan is a legally binding construction protocol approved by the county AQMD mandating continuous water spray, vehicle wheel-washing, and air monitoring during earthwork.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

The naturally occurring asbestos situation in El Dorado Hills illustrates that asbestos hazards can exist naturally in pristine outdoor environments. Decades of scientific research, air monitoring, and municipal planning have demonstrated that with rigorous dust suppression ordinances, paved roads, clean soil capping, and informed community awareness, residents can live safely in regions with naturally occurring minerals. Understanding local soil conditions and following county dust guidelines protects respiratory health across the Sierra foothills.
