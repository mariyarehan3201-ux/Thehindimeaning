Asbestos Siding Replacement

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Replacing asbestos cement siding shingles—frequently referred to by historic trade names such as Transite—is one of the most common exterior remodeling projects undertaken on mid-twentieth-century homes. Manufactured extensively from the nineteen-thirties through the nineteen-seventies by combining Portland cement with fifteen to thirty percent chrysotile asbestos fibers, these rigid, fireproof shingles offered unmatched resistance to rot, insects, and weather. Today, homeowners face important decisions regarding whether to completely remove legacy shingles or utilize safe siding-over encapsulation techniques.

Material Characteristics: Transite Durability and Brittleness

Asbestos cement siding shingles were engineered as a virtually indestructible exterior cladding material during the mid-century housing expansion. Pressed into rigid rectangular panels measuring twelve inches by twenty-four inches, these shingles frequently featured decorative wood-grain textures, scalloped edges, or wavy lower margins. The dense Portland cement matrix locked mineral fibers securely in place, creating a non-porous cladding that never rotted, was impervious to termite damage, and earned Class A fire resistance ratings.

However, decades of environmental weathering, ultraviolet sun exposure, and thermal cycling make vintage shingles increasingly brittle. While intact siding is classified under environmental regulations as Category II non-friable asbestos—meaning it does not release airborne fibers under normal conditions—impacts from ladders, tree branches, or uncoordinated removal attempts can cause shingles to shatter cleanly. Fracturing or breaking shingles breaks the cement bond, exposing fibrous edges and creating localized airborne contamination risks.

Replacement Strategies: Complete Removal Versus Siding-Over Encapsulation

When planning an exterior update, homeowners must choose between two primary approaches: complete abatement removal or siding-over encapsulation. Encapsulation is widely recognized by the EPA and building codes as a cost-effective, safe strategy. Contractors install rigid foam insulation board directly over the existing flat shingles, securing the foam with long screws driven into structural wall studs. Modern vinyl, composite, or fiber-cement siding is then installed over the foam, completely entombing the legacy shingles behind an insulated, weather-tight barrier without generating hazardous waste.

Complete removal, by contrast, is necessary when siding is severely cracked, warped, or loose, or when exterior wall modifications (such as window additions or structural insulation upgrades) are planned. Full abatement requires careful manual unfastening of each shingle using specialized nail-cutters or flat pry bars to avoid breaking panels. Technicians continuously mist shingles with surfactant-amended water, lower panels to ground level without dropping them, wrap stacks in heavy six-mil plastic, and manifest them for disposal at a licensed hazardous landfill.

Regulatory Compliance, Worker Safety, and Waste Manifesting

Executing complete asbestos siding removal requires strict adherence to environmental regulations. While exterior siding is non-friable, federal OSHA standards mandate that workers wear appropriate respiratory protection (P100 half-mask respirators) and disposable coveralls. Power saws, abrasive angle grinders, and pressure washers are strictly prohibited, as high-pressure water blasting strips the surface cement and aerosolizes fibers into the air. Ground areas beneath the siding must be lined with polyethylene drop cloths to capture any accidental chips.

Disposal regulations for asbestos cement siding vary by municipality and state. While non-friable siding waste is accepted at many municipal construction landfills, other jurisdictions require it to be manifested and delivered to dedicated hazardous material landfills. Shingle stacks must be wetted, wrapped in double-layer six-mil polyethylene sheeting, sealed with duct tape, and labeled with OSHA warning placards. Homeowners should always retain a copy of the certified waste manifest from their contractor to prove lawful disposal.

Selecting the right replacement strategy ensures an attractive exterior upgrade while upholding environmental safety.

How to Manage an Asbestos Siding Replacement in 5 Steps

Follow these practical steps to plan and execute an exterior asbestos siding replacement project safely.

  1. Evaluate Shingle Condition and Wall Flatness

    Inspect exterior siding for broken, crumbling, or loose panels to determine whether encapsulation or full removal is appropriate.

  2. Consult Local Municipal Building Codes

    Check with your local building department regarding permit mandates and hazardous siding disposal regulations.

  3. Solicit Siding and Abatement Estimates

    Obtain bids from licensed siding contractors experienced in encapsulation, or certified abatement firms if removing.

  4. Enforce Wet Removal and Non-Abrasive Protocols

    If removing shingles, ensure technicians hand-pry nails, mist siding continuously, and never use power circular saws.

  5. Verify Waste Manifest Documentation

    Require your contractor to provide signed copies of the hazardous waste disposal manifest from the receiving landfill.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is it safe to side over old asbestos siding?

Yes, installing rigid foam insulation and new vinyl or composite siding directly over intact asbestos siding is an EPA-approved encapsulation method.

Q2: How much does it cost to remove asbestos siding?

Professional removal typically costs between eight and fifteen dollars per square foot, or roughly eight thousand to twenty thousand dollars for a full house.

Q3: Can a homeowner remove asbestos siding themselves?

In many jurisdictions, single-family homeowners can legally remove siding, but following strict wet-removal and bagging rules is essential.

Q4: Why is pressure washing asbestos siding dangerous?

High-pressure water blasting strips the cement binder and aerosolizes microscopic asbestos fibers, spreading toxic slurry across your yard and neighborhood.

Q5: Can you cut asbestos siding with a circular saw?

No, cutting asbestos siding with high-speed power saws is strictly illegal under OSHA regulations because it generates dense clouds of toxic dust.

Q6: What modern siding looks like vintage asbestos shingles?

Modern non-asbestos fiber-cement shingles (such as James Hardie) offer authentic wood-grain and wavy-edge patterns matching historic shingles.

Q7: Does exterior asbestos siding reduce home value?

It can slightly reduce buyer appeal if buyers anticipate future removal costs, but intact siding is completely safe, fireproof, and durable.

Q8: How is waste asbestos siding transported and buried?

Shingles must be wetted, wrapped in double six-mil poly plastic, labeled with OSHA warnings, and transported under a manifest to an approved landfill.
