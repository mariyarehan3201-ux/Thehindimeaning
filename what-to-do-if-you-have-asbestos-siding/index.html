What to Do if You Have Asbestos Siding?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Discovering that your home has asbestos siding is a common experience for owners of residential properties constructed between the 1920s and late 1970s. Manufactured primarily as cement-asbestos shingles or Transite panels, these exterior claddings blend Portland cement with mineral fibers to create an extremely durable, fireproof exterior envelope. Knowing what to do if you have asbestos siding begins with understanding that intact, undamaged siding poses zero immediate health risk, and learning when encapsulation, painting, or professional abatement is the safest and most economical choice.

Millions of residential homes across North America are clad in vintage cement-asbestos shingles, often chosen by mid-century homebuilders for their rot resistance, insect immunity, and class-A fire ratings. In an undisturbed state, the hazardous mineral fibers are bonded tightly inside a heavy Portland cement matrix, classifying the siding as Category II non-friable material. Understanding what is asbestos in homes helps property owners evaluate exterior claddings realistically, recognizing that stable exterior shingles do not release airborne fibers into living areas unless mechanically fractured, sawed, or abraded.

Assessing the Physical Condition of Asbestos Siding

The first critical action step is performing a comprehensive visual inspection around the entire exterior perimeter. Property owners should inspect each elevation for cracked shingles, broken corners, loose panels, or impact damage from hail, falling tree branches, or lawn care equipment. If the shingles are smooth, intact, and firmly fastened with nails, the EPA and public health agencies recommend leaving them undisturbed, as removing good siding creates far more fiber release risks than maintaining it in place.

However, if panels show extensive chipping, surface pulverization, or water intrusion behind the cladding, remediation becomes necessary. Reviewing historical definitions such as what is the meaning of asbestos clarifies why the tensile strength that made these mineral silicates popular in construction also makes them indestructible within biological tissues when inhaled. Consequently, physical maintenance must be executed with extreme care to avoid converting non-friable cement into respirable dust.

Encapsulation, Painting, and Siding Over Existing Panels

For siding that is structurally sound but cosmetically faded, surface encapsulation provides an outstanding, cost-effective solution. Homeowners should consult specialized guides on how to paint asbestos siding to ensure proper surface preparation without releasing toxic particles. Under no circumstances should anyone power wash, sand, or wire-brush asbestos siding before painting; instead, clean the panels gently using a garden hose and non-abrasive detergent, followed by high-build elastomeric or latex exterior paint to seal microscopic fibers.

Another popular option is re-siding directly over the existing asbestos shingles with modern vinyl or composite siding. Contractors install a layer of rigid polystyrene fan-fold insulation directly over the asbestos panels, which creates a smooth underlayment while adding thermal resistance. When hanging new siding, technicians use pre-drilled pilot holes or non-impact fastening systems to avoid shattering the underlying brittle shingles, effectively encapsulating the material behind a fresh exterior facade.

When Full Removal Is Necessary and Cost Projections

Complete removal of asbestos siding becomes mandatory when the building is scheduled for structural demolition, when severe weather has shattered large sections of the exterior envelope, or when a homeowner desires complete elimination of hazardous materials for property value optimization. Full removal requires hiring state-licensed asbestos abatement contractors who deploy ground containment tarps, continuous amended water wetting, and HEPA vacuum cleanup.

Professional removal costs typically range between $8 and $16 per square foot, which equates to approximately $12,000 to $25,000 for a standard two-story residential dwelling. This budget covers EPA and municipal permitting fees, specialized manual labor, sealed double-bag packaging, and tipping fees at permitted hazardous waste landfills. Once the shingles are safely stripped, property owners can install energy-efficient modern claddings such as fiber-cement lap siding or insulated vinyl panels.

How to Safely Manage and Maintain Asbestos Siding

Follow these five practical homeowner steps to inspect, protect, or plan the remediation of exterior asbestos-cement shingles.

  1. Conduct a Visual Condition Inspection

    Examine exterior walls for cracked shingles, broken corners, or water damage, noting whether panels are stable and intact.

  2. Confirm Content via Laboratory Testing

    If planning renovations, hire an accredited inspector to collect a small chipped piece for Polarized Light Microscopy analysis.

  3. Seal Surfaces with Exterior Elastomeric Paint

    Apply high-grade elastomeric primer and latex topcoats using a soft brush or roller to seal loose surface mineral fibers.

  4. Avoid Power Washing and Power Tool Abrasion

    Never use pressure washers, power sanders, or dry scrapers that pulverize the cement matrix into respirable dust.

  5. Hire Certified Abatement Specialists for Demolition

    Engage licensed environmental contractors equipped with ground drop cloths and wet-removal tools if full replacement is required.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Is asbestos siding dangerous if it is in good condition?

No, asbestos siding in good condition is not dangerous because the hazardous mineral fibers are bonded securely within a Portland cement matrix and cannot become airborne on their own.

Q2: Can I power wash my asbestos siding before painting?

No, power washing asbestos siding is illegal in many jurisdictions and extremely hazardous because high-pressure water strips the cement surface, aerosolizing dangerous fibers across your property.

Q3: Can new vinyl siding be installed over old asbestos siding?

Yes, siding over asbestos shingles with rigid foam backing and vinyl or fiber-cement cladding is a widely accepted and legal method of encapsulating the material safely.

Q4: How much does it cost to remove asbestos siding from a house?

Complete professional removal typically costs between $8 and $16 per square foot, resulting in total costs ranging from $12,000 to $25,000 for an average-sized residential home.

Q5: Can I drill holes in asbestos siding to mount fixtures or wiring?

Drilling into asbestos siding is discouraged, but if necessary, use a low-RPM drill with amended water misting or shaving foam to trap dust, and always wear a NIOSH-approved P100 respirator.

Q6: What type of paint is best for sealing asbestos siding?

High-grade 100% acrylic latex paint or high-build elastomeric exterior coating is ideal because it forms a thick, flexible membrane that seals surface fibers against weathering.

Q7: Does having asbestos siding reduce my home's resale value?

While some buyers may express concern, intact asbestos siding generally does not lower home values significantly if it has been well maintained, painted, or cleanly re-sided over.

Q8: How do I replace a single broken asbestos siding shingle?

You should not attempt to cut original salvage asbestos tiles; instead, purchase modern non-asbestos fiber-cement replica shingles designed specifically to match vintage patterns.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Discovering asbestos siding on your home does not require panic or emergency measures. As long as the shingles remain in good physical condition and are not disturbed by abrasive power tools, they provide durable, weather-resistant protection for decades. By opting for careful cleaning, elastomeric paint encapsulation, or engaging certified abatement professionals when major renovations arise, homeowners can preserve safety, maintain curb appeal, and protect property values.
