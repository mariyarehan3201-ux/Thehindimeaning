What Is a Heggstad Petition?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

A Heggstad Petition is a specialized judicial probate procedure in California governed by California Probate Code § 850. Originating from the landmark 1993 appellate court ruling Estate of Heggstad, this petition allows a successor trustee or beneficiary to legally transfer real property or assets into a deceased person's revocable living trust without going through formal, protracted probate administration.

The Problem: The Unfunded Trust and the Title Gap

In California estate planning, the Revocable Living Trust is the foundational instrument utilized by homeowners to ensure their assets transfer privately, swiftly, and economically to heirs upon death, completely bypassing California's notoriously expensive and bureaucratic probate courts.

However, establishing a living trust is a two-step legal process. First, the estate planning attorney drafts and the client signs the formal Trust Agreement. Second, the client must Fund the Trust. Funding requires executing and recording a new Grant Deed or Quitclaim Deed with the county recorder of deeds, transferring title from the individual's personal name into their name as Trustee of the living trust.

Tragically, thousands of California property owners complete step one but fail to complete step two. A homeowner creates a trust, lists their residential home on the trust's initial Schedule of Assets (Schedule A), but neglects to record the deed. Or, more commonly, the homeowner refinances their home mortgage years later, and the mortgage lender temporarily deeds the property out of the trust into the owner's personal name to close the loan—and the owner forgets to deed the home back into the trust. When the homeowner passes away, title to the multi-million-dollar real estate is frozen outside the trust. The Landmark Ruling: Estate of Heggstad (1993)

Prior to 1993, an unfunded piece of real estate was doomed to full, court-supervised probate administration, forcing heirs to endure years of delays and pay thousands in statutory attorney fees, regardless of what the decedent intended.

This injustice was permanently overturned by the California Court of Appeal in the historic case Estate of Heggstad (1993) 16 Cal.App.4th 943. In that case, Halvard L. Heggstad executed a revocable living trust and attached a written 'Schedule A' inventory explicitly listing his Menlo Park residential real property as a trust asset. However, he never executed or recorded a formal grant deed transferring legal title to the trust prior to his death.

The appellate court ruled that a written declaration of trust by the property owner, identifying the real property as an asset of the trust on an attached schedule, is legally sufficient to create a valid trust in real property under California trust law, even without a separate formal deed conveyance. The court held that the grantor's clear, written expression of intent governs. Today, this doctrine is codified under California Probate Code §§ 850 through 859, providing a streamlined statutory mechanism to confirm trust title. Filing the Petition: Procedure and Courtroom Hearing

Executing a successful Heggstad Petition requires meticulous adherence to California probate court procedural rules.

The successor trustee retains a California probate attorney to draft the formal Verified Petition Under Probate Code Section 850. The petition details the execution of the trust, the historical chain of title deeds, the mortgage refinancing paper trail, and attaches certified copies of the trust agreement, Schedule A, and the county recorder's deed records. Under California Probate Code § 851, formal written notice of the hearing along with a copy of the petition must be served on all trust beneficiaries, intestate legal heirs, and named executors at least thirty days prior to the hearing date.

At the court hearing, the probate judge reviews the verified pleadings. If no disinherited heir files a formal objection contesting the trust's validity, the judge signs the formal Court Order Confirming Trust Ownership of Real Property. The attorney obtains a certified copy of this court order from the clerk of court and records it directly in the county recorder's office where the real estate is located. The recorded court order acts as a legal deed conveyance, instantly clearing title and empowering the successor trustee to manage, refinance, or sell the property.

How to File a California Heggstad Petition

Step-by-step legal roadmap for successor trustees to confirm real estate into a living trust.

  1. Audit Trust Agreement and Schedule of Assets (Schedule A)

    Review the original trust agreement to verify the real estate parcel is explicitly listed by street address or Assessor's Parcel Number (APN).

  2. Retrieve the Historical Recorded Chain of Title Deeds

    Obtain copies of the most recent recorded grant deed and mortgage refinance deeds from the county recorder to document why title was outside trust.

  3. Draft and File Verified Petition Under Probate Code § 850

    Have your probate attorney file the formal petition in the county superior court probate department, securing a scheduled hearing date.

  4. Serve Formal 30-Day Notice and Record the Court Order

    Serve notice on all heirs under Probate Code § 851, attend the hearing, and record the certified signed court order with the county recorder.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can a Heggstad petition be used for bank accounts?

Yes! A Heggstad petition can be used to transfer unfunded brokerage accounts, bank accounts, or business shares explicitly listed in trust schedules.

Q2: How long does a Heggstad petition take in California?

A Heggstad petition typically takes between 60 and 90 days from the filing of the petition to the issuance of the final signed court order.

Q3: How much does a Heggstad petition cost?

Attorney fees for a standard uncontested Heggstad petition typically range from $3,500 to $7,500, plus court filing fees (approximately $435).

Q4: What happens if an heir contests the Heggstad petition?

If an heir objects, the matter enters contested probate litigation, requiring an evidentiary hearing or trial to determine decedent intent.

Q5: Can I file a Heggstad petition without an attorney?

While theoretically possible, probate court procedural rules, notice requirements, and local rules make retaining a probate lawyer strongly advisable.

Q6: What if the house is not listed on Schedule A?

If the house is not on Schedule A, the petition can still succeed if other written documents (pourover will, assignment of assets) prove trust intent.

Q7: Does title insurance accept a Heggstad court order?

Yes. California title insurance underwriters universally recognize certified recorded Probate Code 850 court orders to issue clear title policies.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

A Heggstad Petition is one of the most powerful, cost-saving remedial instruments in California probate law. By honoring the decedent's clear written intent to include real property in their revocable living trust, Probate Code § 850 rescues families from the crushing financial and emotional burdens of full probate administration. If a deceased loved one left behind real estate titled in their individual name but referenced in their trust, consult an experienced California probate attorney immediately to file a Heggstad petition and secure title within months.
