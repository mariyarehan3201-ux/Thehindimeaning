Linguistic Origins: Japanese Etymology and Martial Contractions

In Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu (BJJ), Judo, and classical Japanese Karate, the word OSS (frequently pronounced 'Osu' in Japanese) serves as a universal phonetic greeting, respectful acknowledgment, and expression of relentless determination. While widely popularized globally through the Gracie family and modern grappling culture, the term traces its roots to early twentieth-century Japan, particularly the Kyoto Martial Arts Academy (Butokukai) and Japanese naval academies.

Linguistically, martial scholars identify two primary Japanese origins for the utterance. The most prevalent theory explains OSS as a phonetic contraction of 'Oshi Shinobu' (押し忍ぶ). The first kanji, 'Osu' (押), translates to pushing or exerting force, while the second kanji, 'Shinobu' (忍), translates to enduring, bearing patiently, or persevering through hardship. Combined, Osu encapsulates the definitive martial ethos: to persevere under intense pressure without breaking.

Review linguistic etymologies and contextual translations of OSS in martial arts:

Etymological Source Japanese Kanji Phrasing Literal Linguistic Meaning Martial Arts Application
Oshi Shinobu (Contraction) 押し忍ぶ (Osu + Shinobu) Pushing forward while enduring hardship with patience Persevering when pinned under heavy cross-side control or exhaustion
Ohayō Gozaimasu (Abbreviation) お早うございます Good morning / formal daily greeting Slang abbreviation used by young martial arts cadets entering dojos
Onegai Shimasu (Phonetic) お願いします Please teach me / grant me this favor Mutual bow before beginning a sparring session (randori or rolling)
Arigatō Gozaimashita (Closing) ありがとうございました Thank you deeply for this experience Post-training bow expressing gratitude to instructors and training partners

Mat Culture: Respect, Acknowledgment, and Academy Etiquette

On modern BJJ tatami mats, OSS functions as an all-encompassing verbal tool. When a black belt professor finishes demonstrating an intricate sweep or submission technique, students respond with a crisp, unified 'OSS!' to signal comprehension, gratitude, and readiness to drill. It replaces casual phrases with disciplined martial focus, reinforcing the sacred bond between teacher and student.

Furthermore, practitioners utter OSS when bowing onto or off the mat surface, greeting fellow practitioners with a slap-and-bump before a roll, and accepting promotional belt stripes. However, martial etiquette cautions against overuse outside the dojo. In traditional civilian Japanese culture, 'Osu' is considered rough, overly masculine street slang; in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu academies worldwide, it remains a cherished emblem of camaraderie and shared suffering.

Formal Civilian Interaction Speaking OSS to a civilian waiter or corporate manager Inappropriate; avoided Reserved strictly for martial academies and fellow grappling practitioners

Embodying the true spirit of OSS means carrying patience, grit, and humility far beyond the mats into every dimension of life.

How to Properly Use OSS in a Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu Academy

Adopt proper grappling mat etiquette by following these guidelines on when and how to say OSS.

  1. Bow When Stepping Onto and Off the Tatami

    Pause at the mat edge, face the center of the room, bow from the waist, and murmur OSS respectfully.

  2. Acknowledge Professor Instructions with Conviction

    When your instructor claps twice to end a demonstration, clap in unison and voice a clear OSS.

  3. Slap and Bump Before and After Rolling

    Exchange a hand slap, fist bump, and quiet OSS with your training partner before initiating live sparring.

  4. Congratulate Teammates on Belt Promotions

    Applaud and state OSS when coaches award new colored belts or ranking stripes to dedicated teammates.

  5. Live the Philosophy of Endurance Off the Mats

    Remember that saying OSS obligates you to face life hardships with quiet perseverance and unwavering discipline.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: What does OSS mean in BJJ?

In BJJ, OSS is a respectful martial arts expression meaning perseverance, respect, and acknowledgment, derived from the Japanese phrase 'Oshi Shinobu.'

Q2: What is the literal translation of Oshi Shinobu?

It translates to 'pushing forward while patiently enduring,' representing the determination to persevere through physical and mental hardship.

Q3: Who brought OSS into Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu?

Japanese judo master Mitsuyo Maeda and early Carlson Gracie and Grandmaster Helio Gracie lineages integrated Japanese martial terminology into Brazilian academies.

Q4: Can you say OSS to a Japanese person in public?

Generally no. In mainstream Japan, 'Osu' is seen as rough masculine slang used by male athletes or yakuza, not polite daily speech.

Q5: When should you say OSS in a BJJ class?

Say it when bowing onto the mats, after an instructor explains a technique, before sparring, and when congratulating teammates on promotions.

Q6: Is OSS used in other martial arts?

Yes. OSS is widely used in Kyokushin Karate, Shotokan Karate, Judo, Japanese Jiu-Jitsu, and mixed martial arts (MMA).

Q7: How do you pronounce OSS?

It is pronounced as a sharp, single-syllable 'OSS' or slightly elongated 'OH-soo' with a subtle final vowel.
