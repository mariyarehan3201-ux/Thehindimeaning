Dessercom Asbestos

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

The issue of Dessercom asbestos centers on occupational safety investigations, facility air testing, and hazardous building material abatement across ambulance stations and administrative buildings operated by Dessercom in Quebec, Canada. As one of the largest pre-hospital emergency medical service organizations in North America, Dessercom faced intense scrutiny from paramedic unions and workplace health regulators following discoveries of deteriorating asbestos insulation in aging municipal dispatch barracks.

The Background of the Dessercom Building Exposure Controversy

Dessercom operates numerous paramedic barracks across urban and rural Quebec, many housed in municipal facilities constructed between 1950 and 1975. During structural upgrades and routine maintenance, inspections revealed degraded acoustic plasters, crumbling pipe lagging, and contaminated ceiling plenums directly above ambulance bays and crew sleeping quarters. Paramedics and medical technicians raised urgent concerns regarding secondary fiber inhalation caused by HVAC air circulation.

Quebec workplace safety authority CNESST (Commission des normes, de l'equite, de la sante et de la securite du travail) intervened, ordering comprehensive environmental air monitoring, facility registries, and immediate worker risk protocols. Decades of medical research on cancer caused by asbestos demonstrate that even non-industrial workers who spend long twenty-four-hour shifts in contaminated municipal buildings face measurable risks of pleural thickening, asbestosis, and mesothelioma.

Regulatory Mandates Under Quebec Workplace Safety Codes

Under Quebec environmental statutes and the Regulation Respecting Occupational Health and Safety (ROHS), employers must maintain an up-to-date register of all materials containing more than 0.1 percent asbestos. When building components degrade or become friable, certified remediation specialists must be retained to execute containment under rigid Class I or Class II procedures. Properly managing hazardous residues detailed in comprehensive guides on how to dispose of asbestos guarantees that contaminated construction debris is transported to licensed hazardous waste containment sites.

This led to standardized building inspection protocols across all public emergency response facilities in the province.

Lessons for Municipal and Emergency Service Infrastructure

The Dessercom experience highlights the unique environmental liabilities associated with public service buildings that operate continuously. Unlike office workers who occupy buildings for eight hours, paramedics work extended twelve-to-twenty-four-hour shifts in living quarters, requiring that municipal facilities maintain pristine indoor environmental air quality through rigorous, proactive inspection schedules.

How to Manage Asbestos Contamination in Public Service Facilities

Step-by-step procedures for managing hazardous building materials in 24-hour public service and first responder facilities.

  1. Establish a Formal Facility Asbestos Register

    Conduct a comprehensive bulk sampling survey to document the exact location and physical state of all asbestos materials.

  2. Inspect High-Vibration Mechanical Zones

    Regularly examine ambulance bays, equipment garages, and mechanical rooms where vibrating equipment can dislodge ceiling fibers.

  3. Institute Immediate Class I Containment

    Isolate any damaged or friable materials behind sealed plastic barriers and operate certified HEPA air filtration.

  4. Engage Certified Abatement Specialists

    Contract with licensed remediation firms to remove or encapsulate hazardous products during planned facility downtimes.

  5. Maintain Permanent Occupational Health Records

    Document potential staff exposure histories in permanent employment health files to ensure worker compensation protections.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What is the Dessercom asbestos issue?

It refers to workplace health audits, asbestos discoveries, and facility remediation across ambulance stations and dispatch facilities operated by Dessercom in Quebec.

Q2: Who regulates workplace asbestos in Quebec?

Workplace asbestos safety in Quebec is regulated by the CNESST (Commission des normes, de l'equite, de la sante et de la securite du travail).

Q3: What materials in older ambulance stations commonly contain asbestos?

Common materials include acoustic ceiling plaster, thermal pipe insulation, fireproof structural beam spray, and vinyl-asbestos floor tiles.

Q4: Why were paramedics particularly concerned about building asbestos?

Paramedics work long 12-to-24-hour shifts in station living quarters, and vibrations from heavy garage doors can dislodge brittle fibers into breathing air.

Q5: What is an asbestos register under Quebec law?

An asbestos register is an official employer document listing all materials containing over 0.1% asbestos, their condition, and inspection history.

Q6: What should an employee do if they suspect asbestos in a workplace?

Employees should report the condition immediately to their joint health and safety committee and request formal environmental inspection.

Q7: Can emergency medical workers receive compensation for asbestos illnesses?

Yes, workers diagnosed with recognized asbestos-related occupational diseases can receive medical treatment and disability benefits through the CNESST.

Q8: How is asbestos removed from operational public facilities?

Remediation crews set up sealed negative-pressure containment units with HEPA filtration, often working overnight or during staged department relocations.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

The Dessercom asbestos audits underscore the vital importance of environmental vigilance in public safety infrastructure. By implementing comprehensive building registers, executing certified remediation under regulatory oversight, and establishing transparent medical tracking for emergency medical personnel, emergency service organizations successfully protect first responders from latent toxic hazards while maintaining uninterrupted pre-hospital emergency care.
