Selling to a DSO

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Selling to a Dental Support Organization (DSO) is a major strategic liquidity event where a dentist transitions administrative, billing, and procurement burdens to a corporate dental group in exchange for substantial cash at closing, equity rollover upside, and a multi-year clinical employment agreement.

The Corporate Consolidation of Private Dental Practice

Over the past decade, the American dental landscape has undergone a monumental structural transformation. Historically, private dental healthcare was an intensely fragmented cottage industry dominated by solo practitioner dentists who owned their private practices, managed front-office scheduling, handled insurance billing, and provided patient clinical care. Today, relentless reimbursement pressures from dental insurance PPOs, rising compliance overhead, and soaring student loan debt have catalyzed a massive wave of corporate consolidation.

Enter the Dental Support Organization (DSO). DSOs are specialized corporate management entities—frequently backed by private equity firms or institutional healthcare investors—that purchase the non-clinical business assets of dental practices. Under modern healthcare corporate law, corporate non-dentists are legally prohibited from practicing dentistry (the Corporate Practice of Medicine/Dentistry doctrine). DSOs navigate this regulation by managing marketing, regulatory compliance, payroll, supply chain purchasing, and facilities, while the selling dentist continues to lead clinical patient care.

For practice owners contemplating retirement, debt elimination, or freedom from administrative headaches, selling to a DSO offers unprecedented liquidity and enterprise valuation multiples far exceeding traditional sales to individual associate dentists. However, navigating DSO acquisitions requires an understanding of complex deal structures, tax ramifications, and post-sale clinical commitments. EBITDA Multiples and Practice Valuation Mechanics

The single greatest financial catalyst driving practice owners toward DSOs is practice valuation. In a private sale to an associate, a practice collecting $1,500,000 might sell for 75 percent of collections—yielding approximately $1,125,000. In a DSO acquisition, valuations are calculated using adjusted EBITDA (Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization).

To calculate adjusted EBITDA, a dental CPA performs a process known as Normalization or Recasting. They strip away discretionary owner perks (such as personal automobile leases, family cell phone plans, and non-operational travel) and adjust the doctor's historical compensation down to fair market associate production pay (typically 30 to 35 percent of collections). The remaining net operating profit represents true corporate EBITDA.

DSOs value practices by applying an enterprise multiple to this adjusted EBITDA. The multiple is dictated by practice scale, specialty (pediatric and orthodontic practices command premium multiples over general dentistry), geographic density, and whether the practice operates across multiple satellite locations. A dental group generating $500,000 in clean adjusted EBITDA can command a 6x to 8x multiple, unlocking a $3,000,000 to $4,000,000 enterprise valuation. Deal Structuring: Cash at Close, Rollover Equity, and Employment

When negotiating a letter of intent (LOI) with a DSO, sellers must look far beyond the headline purchase price. Deal structuring determines how much money lands in your bank account, your future tax liabilities, and your daily quality of life.

Most institutional DSO transactions utilize a three-part financial structure: Cash at Closing (typically 70% to 80% of purchase price taxed at favorable long-term capital gains rates), Rollover Equity (15% to 25% rolled into parent company shares), and an Earnout or Holdback (contingent on maintaining collections). Rollover equity allows the selling dentist to participate in the 'second bite of the apple'—when the private equity sponsor recapitalizes or sells the entire DSO platform five years later, the doctor's rollover shares can multiply by 2x to 4x in value.

However, the trade-off is the mandatory Post-Sale Employment Agreement. DSOs are purchasing an operating business driven by your clinical patient relationships. Consequently, every DSO requires the selling doctor to execute a binding employment contract committing to practice dentistry full-time for a mandatory transition window—typically between two and five years. The contract specifies your compensation rate (usually 30% to 35% of personal collections), daily production expectations, and enforces strict, unforgiving Non-Compete Covenants (typically restricting you from practicing within 10 to 15 miles for two to three years post-departure).

How to Prepare and Sell Your Practice to a DSO

Step-by-step M&A roadmap for a dentist evaluating, marketing, and closing a sale to a Dental Support Organization.

  1. Recast Financial Statements to Calculate True EBITDA

    Work with a dental CPA to normalize your P&L, removing personal perks and adjusting doctor compensation to establish verified EBITDA.

  2. Retain a Specialized Dental M&A Broker and Legal Counsel

    Hire an M&A advisory firm that specializes in dental practice transactions to market your practice confidentially to multiple competing DSOs.

  3. Solicit Competing Letters of Intent (LOIs)

    Evaluate multiple competing offers, comparing headline purchase price, cash-at-close percentages, rollover equity terms, and employment commitments.

  4. Complete Due Diligence and Execute Asset Purchase Agreement

    Navigate 60 to 90 days of operational due diligence, negotiate clinical autonomy clauses in your employment contract, and execute closing documents.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: What is a DSO in dentistry?

A Dental Support Organization (DSO) is a corporate entity that manages the business, marketing, HR, and administrative operations of dental practices.

Q2: What EBITDA multiple do DSOs pay for dental practices?

Single practices typically sell for 4x to 7x EBITDA, while multi-location dental groups command premium multiples of 8x to 11x+ EBITDA.

Q3: Can I sell to a DSO and retire immediately?

Generally no. Most DSOs require selling dentists to sign a mandatory 2 to 5-year employment contract to maintain patient retention and production.

Q4: What is rollover equity in a DSO deal?

A portion of your sale proceeds (15% to 25%) rolled into shares of the parent DSO holding company, providing substantial cash upside when the DSO is resold.

Q5: Do DSOs control clinical patient treatment decisions?

Under state dental board laws, non-dentist DSOs are legally prohibited from interfering with clinical diagnosis, materials, or treatment plans.

Q6: How long does it take to close a sale to a DSO?

From signing the initial Letter of Intent (LOI) through financial due diligence to closing, a DSO transaction typically takes 90 to 120 days.

Q7: Are non-compete agreements enforced after selling to a DSO?

Yes. Non-competes associated with the sale of a business goodwill are strictly enforced by courts, typically restricting practice within 10 to 15 miles for 2 to 5 years.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Selling to a DSO is a transformative financial liquidity event that relieves practice owners of administrative headaches while capturing high EBITDA valuation multiples. However, signing a DSO agreement permanently changes your status from autonomous business owner to employed clinical associate. Assembling a specialized deal advisory team—including a dental-specific transaction attorney, CPA, and dental M&A broker—ensures you protect your clinical autonomy, minimize tax liabilities, and maximize enterprise valuation.
