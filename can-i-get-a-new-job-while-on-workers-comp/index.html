Can I Get a New Job While on Workers' Comp?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Suffering an injury on the job often throws your professional career and financial security into turmoil. While receiving workers' compensation benefits, you may find that your former employer cannot accommodate your physical restrictions, or an appealing new career opportunity may present itself at another company. However, accepting a new position while actively receiving workers' compensation benefits involves complex legal and financial considerations. While you are generally not prohibited by law from seeking or starting new employment, taking a new job will directly impact your wage replacement payments, medical treatment authorizations, and pending settlement buyout negotiations.

How New Employment Affects Wage Replacement Benefits (TTD and TPD)

Workers' compensation indemnity benefits are designed to replace lost wages when an occupational injury prevents you from earning your normal income. The most common category of bi-weekly wage replacement is Temporary Total Disability (TTD). TTD benefits are legally predicated on the clinical finding that you are temporarily 100% disabled from performing any gainful work. Therefore, if you accept and begin a new job while receiving TTD benefits, your entitlement to those total disability checks terminates immediately. Continuing to cash TTD checks while performing work and earning compensation is classified under state penal laws as workers' compensation insurance fraud, carrying severe criminal penalties, restitution orders, and permanent benefit forfeiture.

However, transition into new employment does not always mean complete forfeiture of wage benefits if your earning capacity remains diminished. If your treating physician releases you to light-duty or modified work with strict physical limitations, and your new job pays less than your pre-injury average weekly wage (AWW), you may be eligible for Temporary Partial Disability (TPD) or partial wage replacement benefits. In most state jurisdictions, TPD pays roughly two-thirds (66.67%) of the wage differential between what you earned prior to the injury and what you are currently capable of earning at your new lighter-duty position. When evaluating broad career transitions, understanding whether can you quit a job while on workers comp clarifies how voluntary resignations impact your ongoing benefit claims.

The cardinal legal requirement when starting any new job—including part-time employment, remote freelancing, or independent contractor gig work—is immediate, written disclosure. You must formally report your new employment, start date, hourly pay rate, and weekly hours to both the workers' compensation insurance adjuster and your state workers' compensation board. Transparency preserves your legal integrity and prevents insurance fraud investigations.

Medical Benefits and Ongoing Treatment: Does Coverage Continue?

A widespread misconception among injured employees is that starting a new job automatically cancels all workers' compensation benefits, including medical coverage. Under statutory workers' compensation law, medical benefits and wage indemnity benefits operate as separate legal components. The insurance carrier responsible for your initial injury remains legally liable for all reasonable, necessary, and causally related medical treatment until you reach Maximum Medical Improvement (MMI) or enter into a full and final settlement buyout. Even if you resign from your original employer and transition to a new corporation, your existing workers' compensation claim remains open to pay for approved physical therapy, specialist consultations, diagnostic imaging, and necessary surgeries.

Nevertheless, working at a new job introduces serious medical and legal vulnerabilities regarding re-injury or condition aggravation. If your new job requires physical tasks that exceed your authorized medical restrictions, the insurance company will argue that any worsening of your symptoms constitutes a new intervening injury sustained at the new employer rather than an ongoing consequence of the original workplace accident. Injured workers must strictly provide their formal functional capacity evaluation (FCE) or doctor work restriction sheet to their new supervisor.

Impact on Settlement Negotiations and Buyout Value

Accepting a new job has profound strategic ramifications on the financial valuation of your pending workers' compensation settlement. When insurance companies calculate settlement proposals—such as a Compromise and Release (C&R) or Section 32 Agreement—a substantial portion of the calculated lump sum reflects the insurer's future wage indemnity exposure. If you remain out of work with serious medical restrictions and your former employer cannot accommodate you, the insurer faces months or years of mandatory weekly benefit payouts. To eliminate this financial liability, they are typically willing to offer a significant lump-sum settlement payout.

However, the moment you secure and start a new position that pays equal to or greater than your pre-injury wage, the insurance company's future wage indemnity liability evaporates. Armed with proof of your new employment, the claims adjuster will promptly reduce their settlement offer, often limiting negotiations solely to the estimated cost of future medical care. For this reason, seasoned workers' compensation attorneys frequently recommend resolving settlement negotiations prior to officially commencing new employment whenever feasible. Medical Work Restrictions and Employer Accommodation Rules

If you choose to accept a new job while managing an open workers' compensation claim, your physician's physical restrictions must govern every aspect of your daily work activities. Workers' compensation insurance carriers routinely employ private investigators to conduct video surveillance on injured claimants. If an investigator videotapes you lifting 50-pound boxes or loading heavy machinery at a new job when your medical chart restricts you to sedentary work with a 10-pound lifting limit, the insurer will immediately file a fraud complaint with the state insurance commissioner. This evidence will be used to revoke all benefits, seek full restitution, and refer your file for criminal prosecution.

Under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) and applicable state civil rights laws, you have the right to request reasonable accommodations from a new employer for documented physical impairments, provided you can perform the essential functions of the role. You are not obligated to disclose the historical circumstances of your workers' compensation lawsuit during a job interview; however, you must be honest about whether you can perform specific physical tasks with or without reasonable accommodation. How to Transition to a New Job While on Workers' Compensation

Follow this strategic legal and procedural protocol to protect your workers' compensation benefits, report income properly, and prevent allegations of insurance fraud.

  1. Obtain Updated Written Work Restrictions from Your Doctor

    Schedule an examination with your authorized treating physician to obtain an official medical report specifying your precise lifting, standing, sitting, and repetitive motion limits.

  2. Verify That New Job Duties Match Physical Capabilities

    Review the formal job description of the prospective new position to ensure no mandatory daily tasks violate your doctor's designated medical restrictions.

  3. Consult Your Workers' Compensation Attorney Before Accepting

    Discuss the job offer with your attorney to evaluate its impact on your settlement value and determine whether negotiating an expedited buyout is strategically advantageous.

  4. Formally Report New Employment and Earnings in Writing

    Submit written notification of your new employment, start date, hourly rate, and employer details to the insurance adjuster and the state workers' compensation board.

  5. Provide Bi-Weekly Paystubs to Calculate Partial Benefits

    If earning less than your pre-injury average wage, submit consecutive copies of your paystubs to your claims adjuster to receive lawful Temporary Partial Disability (TPD) payments.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I legally accept a new job while receiving workers' compensation?

Yes. There is no law prohibiting an injured worker from seeking or starting a new job. However, you must immediately report your new employment and earnings to the insurance carrier and state board, as your wage replacement benefits will be adjusted or stopped.

Q2: What happens if I work a new job and do not report it to workers' comp?

Failing to report new employment and continuing to cash total disability checks constitutes criminal workers' compensation insurance fraud. You face mandatory repayment of benefits, severe civil fines, loss of future medical coverage, and potential felony criminal prosecution.

Q3: Will my workers' comp medical treatment stop if I start a new job?

No. Medical benefits for your original workplace injury remain the responsibility of the original workers' compensation insurance carrier until you reach maximum medical improvement or agree to a full settlement buyout, regardless of where you currently work.

Q4: Can I get workers' comp partial benefits if my new job pays less?

Yes. If your medical restrictions limit you to lighter work that pays less than your pre-injury average weekly wage, you may qualify for Temporary Partial Disability (TPD) benefits, which generally pay roughly two-thirds of the wage difference.

Q5: Does getting a new job ruin my workers' comp settlement amount?

It can significantly reduce it. Insurance carriers base large settlement buyouts primarily on their liability for future wage replacement payments. If you secure equal or higher-paying work, the carrier's wage liability drops to zero, reducing the settlement offer substantially.

Q6: Do I have to tell my new employer that I have an open workers' comp claim?

You do not have to disclose that you have an open legal claim or past lawsuit. However, you must be honest about whether you have physical restrictions that require reasonable accommodation to perform the essential functions of the job.

Q7: What happens if I aggravate my old injury at the new job?

If you perform physical work beyond your medical restrictions and worsen your condition, the original insurer will claim a new intervening injury occurred, while the new employer's insurer may claim it was a pre-existing condition, creating severe coverage disputes.

Q8: Can I do remote or work-from-home freelance work while on workers' comp?

Yes, provided the duties comply with your medical restrictions. However, all self-employment, independent contracting, and 1099 freelance earnings must be fully reported to the claims administrator, as net earnings will reduce your disability payment calculations.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

You can legally get a new job while on workers' comp, but doing so requires absolute honesty, meticulous income reporting, and strict adherence to your doctor's physical restrictions. While starting a new position can rejuvenate your career and restore financial independence, it permanently alters your wage replacement payments and can drastically diminish pending settlement valuations. By reporting new earnings promptly, ensuring your new job matches your medical limitations, and consulting an experienced workers' compensation attorney before signing employment contracts, you can successfully transition into a new career without jeopardizing your hard-won benefits.
