How Do You Transfer Property to a Family Member?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how do you transfer property to a family member is a vital estate planning, financial, and tax consideration for parents, grandparents, and siblings wishing to convey real estate. While gifting or conveying a family home, vacation cabin, or parcel of land seems straightforward, transferring real estate involves complex legal deed mechanics, potential federal gift tax liabilities, future capital gains tax consequences, and dangerous Medicaid look-back implications. Choosing the appropriate transfer method protects family wealth and ensures unclouded legal title.

Deed Selection: Quitclaim Deeds, Warranty Deeds, and Lady Bird Deeds

The legal mechanism for conveying real property between family members is executed through a formal written deed filed with the county recorder of deeds. The most common instrument used between relatives is a Quitclaim Deed. A quitclaim deed transfers whatever legal interest the grantor currently holds in the property to the grantee without making any covenants or warranties regarding clear title. While simple and inexpensive, quitclaim deeds provide zero protection if undisclosed liens or boundary defects exist.

For superior title protection, families often use a Special or General Warranty Deed, which legally guarantees that the title is free from encumbrances. In states like Florida, Texas, Michigan, and North Carolina, families frequently utilize an Enhanced Life Estate Deed (commonly known as a Lady Bird Deed). A Lady Bird deed allows the owner to retain total control, rental income, and power to sell or mortgage the property during their lifetime, while automatically conveying remainder ownership to adult children upon death without probate court involvement.

Tax Ramifications: Stepped-Up Basis vs. Carryover Basis

The single largest financial trap in family property transfers is the capital gains tax basis. If you gift a home to an adult child while you are still alive using a standard quitclaim deed, the child receives a 'Carryover Basis'—meaning their tax basis is equal to what you originally paid for the property decades ago. When the child eventually sells the home, they face massive federal and state capital gains taxes on all the appreciation that accumulated during your ownership.

Conversely, if the child inherits the property upon your death (through a will, living trust, or Transfer on Death deed), they receive a 'Stepped-Up Basis.' The property's tax basis automatically resets to its fair market value on your exact date of death. If the child sells the property shortly after inheritance, their taxable capital gain is essentially zero, saving tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars in taxes.

The Medicaid 5-Year Look-Back Period and Due-on-Sale Clauses

Another critical hazard is Medicaid long-term care eligibility. Under federal law, state Medicaid agencies enforce a strict 60-month (5-year) Look-Back Period on all uncompensated asset transfers. If a senior gifts their home to an adult child for zero or nominal consideration ($1.00) and requires nursing home care within five years, Medicaid will impose a lengthy penalty period of ineligibility, forcing the family to pay catastrophic private nursing home rates out of pocket.

Fortunately, regarding existing mortgages, the federal Garn-St. Germain Depository Institutions Act of 1982 explicitly protects intrafamily transfers. Mortgage contracts standardly contain 'Due-on-Sale' acceleration clauses; however, Garn-St. Germain legally prohibits lenders from calling a loan due when real property is transferred to a spouse, children, or a family living trust, allowing the relative to continue making regular monthly mortgage payments.

How to Legally Transfer Real Estate to a Family Member

Follow these five strategic steps to prepare, execute, and record an intrafamily property transfer.

  1. Consult an Estate Planning Attorney and CPA

    Evaluate capital gains basis, gift tax return rules, and Medicaid implications before executing any deed.

  2. Select the Optimal Deed Instrument

    Choose between a Lady Bird Deed, Transfer on Death Deed, Living Trust transfer, or Quitclaim Deed.

  3. Draft the Deed with Legal Property Description

    Ensure the document contains the exact metes-and-bounds legal description and parcel ID from prior deeds.

  4. Sign Before a Notary Public

    Execute the deed before a certified notary public in accordance with state statutory acknowledgment laws.

  5. Record the Deed with the County Clerk / Recorder

    File the original deed at your local county land records office, paying recording fees and transfer tax affidavits.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I transfer my house to my child for $1 dollar?

You can legally do so on a quitclaim deed, but the IRS treats it as a gift of fair market value, triggering carryover basis and Medicaid lookback penalties.

Q2: What is a stepped-up basis in real estate?

Stepped-up basis resets the property's tax value to its fair market value on the owner's date of death, eliminating capital gains taxes on prior appreciation.

Q3: Does transferring property to a child trigger gift tax?

If the property value exceeds the annual exclusion ($18,000+), you must file IRS Form 709, but no actual tax is owed unless you exceed your lifetime exemption ($13M+).

Q4: Will my mortgage company demand immediate payment if I transfer the deed?

Under the federal Garn-St. Germain Act, lenders cannot invoke due-on-sale clauses for transfers to spouses, children, or family trusts.

Q5: What is the Medicaid 5-year look-back rule?

Medicaid audits all asset gifts made within 60 months of applying for nursing home benefits; gifting a home triggers a disqualification penalty.

Q6: What is a Lady Bird deed?

An enhanced life estate deed allowing the owner to retain total control and sell or mortgage property, while passing it to heirs upon death outside probate.

Q7: Does a quitclaim deed clear liens and back taxes?

No, quitclaim deeds transfer property subject to all existing mortgages, tax liens, and municipal judgments; it does not erase debt.

Q8: What is a Transfer on Death (TOD) deed?

A deed permitted in roughly 30 states that names a beneficiary who automatically inherits the property upon death without probate court.
