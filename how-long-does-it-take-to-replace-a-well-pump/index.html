How Long Does It Take to Replace a Well Pump?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how long does it take to replace a well pump depends on the well's depth, the pump technology installed, and accessibility to the wellhead. For a professional water well contractor equipped with a motorized pump-pulling rig, replacing a standard residential submersible well pump (200 to 400 feet deep) typically takes between 2 to 4 hours. However, in cases involving extreme depths (over 600 feet), manual pipe pulling, corroded galvanized drop pipe, or faulty electrical control boxes, the replacement can take 4 to 8 hours.

Well Pump Types: Submersible Deep Well vs. Above-Ground Jet Pumps

The primary determinant of replacement speed is the type of pump system installed. Above-ground jet pumps are used in shallow wells (less than 25 to 50 feet deep) and reside inside a basement, utility closet, or well shed. Replacing an above-ground jet pump is relatively fast, taking a licensed plumber 1.5 to 3 hours, as there is no heavy machinery required to pull hundreds of pounds of water-filled drop pipe from the earth.

The vast majority of modern private wells utilize deep submersible pumps suspended hundreds of feet below the surface. Submersible pumps are submerged inside the steel or PVC well casing at the bottom of the aquifer. Replacing a submersible unit requires unbolting the sanitary well cap, disconnecting the pitless adapter located below the frost line, and hoisting the pump, safety rope, electrical submersible wire, and hundreds of feet of water-laden pipe out of the casing.

Step-by-Step Breakdown: Pulling, Wiring, Setting, and Sanitizing

A professional well service crew executes a streamlined, chronological sequence of operations. The first 30 minutes involve troubleshooting: testing the pressure switch contacts, measuring motor winding resistance (ohms) with an ohmmeter, checking capacitor capacitance in the control box, and testing the pressure tank bladder to ensure the pump itself is truly dead. Once confirmed, electrical power is locked out, the well cap is opened, and a T-bar wrench engages the pitless adapter.

The pulling phase takes 45 to 90 minutes. A motorized wheel puller or crane boom hoists the drop pipe while the technician inspects the submersible wiring every 10 feet for chafing. Once the failed pump reaches the surface, a brand-new pump is assembled with a heavy-duty brass check valve, torque arrestor, and watertight heat-shrink electrical splice kits. Lowering the new assembly takes 30 to 45 minutes, followed by re-seating the pitless adapter, connecting the electrical box, and testing operating pressure and amp draw.

Common Complications That Can Extend the Job

While most well pump replacements conclude in under 4 hours, unforeseen mechanical complications can prolong the project. The most severe complication is a stuck pitless adapter or a collapsed casing that pinches the pump during the pull. In older wells with rigid galvanized steel pipes, rusted threads can seize or snap, requiring specialized well fishing tools to retrieve the pump assembly from the bottom of the borehole.

Furthermore, if the failed pump caused the water pressure tank bladder to waterlog or rupture, the contractor must also replace the pressure tank, pressure relief valve, and 40/60 pressure switch, adding another 1 to 2 hours of indoor plumbing labor. Finally, a shock chlorination procedure is performed to eliminate bacteria introduced during the pull, requiring homeowners to flush their plumbing lines.

How to Manage a Well Pump Replacement

Follow these five tactical steps when your well pump fails to restore running water safely.

  1. Verify Breakers and Pressure Switch Before Calling

    Check your main electrical panel for tripped double-pole 240V breakers and tap the pressure switch contacts.

  2. Locate and Clear Access to the Wellhead Casing

    Ensure the outdoor well cap is visible, marked, and clear of overgrown bushes, landscaping mulch, or snow.

  3. Have Your Well Completion Report Accessible

    Provide your well contractor with the well log showing total depth, casing diameter, static water level, and GPM yield.

  4. Verify New Check Valve and Heat-Shrink Splices

    Ensure the installer uses dual check valves and waterproof epoxy or heat-shrink electrical splice connections.

  5. Perform Shock Chlorination and Flush Outdoor Spigots

    Chlorinate the well after installation and run an outdoor garden hose until water flows crystal clear before using indoor fixtures.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can a homeowner replace a submersible well pump alone?

It is not recommended; pulling 300+ feet of water-filled pipe and wiring weighs hundreds of pounds and risks dropping the pipe.

Q2: How long can a house go without water during pump replacement?

Water is typically restored within 3 to 5 hours from the time the well contractor begins work on-site.

Q3: How long does a submersible well pump last?

A high-quality submersible pump typically lasts 10 to 15 years, depending on water sediment levels and cycle frequency.

Q4: How do you know if the pump or the pressure tank is bad?

Rapid clicking of the pressure switch or water hammer indicates a failed tank bladder, while zero water and normal voltage indicates pump failure.

Q5: What is shock chlorination after well pump replacement?

It is the process of pouring unscented chlorine bleach down the well to sanitize bacteria introduced during the outdoor repair.

Q6: How much does it cost to replace a well pump?

Professional replacement typically costs between $1,500 and $3,500 depending on depth, horsepower, and piping type.

Q7: Why does the water smell like chlorine after pump replacement?

The contractor disinfected the well system; run an outdoor hose for 30 to 60 minutes to flush the chlorine out.

Q8: Can a well pump be replaced in freezing winter temperatures?

Yes, well contractors operate year-round; the pitless adapter connects below the frost line (typically 4 to 6 feet underground).
