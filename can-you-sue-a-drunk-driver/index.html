Can You Sue a Drunk Driver?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, you can sue a drunk driver in civil court for personal injuries and property damage. Unlike ordinary car accidents involving simple negligence, civil lawsuits against impaired drivers allow victims to pursue substantial Punitive Damages in addition to compensatory damages. Furthermore, civil judgments arising from intoxicated driving cannot be discharged in bankruptcy.

The Twin Tracks of Justice: Criminal Prosecution vs Civil Lawsuit

Surviving a violent motor vehicle collision caused by an intoxicated driver is an agonizing, traumatic experience. When a driver chooses to operate a multi-ton motor vehicle while severely impaired by intoxicating substances, their reckless behavior shatters lives, causing catastrophic spinal trauma, traumatic brain injuries, and wrongful death.

In American jurisprudence, legal accountability for an impaired driving crash operates along two distinct, parallel tracks: the Criminal Justice System and the Civil Tort System. In the criminal justice system, state prosecutors prosecute the impaired driver for state crimes (such as DUI, DWI, intoxication assault, or vehicular manslaughter). The criminal court's primary objective is societal punishment—imposing county jail or state prison sentences, driver license revocations, and criminal fines paid to the state treasury.

However, the criminal justice process rarely compensates injured victims for massive lifetime financial damages. That critical function is fulfilled exclusively by the Civil Personal Injury Lawsuit. As an injured victim, you possess the constitutional right to retain a personal injury trial attorney and file an independent civil lawsuit directly against the impaired driver to recover financial compensation for every dimension of physical, economic, and emotional harm. The table below delineates the profound differences between criminal and civil proceedings.

Legal Proceeding Dimension Criminal Justice Prosecution Civil Personal Injury Lawsuit
Party Initiating Action State Government (District / State Attorney) The Injured Victim (Represented by private lawyer)
Burden of Proof Required Beyond a Reasonable Doubt (99% certainty) Preponderance of the Evidence (More likely than not / 51%)
Primary Legal Objective Incarceration, fines, license loss, deterrence Maximum financial compensation paid to the victim
Victim Direct Control Zero control: prosecutor decides plea deals Complete control: victim decides whether to settle or try
Financial Award Recipient Fines paid to state treasury; minor restitution 100% of settlement or verdict proceeds paid to victim

The Power of Punitive Damages in Impaired Driving Lawsuits

The defining legal weapon in an impaired driving lawsuit—distinguishing it from an ordinary rear-end collision or distracted driving case—is the availability of Punitive Damages (also referred to as Exemplary Damages).

In standard auto accident litigation, an injured plaintiff can recover only Compensatory Damages. Compensatory damages are strictly designed to 'make the victim whole' by reimbursing out-of-pocket medical bills, future surgical care, lost wages, and non-economic physical pain and suffering. However, civil tort law universally recognizes that choosing to drive while intoxicated transcends ordinary carelessness; it constitutes Gross Negligence, Willful and Wanton Misconduct, and a Conscious Disregard for human life.

Because impaired driving is classified as an intentional reckless act, judges and juries are legally permitted to award substantial punitive damages. Punitive damages are not tied to the cost of your hospital bills; they are designed specifically to punish the wrongdoer financially and make an unmistakable public example of them. In many jurisdictions, statutory caps that limit pain-and-suffering awards in standard accidents are completely waived when the defendant was driving while intoxicated. The table below outlines the types of damages recoverable in a civil drunk driving lawsuit.

Damage Classification Specific Losses Covered Insurance Policy Availability Legal Objective
Economic Compensatory Emergency ICU care, surgeries, physical therapy, lost pay Covered under standard bodily injury auto policy Repays actual out-of-pocket financial losses
Non-Economic Compensatory Physical pain, emotional PTSD, permanent disfigurement Covered under standard bodily injury auto policy Compensates human suffering and loss of life quality
Punitive / Exemplary Damages Additional monetary penalty assessed by jury Varies by state (paid by driver personal assets / insurer) Punishes outrageous conduct and deters society
Wrongful Death Damages Funeral costs, loss of spousal consortium, lost guidance Covered under auto liability & commercial umbrella Compensates surviving family members of fatal crash

The Bankruptcy Shield Fails: 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(9)

A frequent defense tactic attempted by reckless defendants facing massive civil lawsuits is threatening to declare Chapter 7 or Chapter 13 bankruptcy to wipe out their financial liabilities and avoid paying the victim.

In standard personal injury claims involving simple driving errors, a civil judgment can occasionally be discharged in bankruptcy court. However, in personal injury claims arising from intoxicated driving, federal bankruptcy law explicitly protects crash victims. Under Title 11 of the United States Code (specifically 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(9)), the Federal Bankruptcy Code establishes an absolute, ironclad statutory exception to dischargeability.

The statute dictates that a bankruptcy court cannot discharge any debt 'for death or personal injury caused by the debtor's operation of a motor vehicle, vessel, or aircraft if such operation was unlawful because the debtor was intoxicated from using intoxicating substances.' This means that even if the at-fault driver declares total bankruptcy, your civil judgment survives completely intact. Your legal team can pursue the driver's personal real estate equity, bank accounts, investment portfolios, and garnish their employment paychecks for decades until the judgment is paid in full.

How to Build a Civil Lawsuit Against an Impaired Driver

Step-by-step litigation roadmap to establish liability and recover punitive damages.

  1. Confirm Responding Police Preserve Chemical Toxicology Evidence

    Ensure the police crash report documents field sobriety tests, preliminary breath tests, or hospital forensic blood draw warrants.

  2. Monitor the Parallel Criminal Court Proceedings

    Track the criminal DUI prosecution; a guilty plea or criminal conviction establishes civil liability automatically under Negligence Per Se.

  3. Audit All Primary, Secondary, and Umbrella Insurance Policies

    Have your attorney demand policy disclosure from the driver's insurer, your own Underinsured Motorist (UIM) carrier, and potential umbrella policies.

  4. File a Formal Civil Lawsuit Asserting Punitive Damages

    File a civil complaint pleading gross negligence and willful misconduct to seek punitive damages that cannot be discharged in bankruptcy.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can I sue a drunk driver if they were not convicted in criminal court?

Yes. The civil burden of proof (preponderance of evidence / 51%) is much lower than the criminal burden (beyond reasonable doubt / 99%).

Q2: What are punitive damages in a drunk driving accident lawsuit?

Punitive damages are additional financial penalties awarded by a jury to punish the impaired driver for reckless, willful disregard of human life.

Q3: Can a drunk driver file bankruptcy to avoid paying a settlement?

No. Under federal bankruptcy law (11 U.S.C. 523(a)(9)), civil judgments for personal injuries caused by intoxicated driving cannot be discharged.

Q4: Does auto insurance pay if the driver was intoxicated?

Yes. An auto insurance company cannot deny coverage to innocent third-party victims simply because their insured driver committed a crime.

Q5: Can I sue the establishment that served the drunk driver?

Yes. Under state Dram Shop acts, commercial establishments can be sued if they knowingly served alcohol to a visibly intoxicated patron or minor.

Q6: How much is an average drunk driving accident settlement?

Settlements frequently range from $100,000 for moderate injuries to several million dollars for severe spinal trauma, brain injuries, or fatal crashes.

Q7: Can I garnish a drunk driver's wages to pay my judgment?

Yes. If insurance limits are insufficient, a civil judgment allows your attorney to garnish the driver's wages and seize non-exempt personal assets.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

You can sue an impaired driver in civil court to secure full justice and financial compensation beyond what the criminal justice system provides. Intoxicated driving cases unlock powerful punitive damage claims that bypass standard damage caps and cannot be erased through bankruptcy filings. Ensure responding police preserve breathalyzer and blood toxicology lab results, seek emergency medical evaluation immediately, and retain an experienced personal injury trial attorney to hold the impaired driver fully accountable.
