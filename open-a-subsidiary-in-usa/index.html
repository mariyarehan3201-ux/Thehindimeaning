Open a Subsidiary in USA

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

Expanding an international enterprise into the United States represents one of the most lucrative strategic milestones for growing global companies. As the world's largest consumer and commercial market, the United States offers foreign enterprises unmatched access to capital, innovation ecosystems, skilled talent, and global trade networks. However, establishing a legal operating presence requires establishing a distinct corporate structure capable of shielding the foreign parent corporation from domestic liability and cross-border tax exposure. Learning how to open a subsidiary in the USA involves critical legal and operational decisions—including selecting between a C-Corporation and a Limited Liability Company (LLC), choosing the optimal state of incorporation, obtaining an Employer Identification Number (EIN) without a US Social Security Number, and maintaining strict corporate governance.

Corporate Entity Selection: C-Corporation vs. Limited Liability Company (LLC)

When an international enterprise establishes a physical or commercial foothold in the United States, selecting the appropriate legal entity structure is the foundational corporate decision. The legal vehicle chosen dictates how profits are taxed, how capital is raised, how liability is partitioned, and how corporate governance is executed across international boundaries. Under US corporate law, the two primary business entities available to foreign corporations seeking to form a wholly owned subsidiary are the C-Corporation (C-Corp) and the Limited Liability Company (LLC). Notably, S-Corporations are statutorily unavailable to foreign entities, as federal law strictly limits S-Corp shareholders to individual US citizens and permanent residents.

For the vast majority of international businesses, forming a US C-Corporation as a subsidiary is the gold standard and recommended structure. A C-Corporation is a separate and distinct taxable entity under the Internal Revenue Code (IRC). It files its own corporate income tax returns (IRS Form 1120) and pays federal corporate taxes at the flat 21 percent rate, along with applicable state corporate taxes. Crucially, a C-Corporation establishes an impermeable liability and tax shield around the foreign parent company. Because the subsidiary pays its own income taxes at the entity level, the foreign parent corporation is not required to register with the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) or disclose its worldwide balance sheets, thereby avoiding onerous US branch profits taxes.

Conversely, selecting a Limited Liability Company (LLC) creates substantial tax complexity for foreign parent corporations. By default, a single-member LLC owned by a corporate entity is classified by the IRS as a disregarded entity, while a multi-member LLC is treated as a pass-through partnership. Under pass-through taxation, all US source income flows directly through to the foreign owners. This forces the foreign parent company into filing non-resident US corporate tax returns, subjecting foreign executives to domestic tax scrutiny and triggering a potential 30 percent branch profits tax on deemed dividend distributions. While an LLC can elect to be taxed as a corporation by filing IRS Form 8832, founders navigating corporate restructuring often analyze procedures for converting an entity into an LLC to comprehend state registration filings. Executives evaluating state formation databases must also understand managerial classifications, such as what does AMBR mean in an LLC when assigning officer authority. State Selection, Registered Agents, and Intercompany Governance

In the United States, there is no single federal corporate registration office; corporate entities are chartered at the state level under the sovereign laws of one of the fifty individual states or the District of Columbia. Consequently, international executives must choose where to formally charter their subsidiary. Delaware has long maintained its reputation as the preeminent state of incorporation for both Fortune 500 corporations and foreign subsidiaries. Delaware's corporate appeal is rooted in its highly specialized Court of Chancery, a dedicated business court where corporate disputes are adjudicated by expert judges without juries, creating a century of stable, predictable corporate case law. Furthermore, Delaware does not levy corporate income tax on companies that do not physically conduct business within the state.

However, incorporating in Delaware or business-friendly states like Wyoming and Nevada does not exempt an expanding foreign enterprise from registering in the states where it physically conducts commerce. Under US corporate law, if your Delaware subsidiary leases physical office space, warehouses inventory, employs local workers, or meets economic sales thresholds in California, New York, or Texas, it must file for Foreign Qualification in those operational states. Foreign qualification involves registering as an out-of-state entity, paying local franchise fees, and complying with state corporate income and employment tax withholding obligations. Therefore, if a foreign company plans to concentrate its physical operations entirely in one state, incorporating directly in that home state often avoids redundant annual filing fees.

Every US state mandates that corporations maintain a designated Registered Agent with a physical street address in the formation jurisdiction to accept official legal service of process and statutory state notices during normal business hours. Beyond basic state compliance, establishing robust intercompany governance is paramount. Multinational enterprises must draft comprehensive intercompany service agreements, intellectual property licensing frameworks, and transfer pricing documentation compliant with IRC Section 482 and OECD guidelines. Transactions between parent and subsidiary must adhere to arm's length pricing standards to prevent tax penalties. Furthermore, when corporate leadership rotates, enterprises must follow strict internal governance rules, similar to knowing how to remove a member from an LLC or amending corporate officer registers. Federal Tax Registration (EIN), Banking, and Compliance Protocols

Once state incorporation articles are officially filed and approved by the Secretary of State, the subsidiary must secure its federal taxpayer identity from the Internal Revenue Service. The subsidiary must obtain an Employer Identification Number (EIN) by submitting IRS Form SS-4. For domestic entrepreneurs with a US Social Security Number (SSN) or Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN), this process takes minutes online. However, for foreign founders and international corporate parents lacking US tax credentials, the online portal cannot be used. The application must be prepared with the designated foreign responsible party and submitted to the IRS via specialized international facsimile or mail, a process that typically requires three to five weeks to secure the official EIN confirmation letter (Notice CP 575).

Opening a commercial bank account represents one of the most demanding operational hurdles for foreign-owned US subsidiaries. Following federal anti-money laundering legislation and the enactment of FinCEN regulations under the Corporate Transparency Act (CTA), US financial institutions enforce rigorous Know Your Customer (KYC) compliance. International subsidiaries must supply certified corporate formation documents, corporate bylaws, proof of EIN, certified organizational charts detailing beneficial ownership exceeding 25 percent, and valid government passports for all directors. While global institutions like JPMorgan Chase or Bank of America often require an authorized corporate officer to visit a physical US branch in person, modern digital business platforms such as Mercury, Brex, or international partner banks provide streamlined remote onboarding for qualifying international companies.

Ongoing compliance requires continuous vigilance across federal, state, and local jurisdictions. At the federal level, foreign-owned US corporations must file annual corporate tax returns (Form 1120) and accompany them with Form 5472, an information return detailing all reportable transactions between the US subsidiary and its foreign parent or related parties. Failing to file Form 5472 carries severe minimum statutory penalties starting at twenty-five thousand dollars per violation. Furthermore, companies must register for state sales tax permits under economic nexus standards established post-Wayfair, fulfill local business licensing requirements, and file required federal Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reports with FinCEN.

How to Open a US Subsidiary for a Foreign Company

Follow this comprehensive legal and administrative procedure to incorporate, capitalize, and launch a compliant US subsidiary company for an international enterprise.

  1. Select Corporate Structure and Formation Jurisdiction

    Choose between a C-Corporation and an LLC (with C-Corp strongly recommended for foreign parents) and select your formation state, such as Delaware for corporate law or your operational hub.

  2. Appoint a Registered Agent and File Formation Articles

    Retain a commercial registered agent in your chosen state and file the Certificate of Incorporation or Articles of Organization with the Secretary of State.

  3. Obtain an Employer Identification Number (EIN) via IRS Form SS-4

    Complete IRS Form SS-4 designating an international executive as the responsible party and transmit the filing via fax or mail to the IRS international processing center.

  4. Draft Corporate Bylaws and Intercompany Service Agreements

    Formulate comprehensive corporate bylaws, issue stock certificates to the foreign parent entity, and execute arm's-length intercompany transfer pricing and licensing agreements.

  5. Open a US Commercial Bank Account and File Federal BOI Reports

    Submit formation credentials and beneficial ownership records to establish a US business bank account, and complete your mandatory Beneficial Ownership Information filing with FinCEN.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Does a foreign company need a US resident director to form a subsidiary?

No. Under US corporate law in most states, including Delaware and Wyoming, corporate officers, directors, and shareholders do not need to be US citizens or permanent residents.

Q2: What is the primary difference between a US branch office and a US subsidiary?

A US branch office is merely an extension of the foreign parent company, exposing the foreign parent directly to US legal liabilities and taxation. A subsidiary is an independent legal corporation that shields the parent from domestic liability.

Q3: How can an international founder obtain an EIN without an SSN or ITIN?

Foreign founders obtain an Employer Identification Number by preparing IRS Form SS-4, writing Foreign in the SSN field for the responsible party, and submitting the document to the IRS via specialized international facsimile or registered mail.

Q4: Why is Delaware the most popular state for foreign corporate subsidiaries?

Delaware offers the specialized Court of Chancery with dedicated corporate judges, over a century of predictable business jurisprudence, strong privacy protections, and zero state corporate tax on income generated outside the state.

Q5: What is the FinCEN Beneficial Ownership Information (BOI) reporting requirement?

Under the Corporate Transparency Act, newly created US subsidiaries must file an electronic BOI report with FinCEN within mandated deadlines, disclosing identifying information and passports for all beneficial owners with 25 percent or more control.

Q6: How does transfer pricing affect intercompany transactions between parent and subsidiary?

Under IRS Section 482 and OECD guidelines, transactions between related foreign and US entities must be conducted at arm's-length market rates to prevent improper profit shifting, supported by annual transfer pricing documentation.

Q7: Can a US subsidiary sponsor work visas for foreign executives and managers?

Yes. A bona fide US subsidiary with physical operations and commercial activity can petition to sponsor foreign executives and specialized employees through the L-1 intra-company transferee visa program.

Q8: What is the penalty for failing to file IRS Form 5472 for a foreign-owned US corporation?

Failing to file Form 5472 with the annual corporate tax return to report intercompany transactions carries an automatic minimum statutory penalty of $25,000 per violation, with additional penalties for continued failure.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Opening a subsidiary in the USA unlocks immense commercial opportunities for international companies seeking to scale operations, attract institutional investment, and access the world's most dynamic consumer market. Establishing a C-Corporation subsidiary provides an effective liability shield that protects the international parent company while creating a clean, compliant tax vehicle. By selecting the optimal state of incorporation, appointing a reliable registered agent, managing the EIN acquisition process, and adhering to federal transfer pricing and reporting mandates, foreign enterprises can build an enduring, profitable American presence.
