LHS Cable Full Form: Linear Heat Sensor Guide

Full Form
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 17, 2026

The acronym LHS Cable stands for Linear Heat Sensor Cable (frequently called Linear Heat Detection or LHD Cable). In fire safety engineering, industrial building management, and life safety infrastructure, an LHS cable is a specialized continuous thermal detection line designed to detect excessive heat, thermal spikes, or open flames anywhere along its entire physical installation length. Widely deployed across vehicular road tunnels, heavy industrial cable trays, chemical conveyor belts, and power transformer yards, LHS cables provide early fire detection in challenging environments where optical smoke detectors fail.

Early Fire Detection and the Working Principles of Linear Heat Sensor Cables

Detecting a fire outbreak at its earliest inception is the single most critical factor in preventing catastrophic industrial disasters. While standard commercial buildings rely on ceiling-mounted optical smoke detectors or ionization sensors, many industrial facilities present environments where traditional spot detectors are unusable.

In dusty coal conveyors, humid subway tunnels, high-voltage switchgear galleries, and outdoor oil storage tanks, airborne particulate dust and wind velocities blind standard sensors. Linear Heat Sensor (LHS) Cables provide a continuous, rugged thermal detection line engineered specifically for these demanding industrial conditions.

Rather than monitoring a single point, an LHS cable acts as an uninterrupted series of thousands of thermal sensors. A fire or localized overheating event occurring anywhere along a kilometer-long cable run triggers an instantaneous alarm at the central fire panel.

Cable Tray, Tunnel, and Conveyor Belt Installation Topologies

The mechanics of a Digital Linear Heat Sensor cable are simple and reliable. Two spring-tensioned, copper-coated steel conductor wires are each extruded with a specially formulated heat-sensitive polymer insulation. The conductors are twisted together tightly to maintain mechanical spring tension and wrapped in a protective outer jacket.

Under normal room conditions, the polymer insulation isolates the two wires. However, when ambient heat from an electrical cable fault or fire elevates the temperature to the cable calibrated threshold (e.g., 68°C), the polymer melts instantly. The spring tension forces the conductors together, creating a short circuit that triggers the alarm.

How to Install and Wire a Digital Linear Heat Sensor (LHS) Cable

  1. Select Pre-Calibrated Temperature Rating

    Choose an LHS cable with an activation rating suited to ambient conditions (e.g., 68°C for indoor cable trays, 105°C for boiler rooms).

  2. Route Cable in a Serpentine Pattern

    Lay the LHS cable along the heat source or weave in a sinusoidal pattern over industrial cable trays to guarantee thermal contact.

  3. Secure with Specialized Cable Clips

    Fasten the sensor line every 1 to 1.5 meters using non-conductive, fire-rated mounting clips without crushing the outer protective jacket.

  4. Wire to End-of-Line (EOL) Resistor Unit

    Terminate the furthest cable end with a factory-specified End-of-Line monitoring module to detect wire open-circuit breaks.

  5. Connect Interface Module to Fire Alarm Panel

    Wire the cable origin into an addressable LHS Interface Controller, enabling real-time distance-to-fault meter readings on the master panel.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: What is the full form of LHS Cable in fire safety?

LHS Cable stands for Linear Heat Sensor Cable.

Q2: How does a digital LHS cable detect fire?

It consists of two spring-tensioned steel conductors coated with heat-sensitive polymer; when heat reaches the threshold, the coating melts, creating an electrical short circuit.

Q3: What is the difference between Digital and Analog LHS cables?

Digital LHS triggers an alarm at a fixed setpoint temperature and requires splicing after a fire, while Analog LHS measures continuous resistance changes and is resettable.

Q4: What temperature ratings are available for LHS cables?

Standard activation temperatures include 68°C (155°F), 88°C (190°F), 105°C (221°F), and high-temp variants up to 180°C (356°F).

Q5: Can an LHS cable pinpoint the exact location of a fire?

Yes, modern distance-locating interface modules calculate line resistance changes to report the exact distance in meters from the control panel to the hot spot.

Q6: Why are optical smoke detectors replaced by LHS in tunnels?

Tunnels suffer from vehicle exhaust, dust, and wind currents that blind optical smoke detectors and generate constant false alarms; LHS cables are unaffected.

Q7: What outer jackets protect LHS cables in harsh environments?

Options include chemical-resistant fluoropolymer (polypropylene), nylon jackets for UV outdoor resilience, and stainless steel braided armor.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Linear Heat Sensor Cables (LHS Cable) provide an essential line of defense in modern fire safety engineering. By functioning as continuous thermal detection lines capable of operating amidst dust, moisture, and extreme outdoor weather, LHS cables protect tunnels, conveyor belts, and high-voltage power galleries, ensuring swift fire containment and human safety.
