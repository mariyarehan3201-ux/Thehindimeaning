Is Kansas a No Fault State for Car Accidents?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Yes, Kansas is a no-fault state for car accidents. Under the Kansas Automobile Injury Reparations Act (K.S.A. § 40-3101 et seq.), every motor vehicle insurance policy must include mandatory Personal Injury Protection (PIP) benefits to pay medical expenses and lost wages regardless of fault, while restricting lawsuits against at-fault drivers unless a statutory tort threshold is met.

The Kansas No-Fault Framework: Mandatory PIP Benefits

When a motor vehicle collision occurs on Kansas roadways—whether navigating commuter traffic on Interstate 35 in Johnson County or driving along rural highways in Sedgwick County—automobile accident claims are governed by the Kansas Automobile Injury Reparations Act. Unlike pure tort states where injured victims must immediately prove another driver was negligent before receiving a dime for medical treatment, Kansas utilizes a modified no-fault insurance system.

Under Kansas law (K.S.A. § 40-3107), every registered automobile owner must carry mandatory first-party Personal Injury Protection (PIP) insurance. When an accident occurs, your own automobile insurance company is legally required to pay for your emergency room care, hospital bills, rehabilitation therapy, and partial lost wages, regardless of whether you caused the collision, the other driver caused it, or adverse weather conditions played a role.

Kansas statutory PIP requirements are among the most structured in the country. State law establishes minimum mandatory coverage limits across several distinct benefit categories. These benefits protect individuals from immediate financial bankruptcy while recovering from crash-related injuries. The table below outlines mandatory statutory PIP benefit categories and statutory minimum limits under Kansas law.

Medical Expenses $4,500 per person Incurred within 24 months of accident Hospital, physician, diagnostic, surgical, dental care
Disability / Lost Income Up to $900 per month (or 85% wages) Payable up to 12 consecutive months Replaces lost income during medical convalescence
In-Home Essential Services Up to $25 per day Payable up to 365 days Hires household chores, cooking, cleaning assistance
Rehabilitation Expenses $4,500 per person Incurred within 24 months of accident Occupational therapy, physical retraining, speech therapy

Breaking Through the Kansas Tort Threshold: When Can You Sue?

While the Kansas no-fault system provides rapid, guaranteed payment of baseline medical expenses through PIP, it establishes a statutory barrier designed to prevent courts from being clogged with minor personal injury lawsuits. Under K.S.A. § 40-3117, an injured victim cannot sue the negligent at-fault driver for non-economic damages—such as physical pain, suffering, mental anguish, and loss of life enjoyment—unless their injuries satisfy the statutory Kansas Tort Threshold.

To cross this statutory threshold and file a third-party personal injury lawsuit, the injured person must satisfy at least one of two statutory criteria. The first is a monetary threshold: the reasonable and necessary medical expenses incurred by the injured person must equal or exceed $2,000. Because modern emergency room visits, CT scans, and ambulance transports routinely exceed $2,000, many serious car crash victims cross this monetary threshold almost immediately.

The second criterion is a qualitative injury threshold. If the injured victim's medical bills do not reach $2,000, they may still file a lawsuit against the at-fault driver if the collision resulted in any of the following qualifying conditions: permanent disfigurement, a fracture to a weight-bearing bone, a compound, comminuted, displaced, or compressed fracture, permanent loss of a bodily function, or death. The table below compares no-fault PIP claims against third-party liability lawsuits under Kansas statutes.

Who Pays the Claim? Your own auto insurance carrier At-fault driver's bodily injury liability insurer
Requirement to Prove Negligence? Zero proof needed (100% no-fault) Mandatory proof of at-fault driver's negligence
Pain & Suffering Recovery? Strictly excluded (economic benefits only) Full recovery for pain, suffering, emotional distress
Prerequisite Conditions Must be involved in motor vehicle accident Must cross $2,000 medical threshold or severe injury test

Modified Comparative Fault: The Kansas 50 Percent Rule

Once you satisfy the Kansas tort threshold and file a lawsuit against the at-fault driver, your financial recovery is evaluated under the Kansas modified comparative fault statute (K.S.A. § 60-258a). Under this statute, Kansas follows the strict 50 percent bar rule.

This means an injured plaintiff can recover damages from an at-fault driver only if the plaintiff's percentage of causal negligence is less than the defendant's negligence. If a jury determines that you were 49 percent at fault and the other driver was 51 percent at fault, you can recover 51 percent of your total awarded damages. However, if the jury finds that you were exactly 50 percent at fault or more, you are completely barred by law from recovering any monetary damages whatsoever from the defendant.

How to File a Car Accident Claim in Kansas

Step-by-step procedure to file no-fault PIP claims and pursue third-party bodily injury lawsuits under Kansas law.

  1. Notify Your Own Auto Insurer and Request PIP Application

    Contact your insurance company immediately after the collision to complete formal Kansas Personal Injury Protection (PIP) claim benefit forms.

  2. Submit Itemized Medical Invoices and Lost Wage Proof

    Provide your PIP adjuster with hospital treatment bills, physical therapy logs, and employer wage verification letters to receive prompt reimbursement.

  3. Track Medical Expenses to Cross the $2,000 Threshold

    Retain copies of all healthcare bills to prove your medical treatment costs have exceeded the $2,000 statutory Kansas tort threshold.

  4. File Third-Party Bodily Injury Claim Against At-Fault Driver

    Have a personal injury attorney submit a comprehensive settlement demand to the at-fault driver's liability carrier seeking damages for pain and suffering.

Frequently Asked Questions (7 Questions Answered)

Q1: Does Kansas no-fault insurance apply to vehicle damage?

No. Kansas no-fault insurance applies exclusively to bodily injury medical expenses and lost wages; vehicle property damage is paid by the at-fault driver's liability insurance.

Q2: What is the Kansas medical tort threshold?

Under K.S.A. § 40-3117, an injured person must incur at least $2,000 in reasonable medical expenses before they can sue the at-fault driver for pain and suffering.

Q3: Can I sue if my medical bills are under $2,000 in Kansas?

Yes, but only if your injury caused permanent disfigurement, a fracture to a weight-bearing bone, a compound/compressed fracture, permanent disability, or death.

Q4: What is the minimum PIP medical coverage required in Kansas?

Kansas law mandates a minimum of $4,500 in medical benefits and $4,500 in rehabilitation benefits per injured person.

Q5: What is the 50 percent comparative fault rule in Kansas?

Under K.S.A. § 60-258a, you cannot recover any damages from the other driver if you are found to be 50 percent or more at fault for the crash.

Q6: How long do I have to file a car accident lawsuit in Kansas?

The statute of limitations for personal injury lawsuits in Kansas is two years from the date of the collision under K.S.A. § 60-513.

Q7: Does my health insurance pay before Kansas PIP?

No. Under Kansas law, automobile PIP coverage is primary and must pay your initial accident medical bills before your private health insurance begins coverage.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Kansas is undeniably a no-fault automobile insurance state. Its statutory system guarantees rapid, mandatory first-party PIP coverage of at least $4,500 in medical bills and $900 per month in lost wages through your own auto insurer regardless of fault. However, if your medical bills surpass the accessible $2,000 tort threshold or involve serious fractures or disfigurement, you have the full legal right to sue the negligent driver for full pain, suffering, and excess economic damages under Kansas comparative fault laws.
