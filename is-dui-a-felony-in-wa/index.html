Is DUI a Felony in WA?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 30, 2026

In Washington State, driving under the influence is categorized by default as a gross misdemeanor, yet statutory provisions under the Revised Code of Washington specifically dictate when an impaired driving offense escalates to a serious class B felony. For decades, Washington prosecutors treated repeat impaired driving cases primarily within municipal and district courts, but legislative revisions to RCW 46.61.502 substantially lowered felony thresholds to penalize repeat offenders and severe vehicular trauma cases. Individuals facing impaired driving accusations must understand the strict criteria that transform a gross misdemeanor into a felony charge carrying mandatory sentences in state correctional facilities, lifelong stigma, and loss of civil liberties.

Washington RCW 46.61.502: Gross Misdemeanor vs Felony Triggers

Under Washington State law codified in Revised Code of Washington (RCW) 46.61.502 and RCW 46.61.504, driving or being in physical control of a motor vehicle while impaired by intoxicating substances or prescription compounds is generally classified as a gross misdemeanor. For a standard first, second, or third offense within a ten-year window, cases remain within district or municipal court jurisdiction. Gross misdemeanors carry statutory maximum penalties of up to three hundred sixty-four days in county jail and a discretionary fine of up to five thousand dollars, subject to mandatory minimum incarceration periods that increase with prior record history.

However, Washington statutes contain a strict felony enhancement trigger that elevates an impaired driving charge to a Class B felony prosecuted in county Superior Court. Under RCW 46.61.502(6), a person is guilty of a felony offense if either of two statutory thresholds is met: first, if the person has three or more prior qualifying offenses within ten years, meaning the current arrest represents their fourth offense; or second, if the accused person has ever been previously convicted of vehicular assault while impaired under RCW 46.61.522, or vehicular homicide while impaired under RCW 46.61.520. Learning about Washington DUI legal guidelines clarifies how prior out-of-state convictions are factored into this statutory enhancement.

The definition of a prior offense under RCW 46.61.5055 is extraordinarily broad. It encompasses not only previous convictions for driving under the influence, but also deferred prosecutions granted under RCW 10.05, convictions for negligent driving in the first degree amended from an original impaired driving charge, reckless driving plea reductions, and comparable convictions from other states or tribal jurisdictions. If an individual is arrested following a motor vehicle collision, reviewing how impaired driving after a crash is documented by law enforcement is essential for mounting an early defense. Sentencing Reform Act Guidelines and Prison Grid Scoring

Unlike misdemeanor cases where judges retain substantial discretion to suspend jail time in favor of community service or outpatient chemical treatment, felony sentencing in Washington Superior Courts is strictly governed by the Sentencing Reform Act (SRA). Under the SRA, a felony conviction for driving under the influence is categorized as a Seriousness Level IV criminal offense. The exact prison term imposed by the presiding Superior Court judge is determined by intersecting this seriousness level with the defendant's calculated offender score on the statutory sentencing grid.

Calculating the offender score in a felony impaired driving prosecution is complex. Each prior misdemeanor impaired driving offense within the ten-year lookback counts for one full point on the offender score, while prior felony impaired driving convictions or vehicular assault convictions carry even heavier point values. An individual convicted of a fourth offense who enters court with an offender score of three faces a standard sentencing range of thirteen to seventeen months of incarceration. Crucially, this sentence cannot be served in a local county jail; it must be served in a Washington Department of Corrections state prison facility alongside individuals convicted of serious felony offenses.

Furthermore, the Sentencing Reform Act mandates a minimum of twelve to eighteen months of community custody supervision upon release from state prison, administered by state probation officers. Convicted felons are subjected to random chemical screening, home visits, electronic location monitoring, mandatory chemical dependency treatment programs, and extensive financial penalties. Defense Strategies, Prior Conviction Audits, and Evidentiary Challenges

When charged with a felony impaired driving offense in Washington, securing seasoned criminal defense legal counsel is vital to challenging the legal validity of the state's prosecution. A primary defense strategy involves conducting an exhaustive forensic audit of the three alleged prior offenses. For a prior conviction to count toward a felony enhancement, the state must prove that the defendant had legal counsel or executed a constitutionally valid waiver of counsel when entering the prior plea, in accordance with the constitutional standards established in State v. Summers. If a prior municipal conviction or out-of-state deferred adjudication was constitutionally flawed, defense counsel can file a motion to strike that prior, dropping the charge back down to a gross misdemeanor.

Defense attorneys also closely examine the precise timeline of the ten-year statutory lookback window under RCW 46.61.5055. The ten-year calculation is measured strictly from the date of the incident of the prior offense to the date of the incident of the current arrest, rather than the respective conviction dates. If ten years and one day have elapsed between incident dates, the state cannot use that offense to support a felony enhancement under the fourth-offense rule.

Finally, attorneys challenge the underlying scientific evidence of the current arrest. This includes questioning the operational maintenance and diagnostic calibration records of the Draeger Alcotest 9510 evidentiary breath instrument, the legality of police traffic stops under Article I, Section 7 of the Washington State Constitution, and the chain of custody for state toxicological laboratory blood draws. Suppressing chemical test results or establishing lack of probable cause can dismantle the prosecution's case before trial.

How to Build a Defense Against a Felony DUI Charge in Washington

Review key steps undertaken by criminal defense counsel to challenge statutory felony enhancements in Washington State Superior Court.

  1. Audit the Ten-Year Statutory Lookback Period

    Defense counsel calculates the exact calendar dates between the incident dates of all alleged prior offenses to verify whether any fall outside the statutory ten-year window.

  2. Scrutinize the Constitutional Validity of Prior Convictions

    Attorneys obtain official court transcripts from past convictions to verify whether guilty pleas complied with constitutional counsel requirements under Washington precedent.

  3. Inspect Evidentiary Breath and Blood Forensic Records

    The defense subpoenas calibration logs, solution lot records, and maintenance files for the Draeger 9510 breath test machine or state toxicology blood laboratory.

  4. File Pretrial Suppression Motions on Constitutional Grounds

    Lawyers file motions to suppress evidence if the initial traffic stop violated Washington State Constitution Article I, Section 7 protections against warrantless searches.

  5. Negotiate Charge Reductions or Alternative Sentencing

    If priors are successfully struck, counsel negotiates to reduce the felony charge to a gross misdemeanor, avoiding mandatory state prison sentencing under the SRA.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: When does a DUI escalate to a felony in Washington State?

Under Washington RCW 46.61.502, driving under the influence escalates to a Class B felony if the driver has three or more prior qualifying offenses within ten years, or has any prior lifetime conviction for vehicular assault or vehicular homicide while impaired.

Q2: What is the 10-year lookback rule in Washington State?

The ten-year lookback rule calculates the timeframe between prior offenses and the current arrest based on the incident dates rather than the conviction dates. If a prior incident occurred more than ten years before the current incident date, it cannot count toward a fourth-offense felony enhancement.

Q3: Does a prior deferred prosecution count toward a felony DUI in Washington?

Yes. Under Washington RCW 46.61.5055, a completed or active deferred prosecution granted under chapter 10.05 RCW is statutorily defined as a prior offense and will be counted when determining whether a current arrest qualifies for felony enhancement.

Q4: Do out-of-state impaired driving convictions count as priors in Washington?

Yes, out-of-state convictions count if Washington prosecutors prove that the statutes of the other state are legally and factually comparable to Washington's impaired driving statutes. If the foreign statute criminalizes broader conduct, defense counsel can challenge its comparability.

Q5: Can a felony DUI sentence in Washington be served on electronic home monitoring?

No. Under the Washington Sentencing Reform Act, felony impaired driving convictions carry standard prison sentences that must be served within a Washington Department of Corrections state prison facility rather than on home detention or in a municipal jail.

Q6: Can a felony DUI conviction be vacated or expunged in Washington?

No. Under Washington State law, felony convictions for driving under the influence or physical control while impaired cannot be vacated, expunged, or removed from a person's criminal record, resulting in permanent felony status.

Q7: What is the standard prison term for a fourth DUI in Washington?

For an individual with an offender score of three, representing three prior offenses within ten years, the standard sentencing range under Seriousness Level IV is thirteen to seventeen months in state prison, followed by twelve to eighteen months of community custody supervision.

Q8: Does a felony conviction result in the loss of firearm rights in Washington?

Yes. Any felony conviction in Washington State results in the immediate, automatic forfeiture of your constitutional right to possess firearms. Restoring firearm rights requires completing all sentence terms and petitioning the Superior Court after a statutory crime-free waiting period.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

While the vast majority of impaired driving cases in Washington State remain categorized as gross misdemeanors handled in local courts, facing a felony enhancement under RCW 46.61.502 carries life-altering consequences. Accumulating three prior qualifying offenses within ten years, or carrying a single prior conviction for vehicular assault or homicide, moves your case to Superior Court with mandatory prison sentencing under the Sentencing Reform Act. Navigating these high-stakes charges demands aggressive legal representation capable of auditing prior conviction validity, challenging forensic lab testing, and fighting to preserve your freedom.
