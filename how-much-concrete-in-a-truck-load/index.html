How Much Concrete in a Truck Load?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how much concrete in a truck load requires examining the drum volume and legal highway weight limits of commercial ready-mix concrete trucks. A standard full-size commercial concrete transit mixer truck carries between 8 and 10 cubic yards of wet concrete per load (with 10 cubic yards being the maximum legal capacity on public roads). While the physical rotating steel drum has a gross internal volume of 13 to 14 cubic yards, trucks cannot be filled to the brim due to severe weight restrictions and the necessity of space for active drum mixing in transit.

Truck Capacities: Standard 10-Yard Mixers vs. Short-Load Trucks

In commercial construction, ready-mix concrete is sold and delivered by the cubic yard. The standard three-to-four-axle rear-discharge mixer truck—the familiar vehicle seen on residential foundation and driveway pours—features a rated mixing capacity of 9 to 10 cubic yards. Newer multi-axle front-discharge mixer trucks (popular in the Midwest and East Coast) carry an identical 10-to-11-cubic-yard payload, allowing the driver to chute concrete directly into footings from the cab.

For small residential homeowner jobs—such as pouring a single sidewalk slab, hot tub pad, or fence post footings—ordering a massive 10-yard truck is impractical and expensive due to 'short-load fees' charged by ready-mix plants for orders under 5 cubic yards. For these smaller projects, specialized 'short-load' or volumetric on-demand concrete trucks are used. These mobile batch plants carry dry sand, gravel, cement powder, and water in separate compartments, mixing anywhere from 1 to 6 cubic yards on-site on demand.

The Weight Math: Why Concrete Trucks 'Weigh Out' Rapidly

Homeowners are frequently surprised to learn why concrete mixer drums are only filled to roughly 70% of their physical volume. The constraint is extreme physical weight. Standard normal-weight concrete (composed of Portland cement, water, sand, and crushed stone aggregate) weighs approximately 4,050 pounds per cubic yard (roughly 150 pounds per cubic foot).

Multiplying 10 cubic yards by 4,050 pounds yields 40,500 pounds of wet concrete cargo alone. When added to the empty tare weight of a heavy commercial four-axle truck chassis and steel mixing barrel (approximately 26,000 to 30,000 pounds), the gross vehicle weight reaches 66,500 to 70,000 pounds (33 to 35 tons). Federal and state Department of Transportation (DOT) bridge laws strictly regulate gross vehicle weights and per-axle weight limits to prevent crushing asphalt roadways, mandating lift axles and restricting total load size.

Ordering Ready-Mix: Calculating Waste and Short-Load Fees

When ordering ready-mix concrete by the truckload, contractors universally apply the '10% waste buffer rule'. Excavated earth trenches, gravel sub-bases, and wooden formwork are never laser-flat; bowed 2x4 form boards, sub-base settling, and uneven ground dips easily consume an extra 5% to 10% of concrete volume. Running out of concrete with 10 feet of driveway remaining creates an emergency 'cold joint' that ruins structural integrity and looks terrible.

Furthermore, ready-mix dispatchers charge 'short-load fees' if you order less than a full truckload. A typical batch plant considers 6 or 7 cubic yards the minimum threshold for standard delivery; ordering only 2 or 3 yards on a 10-yard truck incurs an extra $100 to $250 short-load surcharge to cover driver wages and truck fuel. Dispatch also charges 'demurrage fees' (typically $1.50 to $2.50 per minute) if your crew takes longer than the standard 60-to-90-minute allowance to unload the truck.

How to Calculate and Order Concrete by the Truckload

Follow these five tactical steps to calculate exact yardage and order ready-mix delivery successfully.

  1. Calculate Cubic Feet of Concrete Needed

    Multiply Length (ft) x Width (ft) x Thickness (ft); for a 4-inch slab, convert thickness to 0.33 feet.

  2. Divide Total Cubic Feet by 27 to Find Cubic Yards

    Convert to yards: for example, 540 cubic feet divided by 27 equals exactly 20 cubic yards (2 full truck loads).

  3. Add a 10% Margin for Sub-Base and Form Deflection

    Multiply calculated yards by 1.10 (e.g., 20 yards x 1.10 = 22 cubic yards) to prevent short-pour cold joints.

  4. Specify Concrete PSI Strength and Slump to Dispatch

    Order 3,000 PSI for footings or 4,000 PSI with air-entrainment (for freeze-thaw climates) at a workable 4-to-5-inch slump.

  5. Prepare Unobstructed Chute Access and Staging

    Ensure the 70,000-pound truck has clear access without driving over delicate curbs, underground septic tanks, or lawns.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: How many cubic yards of concrete are in a full truck load?

A standard full commercial concrete truck carries between 8 and 10 cubic yards of wet concrete.

Q2: How much does a full concrete truck weigh?

A fully loaded 10-yard concrete mixer truck weighs between 66,000 and 70,000 pounds (approx. 33 to 35 tons).

Q3: How many square feet does a truckload of concrete cover?

A 10-cubic-yard truckload covers approximately 810 square feet at a standard 4-inch slab thickness.

Q4: How many bags of concrete equal one truck load?

A 10-yard truckload is equal to 450 sixty-pound bags or 338 eighty-pound bags of concrete.

Q5: What is a short load fee for concrete?

It is a fee (typically $100 to $250) charged by concrete plants when ordering less than 5 to 6 cubic yards on a truck.

Q6: How long do you have to unload a concrete truck?

Ready-mix suppliers allow 60 to 90 minutes of free unloading time before charging standby demurrage fees per minute.

Q7: How much does a full truckload of concrete cost?

At average ready-mix rates of $130 to $170 per cubic yard, a 10-yard truckload costs between $1,300 and $1,700 delivered.

Q8: Can a concrete truck drive on my residential driveway?

Exercise caution; a 70,000-pound loaded truck can crack residential 4-inch concrete driveways and collapse underground pipes.
