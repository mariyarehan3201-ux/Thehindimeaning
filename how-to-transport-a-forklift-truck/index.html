How to Transport a Forklift Truck?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 26, 2026

Transporting an industrial forklift truck over public highways requires meticulous planning, specialized heavy-haul trailer equipment, and strict adherence to Department of Transportation (DOT) cargo securement standards. Forklifts present unique transport hazards: despite their relatively compact physical footprint, they carry immense, concentrated deadweight due to solid cast-iron counterweights, solid rubber or pneumatic tires with minimal road grip, and tall vertical mast uprights that create dangerous overhead clearance risks. Transporting a forklift safely demands selecting the right commercial trailer, calculating working load limits, and executing a certified four-point chain tie-down.

Weight Dynamics: Understanding Counterweights and Center of Gravity

The primary engineering challenge in transporting forklifts is their radical weight distribution. Unlike standard motor vehicles where weight is distributed evenly across four wheels, a forklift's center of gravity is intentionally concentrated over the rear steering axle via a dense, multi-ton solid iron counterweight. This counterweight prevents the machine from tipping forward when carrying heavy palletized loads on its front hydraulic forks.

Consequently, an empty 5,000-pound capacity warehouse forklift often has a total unladen curb weight exceeding 9,000 to 10,000 pounds, with over 60% of that weight resting entirely on the rear steer axle. When loading a forklift onto a commercial trailer, positioning the machine backward (backing it onto the trailer) is frequently necessary to place the heavy counterweight directly over or slightly ahead of the trailer's tandem axles, ensuring the transport rig maintains proper 10% to 15% tongue weight without fish-tailing at highway speeds.

FMCSA 49 CFR Part 393: 4-Point Chain and Binder Regulations

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) enforces strict statutory rules under 49 CFR Section 393.130 governing the securement of heavy machinery and equipment weighing 10,000 pounds or more. Forklifts must be restrained using at least four independent tie-down assemblies attached directly to designated structural anchor points on the machine frame.

Synthetic polyester ratchet straps are strictly prohibited for securing heavy industrial equipment because sharp steel edges on forklift undercarriages can easily sever nylon webbing during transit. Haulers must use Grade 70 transport chains (minimum 5/16-inch or 3/8-inch diameter) paired with drop-forged ratchet load binders. The aggregate Working Load Limit (WLL) of all four tie-down chains must equal at least 50% of the total gross weight of the forklift being transported.

Mast Lowering, Tilt Locking, and Overhead Bridge Clearances

Before moving the transport rig, the forklift's hydraulic mast and carriage require physical securing. Lower the mast completely, tilt the carriage forward until the forks rest flat against the wooden trailer deck, and lower the forks onto a heavy oak timber block to prevent steel-on-steel friction. Relieve hydraulic line pressure by shutting off the engine and cycling the hydraulic control levers.

Most critically, measure the exact vertical clearance from the road surface to the highest point on the forklift mast using an architectural height measuring stick. Standard legal interstate bridge clearance is 13 feet 6 inches (14 feet in western states). If loading on a standard high-deck flatbed (deck height 5 feet) places the top of an 8-foot mast at 13 feet 8 inches, you will collide with municipal highway overpasses, resulting in catastrophic equipment destruction. Moving tall forklifts requires utilizing drop-deck trailers or removing the upper mast stages.

How to Safely Chain Down and Transport a Forklift

Follow this step-by-step roadmap to load, position, tie down, and measure height clearances for industrial forklift transport.

  1. Confirm Trailer Rating and Inspect Deck Surface

    Ensure the trailer's Gross Vehicle Weight Rating (GVWR) exceeds the combined weight of the forklift, and clear oil from the deck.

  2. Load Forklift and Center Counterweight Over Axles

    Drive or back the forklift slowly up low-angle ramps, positioning the heavy rear counterweight to achieve proper trailer tongue balance.

  3. Lower Mast Completely and Set Parking Brake

    Lower forks flat onto hardwood dunnage on the deck, engage the mechanical emergency brake, shut off the engine, and close fuel valves.

  4. Attach Four Grade 70 Transport Chains to Frame Hooks

    Fasten four independent 5/16-inch or 3/8-inch Grade 70 chains in an X-pattern to designated structural towing eyes, pulling at 45-degree angles.

  5. Tension Ratchet Binders and Measure Overhead Clearance

    Crank ratchet binders until chains are rigid, lock binder handles with safety pins, and measure total ground-to-mast clearance with a height stick.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can you transport a forklift with nylon ratchet straps?

No. Federal DOT regulations (49 CFR 393) strictly prohibit securing heavy commercial equipment over 10,000 lbs with synthetic straps. You must use certified Grade 70 or higher steel transport chains and ratchet binders.

Q2: Why are forklifts so heavy compared to cars of the same size?

Forklifts feature massive solid-cast iron counterweights at the rear to counterbalance heavy loads lifted on the front forks, making even compact warehouse forklifts weigh between 7,000 and 12,000 pounds unladen.

Q3: Should a forklift be backed onto a trailer or driven forward?

Backing the forklift onto the trailer is usually preferred because the heavy counterweight is placed closer to or directly over the trailer's axles, preventing excessive tongue weight on the towing vehicle.

Q4: What is the legal highway height limit for transporting a forklift?

The federal legal height limit is 13 feet 6 inches (14 feet in some western states). The total height of the trailer deck plus the collapsed forklift mast must remain strictly below this limit to avoid hitting overpasses.

Q5: What chain size is required for a 10,000 lb forklift?

A 10,000-pound forklift requires at least four Grade 70 5/16-inch chains (WLL 4,700 lbs each) or 3/8-inch chains (WLL 6,600 lbs each), easily satisfying the federal 50% aggregate weight requirement.

Q6: Do you have to shut off the propane tank when hauling a forklift?

Yes. When transporting a propane-powered (LP) forklift, the service valve on the liquid propane cylinder must be closed tightly, and fuel lines should be purged before travel.

Q7: What is an RGN trailer and when is it needed for forklifts?

An RGN (Removable Gooseneck) lowboy trailer drops flat to ground level, offering a deck height of only 18 to 24 inches, essential for transporting heavy, tall mast forklifts that would exceed highway height limits on regular trailers.

Q8: How do you secure the forklift mast during transit?

Tilt the mast forward so the forks rest flat on wooden blocks on the deck, and wrap an independent tie-down chain around the lower mast uprights attached to front trailer D-rings to prevent hydraulic drift.

Final Thoughts & Key Takeaways

Transporting an industrial forklift safely requires treating the machine as concentrated heavy machinery. Always verify unladen curb weight against your trailer's payload rating, load with the heavy rear counterweight positioned properly to manage tongue weight, secure all four corners with certified Grade 70 transport chains and ratchet binders meeting FMCSA regulations, lower and chain the hydraulic mast, and verify total overhead bridge clearance before departing.
