Asbestos Siding Removal

Informational
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 14, 2026

Removing asbestos cement siding shingles—frequently known by historic trade names such as Transite—is a major exterior renovation undertaking common to residential properties constructed between the nineteen-thirties and nineteen-seventies. Formulated by blending Portland cement with fifteen to thirty percent chrysotile asbestos fibers, these rigid exterior shingles provided unmatched fire resistance, rot prevention, and weatherproofing for millions of mid-century homes. However, when siding becomes broken, warped, or must be stripped for architectural remodeling, adhering to strict wet-removal protocols, worker safety standards, and hazardous waste disposal regulations is vital.

Material Science: Transite Durability, Brittleness, and Friability Rules

Asbestos cement exterior siding was engineered as a dense, fireproof cladding material. The Portland cement matrix locked mineral fibers tightly in place, producing rectangular shingles measuring twelve inches by twenty-four inches that resisted fire, moisture, and pests. Under federal EPA NESHAP regulations, intact asbestos cement siding is classified as Category II non-friable asbestos, meaning it cannot be crumbled or pulverized by hand pressure under normal conditions and poses minimal risk while undisturbed.

However, decades of outdoor weathering, temperature cycling, and ultraviolet radiation make vintage shingles exceptionally brittle. When homeowners or contractors attempt removal using pry bars, hammers, or power tools, the brittle shingles can shatter, crack, and pulverize. Fracturing shingles breaks the cement matrix and exposes raw mineral fibers, converting non-friable siding into regulated friable debris that releases microscopic, respirable fibers into ambient air currents.

Step-by-Step Abatement Procedures: Wetting, Un-Nailing, and Ground Protection

Executing asbestos siding removal safely requires a disciplined sequence of engineering controls. Before touching the shingles, technicians lay heavy six-mil polyethylene drop cloths on the ground extending at least ten feet out from the exterior foundation wall to capture any accidental chips or debris. A garden sprayer filled with surfactant-amended water (water mixed with a small amount of liquid detergent) is used to mist the shingles thoroughly prior to unfastening.

Each shingle must be removed intact. Rather than prying outward against the brittle shingle face—which snaps the panel—workers utilize specialized shingle nail rippers or flat pry bars slid gently beneath the shingle to cut or pull the mounting nails directly from behind. Intact shingles are carefully unhooked and lowered by hand to the ground drop cloth; dropping or throwing shingles from scaffolding or ladders is strictly illegal under OSHA regulations. Lowered shingles are stacked neatly and kept damp throughout the workday.

Packaging, Waste Manifesting, and Disposal Compliance

Once shingles are removed and stacked on the ground, strict waste packaging regulations apply. Shingle stacks must be wrapped in double-layer six-mil polyethylene sheeting, sealed airtight with heavy-duty duct tape, and labeled with OSHA hazardous material warning placards indicating asbestos content. Alternatively, waste can be loaded directly into lined, lockable hazardous waste roll-off dumpsters designated exclusively for asbestos disposal.

Transport and disposal must strictly adhere to state environmental protection statutes. Asbestos waste cannot be discarded in standard municipal trash or general construction dumpsters. It must be transported under a formal Uniform Hazardous Waste Manifest to a permitted landfill licensed to accept non-friable asbestos. Homeowners must require their contractor to provide a signed copy of the final manifest from the landfill operator as legal proof of compliant disposal, essential for future property disclosures.

Adhering to certified removal and disposal procedures guarantees a clean, safe exterior ready for modern siding installation.

How to Safely Remove Asbestos Siding in 5 Steps

Follow these standardized procedures to safely plan, execute, and dispose of exterior asbestos cement siding.

  1. Establish Ground Containment Drop Cloths

    Lay double-layer six-mil polyethylene sheeting extending ten feet from the foundation wall to catch any falling chips.

  2. Don Appropriate Personal Protective Equipment

    Wear a NIOSH-approved P100 half-mask respirator, disposable Tyvek coveralls, safety glasses, and puncture-resistant gloves.

  3. Apply Continuous Surfactant Wetting

    Mist shingles thoroughly with water and liquid surfactant using a low-pressure pump sprayer before and during removal.

  4. Extract Mounting Nails Behind Shingles Intact

    Use a shingle nail ripper to cut or pull nails from behind, carefully lowering intact shingles to the ground without dropping.

  5. Package, Label, and Manifest Hazardous Waste

    Wrap damp shingle stacks in double-layer six-mil plastic, seal with duct tape, attach OSHA warning labels, and manifest disposal.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: Can a homeowner remove asbestos siding themselves?

In many states, single-family homeowners can legally remove siding on their primary residence, but following strict wet-removal and disposal rules is mandatory.

Q2: How much does professional asbestos siding removal cost?

Professional removal typically costs between eight and fifteen dollars per square foot, or roughly eight thousand to twenty thousand dollars for an entire home.

Q3: Why is pressure washing asbestos siding prohibited?

Pressure washers strip the protective cement matrix and aerosolize toxic mineral fibers into the surrounding neighborhood air.

Q4: Can you cut asbestos siding with power saws?

No, cutting asbestos siding with circular saws or grinders is illegal because high-speed blades pulverize shingles into dangerous clouds of dust.

Q5: Can I install new siding directly over old asbestos siding?

Yes, installing rigid foam insulation and new vinyl or composite siding directly over intact shingles is an approved encapsulation method.

Q6: What respirator is required for asbestos siding removal?

Workers must wear a NIOSH-approved respirator equipped with P100 particulate filters; standard dust masks offer zero protection.

Q7: How is waste asbestos siding legally disposed of?

Waste must be kept wet, double-wrapped in six-mil poly plastic, labeled with OSHA warnings, and transported under a manifest to an approved landfill.

Q8: Does removing asbestos siding increase home value?

Yes, eliminating legacy asbestos siding and installing modern, energy-efficient siding improves curb appeal, energy efficiency, and resale marketability.
