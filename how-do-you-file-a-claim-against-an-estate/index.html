How Do You File a Claim Against an Estate?

Q&A
Published by TheHindiMeaning Admin•Updated: September 15, 2026

Understanding how do you file a claim against an estate is an essential legal recovery procedure for service providers, private lenders, contractors, and individuals who are owed outstanding monies by someone who has passed away. Debt obligations do not vanish upon a debtor's death; instead, they become legal liabilities of the decedent's probate estate. Recovering these funds requires complying strictly with state non-claim statutes, serving the personal representative, and filing formal verified claims in the county probate court.

The Non-Claim Statute: Unforgiving Probate Deadlines

Every state has enacted a statutory framework known as a Non-Claim Statute. When probate administration is opened, the court appoints an executor (if named in a will) or an administrator (if the deceased died intestate). The personal representative must then publish a formal 'Notice to Creditors' in an approved local newspaper and send direct written notices to all known creditors.

The non-claim statute sets an absolute, unyielding deadline for creditors to present their claims. In most states, creditors have between three and six months from the first date of publication (or 30 to 60 days from receiving direct written notice) to submit a formal claim. Missing this deadline by even a single day acts as an absolute legal bar, permanently extinguishing your right to collect regardless of how legitimate the underlying debt was.

Assembling the Claim: Form Preparation and Corroborating Evidence

Submitting an informal email or standard monthly invoice is insufficient to constitute a valid legal claim in probate court. Creditors must prepare a formal judicial document, typically titled a 'Verified Statement of Claim.' This legal document must specify the creditor's full legal identity, the exact dollar amount of the principal and accrued contractual interest, the underlying basis of the liability, and a sworn declaration that no uncredited payments or offsets exist.

Furthermore, the statement of claim must be substantiated with documentary proof. Attach signed promissory notes, written contracts, itemized service invoices, proof of partial payments, or certified copies of civil judgments. The completed claim must be officially filed with the clerk of the probate court where the estate is pending, and a certified copy must be formally served upon the personal representative or their attorney of record.

Navigating Executor Actions: Allowance, Rejection, and Lawsuits

Once served with a verified claim, the personal representative must examine its validity. The executor has three statutory options: allow the claim in full, allow it in part, or formally reject it in writing. If the claim is allowed, payment is scheduled during the estate accounting and distribution phase, provided the estate contains sufficient funds after satisfying higher-priority administrative costs and tax debts.

If the personal representative rejects your claim, they will file a formal 'Notice of Rejection of Claim.' Receiving this notice triggers an urgent, narrow statutory clock. In most jurisdictions, a creditor has only 30 to 90 days from receipt of a rejection notice to file an independent civil lawsuit against the executor in court to establish the debt. If you fail to file suit before that deadline expires, the claim is permanently forfeited.

How to File a Claim Against an Estate in Probate Court

Follow these five strategic steps to prepare, file, and enforce a creditor claim against a decedent's estate.

  1. Locate the County Probate Case File

    Contact the probate clerk in the county where the deceased resided to find the docket number and executor name.

  2. Verify Non-Claim Statutory Filing Deadlines

    Check the local court publication date to ensure you submit your claim within the 3-to-6-month statutory window.

  3. Complete the Official Statement of Claim Form

    Fill out the state-approved verified claim form detailing the debt amount, origin, and interest calculations.

  4. Attach Full Corroborating Documentation

    Include signed contracts, itemized billing invoices, delivery confirmations, and promissory notes.

  5. File with Probate Court and Serve the Executor

    File the original documents with the probate clerk and send certified copies to the executor via return receipt mail.

Frequently Asked Questions (8 Questions Answered)

Q1: What happens if a creditor misses the estate claim deadline?

Missing the statutory deadline permanently extinguishes the debt; the creditor loses all legal recourse to collect from the estate or heirs.

Q2: Are family members responsible for paying a deceased relative's debts?

No, relatives are not personally liable for a decedent's debts unless they co-signed the loan or shared joint account liability.

Q3: What happens if an estate does not have enough money to pay claims?

The estate is declared insolvent; debts are paid according to statutory priority ranking, and lower-tier unsecured debts are discharged unpaid.

Q4: Can I file a claim against an estate without an attorney?

Yes, individuals and business owners can file claims pro se using standardized probate court claim forms and documentation.

Q5: How long does it take to receive payment from a probate estate?

Payments are typically distributed at the conclusion of probate, which generally takes 6 to 18 months depending on estate complexity.

Q6: Does life insurance pay estate creditor claims?

Generally no; life insurance payouts with designated living beneficiaries bypass probate completely and are protected from creditors.

Q7: What is a Notice of Rejection of Claim?

It is a formal written notice from the executor denying your claim, triggering a 30-to-90-day window to file a civil lawsuit.

Q8: Can a creditor open probate if the family refuses to do so?

Yes, in many states, an unpaid creditor has legal standing to petition the probate court to open administration and appoint an administrator.
